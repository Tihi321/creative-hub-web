The Last Harbor

The harbor had no name worth writing down. It sat on a low spit of land where the river met the sea, and twice a day the tide went out and left the mud flats bare, so that the boats stood on their keels in the silt like patient animals waiting for something.

Nella arrived at four in the morning, before the tide turned. She walked the length of the quay with a canvas bag on her shoulder and her boots making the only sound in the world. The Drift lay on her mooring lines, mast stepped, rigging taut against the dark.

Nella had built her in the winter before, in a shed behind the chandlery, and she knew every fastening in her by hand. Oak at the stem. Cedar in the planks. A teak deck laid over plywood. She had cut the sailcloth herself and stitched the panels with a palm needle, and she had sewn the reef points with a backstitch that would not pull out under load.

She checked the rigging first, because the rigging was the thing that held the sky up. Stainless steel, one-eighth inch, set up with turnbuckles she had tightened the evening before. She ran her fingers along each shroud, feeling for the give that meant a strand had worked loose. The forestay held. The backstay held. The cap shrouds held on both sides. She went along the whole length of the boat twice and then a third time, because the third time was the time she trusted, and because the work of checking was the work she knew best, and the work she knew best was the work that would keep her alive.

The sails were stowed in the locker built into the cockpit sole. She lifted the mainsail out and laid it across the deck in the dark. Cotton duck, twelve ounces, cut in three panels with a bolt-rope along the leech. She ran her hand along the seams and found one small abrasion near the foot, where the cloth had rubbed the boom in a previous season. She marked it with chalk and set the sail aside. The jib was sound. The storm trysail was sound. She counted the spare bolts of cloth and the coils of twine and the tin of needles, and closed the lid.

The water was the thing she worried about most.

She had four casks, each holding fifteen gallons, sealed with rubber gaskets and capped with brass. That was sixty gallons, which at a gallon a day was sixty days of water, and sixty days was what the crossing asked for. She lifted each cask from the bilge shelf and shook it gently, listening for the sound of liquid against the walls. All four were full. She checked the gaskets by pressing her thumb against each seal and feeling for the give that meant a crack. The first three held. The fourth had dried and hardened at the rim, so she cut a new gasket from the sheet she had brought from the chandlery, fitted it, and set the cask back on the shelf.

Sixty days of water. The rain catcher on the coachroof would stretch it, if it rained, and she had no way of knowing whether it would. The margin was thin. She knew it was thin. She had calculated it anyway, because the alternative was to carry more water than the boat could bear, and the boat was already at her limit.

She ate bread with salt and drank tea from a thermos. The harbor was quiet. The mud flats were dark. A single light burned in the chandlery window and the chandlery was closed, which had never stopped the cat from sitting on the sill and watching her. She raised her cup to the cat. The cat did not raise anything back.

She opened her logbook and wrote the date. She wrote the weather: calm, overcast, no wind. She wrote the tide: turning, rising. She wrote the condition of the boat: sound, ready. The sails: one seam to repair. The water: four casks, one gasket replaced. The route: as charted by Halden Reeve, published in 1971, the crossing from this last harbor to the far atoll, two thousand nautical miles across the Windward Drift. Expected duration: sixty days, give or take the weather's mood. Navigation: star paths, seasonal wind, compass only in dark weather.

She had read Reeve's book three times. She had memorized the route and copied the chart onto a sheet of paper and pinned it to the bulkhead inside the cabin. The route ran west-southwest from the harbor, following the North Equatorial Current, then turned northwest at the Intertropical Convergence Zone, then ran west-northwest to the atoll. Reeve had done it in fifty-one days, alone, in a steel-hulled cutter, with a radio and an engine and a crate of tinned beef. He had written it down, and the book had been reprinted four times, and the route had become famous, and the route was now fixed.

Nella had no engine she intended to use, no tinned beef, and no crew. She had a single radio set, a small transceiver bolted to the cabin wall and wired to a whip antenna on the mast. She had Junie Park on the far atoll, a young woman who kept the radio station open and answered boats that came through the Windward Drift. Junie was the only voice from land. Junie was the only connection.

She had not called her yet. She would call in the morning, when the propagation was best. The frequency was written on a card and pinned to the bulkhead, with the call sign under it, and under that the message she meant to send: Drift, departing. Weather check requested. Over.

She slept in the narrow berth she had built herself, under the cockpit sole, and she slept poorly. She woke at five, when the light came through the porthole grey and thin. The harbor was waking too. A man walked his dog along the quay. A woman opened the shutters of the chandlery. The mud flats were covered. The Drift floated.

She made coffee and ate bread and checked everything again: rigging, sails, casks, bilge pump, anchor, flare gun, first-aid box. Then she checked the compass. It was a small brass instrument on the bulkhead, a magnetic needle floating in a bowl of liquid, and she looked at it the way you look at a friend who means well and cannot be relied upon. In the Windward Drift the readings wandered, and they wandered in one direction more than any other, east of truth, and the wandering grew as the boat went south. On the sixth day out it would be thirteen degrees off, and on the eighth, nineteen, and she knew both numbers the way she knew the numbers on a tide table, because she had read them in the book before she ever left.

Otherwise she would steer by the stars at night and by the sun in the daytime, as the old boatmen did, as the pilots who took Zheng He's fleets across the sea had done. She had read about them in a thin book with a green cover, and she had thought about them often. They watched the sky and kept the compass for dark weather. She would do the same.

She turned on the radio. The set was no bigger than a shoe, with a dial and a speaker and a hand microphone. She turned the dial to Junie's frequency and pressed the microphone.

"Drift, departing. Weather check requested. Over."

The speaker crackled. She waited. The silence went on long enough that she began to doubt the antenna, the set, the whole idea of radios. Then the speaker opened, and Junie's voice came through with the slight delay of distance, clear and steady, a voice that had said these things many times and meant all of them.

"Drift, this is Junie. I read you. Weather check. Over."

"Over," Nella said.

Junie paused. She was reading the barometer, the wind, the sky, the color of the water on her side of the ocean, and she spoke slowly, as if she were thinking while she spoke.

"Wind is light, northeast, eight knots. Barometer steady at one thousand and twelve. No change expected for three days. The Convergence Zone is sitting at four degrees north, which is right for the season. The North Equatorial Current is running west as charted. The counter current is weak, which is what we want. You should have fair wind for the first week." A pause. "After that, the wind stress curl may shift and the corridor may narrow. I will watch it. I will call you when I see a change. Over."

Nella wrote the numbers in the logbook. Wind, barometer, the position of the Zone, the direction of the current, and the warning about the curl, and the promise that Junie would watch.

"Thank you, Junie. Over."

"Drift, you are welcome. I will keep the lamp lit. Over."

Nella put the microphone down and sat still for a moment, the way you sit still after a door closes.

She went back on deck and coiled the sheets, stowed the fenders, checked the anchor chain and the bilge. She checked the engine, a small diesel she had no intention of using, because the engine was for emergencies and emergencies were not yet here. She checked the emergency kit: flares, first-aid box, rations, bottled water, and the little hand radio on the second frequency Junie had given her, the one that would work when the main set failed. She put it back in the kit and closed the lid.

She ate lunch in the cockpit, bread and salt and a piece of dried fish, with the chart visible through the open hatch. She ate slowly and looked at the line on the chart, and she thought about the line as a line, and not as a story. Lines can be followed. Stories change on you.

At two in the afternoon the tide was full. The Drift floated clear of the ground. The man with the dog was gone. The woman in the chandlery was inside with her shutters shut, and the cat had moved to the step, and the sky was grey and soft and the water was still.

Nella untied the mooring lines. She walked the stern line along the quay, pulling the boat forward hand over hand, feeling the weight of her in her arms and her shoulders and the small of her back. The boat came, slowly, the way a boat comes when a person who knows her weight pulls her.

She stepped aboard, took the tiller, and set the sails. The mainsail rose. The jib filled. The Drift moved, slowly, the way a boat moves in light wind on a flat sea: without hurry, without complaint, without any interest at all in the fact that a woman had just left everything she knew.

They passed the mud flats. They passed the quay and the chandlery and the last house and the last tree and the last stone. The light in the chandlery window went out behind her, which was only the woman closing up, but Nella noted it anyway and wrote nothing down.

Then the sea was open. The sea was wide and flat and grey and quiet, and it was the Windward Drift, the broad equatorial ocean where the magnetic readings wandered and the compass would not be true and the stars would be the guides and the sun would be the guide and the wind and the current and the chart would be the guides, and the chart was Reeve's route, the route she had copied and memorized and pinned to the bulkhead, the route that had been sailed before and could be sailed again.

She sailed west-southwest.

The sky stayed grey all afternoon. Toward evening the wind freshened a little and came round to the northeast, exactly as Junie had said it would, and the Drift picked up her skirts and began to move like a boat that had been waiting for permission.

Nella set the tiller and sat with her back against the mast and watched the horizon go from grey to blue-grey to nothing much at all. She thought about the water in the casks, sixty gallons, sixty days. She thought about the compass she would not trust. She thought about Junie's lamp, burning on a windowsill two thousand miles away, and about the woman who had lit it, who had answered the call at a quarter past eight on a grey morning when a boat was leaving and there was nothing in the leaving that was any of her business.

She pulled the chart out of its tube once and looked at the line, and along the line were little numbers in Reeve's neat hand, the current and the wind and the run of each day, and a few words at the turns. It looked like a road. That was the trick of it. It had been printed as a road and sold as a road, and she had bought it for the price of a paperback and pinned it to her own bulkhead, and she rolled it up now and put it away and did not think about it again that day.

She did not look back at the harbor. She had decided that in advance, and she kept to it, which she was rather proud of, for about eleven minutes.

Then she looked back once. There was nothing to see. That was the point of looking. She turned her face to the west, where the first star was coming up thin and white over the bow, and took the tiller again, and sailed on.
