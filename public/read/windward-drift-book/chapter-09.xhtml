The Atoll

The light was a pale point on the water in the dawn haze, and over the hours of the morning the point became a smudge, and the smudge became a low grey line, and by nine o'clock the line had grown a shape on its eastern end, which was a stand of palms.

Nella had not seen a tree in twenty-six days.

She stood in the cockpit with one hand on the backstay and looked at it and did not trust her eyes at first, because the sea had been giving her things for weeks that turned out to be cloud or weed or the back of a swell, and she had stopped taking any of them for a sighting until they held their place for a full minute. The palms held. They held through a long roll and a long fall of the boat, and when she looked away and looked back they were still there, a little larger, a little more like palms.

She went below and took the noon sight early, because she wanted the fix on the ruled sheet and not only the shape in her eyes.

The sun came down through the haze, which made the line soft, and she took it three times and worked the arithmetic on the table with the pencil in her half-numb fingers, and the three answers agreed to within a mile and a half, which was good for a hazy morning and better than she had any right to expect. She wrote the position on the ruled sheet and drew the little circle, and the circle sat one hundred and four miles out from the light, bearing two four seven, which meant she was a mile and a half north of her track and three miles west of the fix Junie had given her the night before, which was the current, which was always the current.

She sat and looked at the pencil mark and did the rest of it in her head.

The trade wind had come back in the night behind the storm's last arm, and it was the true trade, out of the east-southeast at twelve and steady, the wind that all the old books said would hold once the disturbances had gone through, and the barometer had stopped falling at 1009 and was rising slowly, the way it does when a system has finished with a piece of ocean. The sea had lain down to a long even swell with no cross in it. The Drift was running west-northwest under working jib and a single-reefed main at five and a half knots, which was her number, the number she had found in the first week and kept the whole way across, and she would hold that number for a hundred and four miles if the wind held, which would bring her to the light in the last hour before dusk.

She wrote the plan in the log and went up on deck and trimmed for it.

The reef was the thing, and she had been holding the thought of it since the fix.

Junie had said four miles off the light and the passage north of it, and Nella had spent enough of her life on coasts to know what four miles off a light without a chart meant. The atoll's south light stood on the reef itself, or just inside it, and the reef ran out from the light in both directions and turned, and the turning was the problem, because a reef that has grown around the rim of an atoll is a ring and the only honest door through a ring is the one the water has kept open, and the water does not advertise it.

She had a chart. She had two charts, and neither of them was good for this. Reeve's sheet had the atoll drawn as a circle with the passage marked on the northeast, but Reeve had never been within a week's sail of the place, and a man's drawing of a coast he never saw was worth exactly what a sailmaker's measurement of a seam she never sewed would be worth, which was nothing. Her own ruled sheet had the atoll as a bearing and a distance and nothing else, because a bearing and a distance was all the sea had ever given her.

What she had, and what she trusted, was her lead line, the colour of the water, and the radio in the cabin wall.

She went forward and rechecked the lead line, because it had been stowed since the ninth day and a stowed line is a line you have not looked at. She ran the lead out and back and it was whole and the marks were still legible, the strip of leather at five fathoms and the two at ten and the white rag at twenty. She hung the lead on the rail by the shrouds, where she could reach it fast.

Then she cleared the cockpit of everything that had grown up in it over three weeks of heavy weather: the bucket, the lashings she had cut and not replaced, the empty tin cup, the flashlight she had stopped using when the batteries went weak, the spare sheet coiled in a bundle by the coaming. She rigged the spare grapnel where she could cut it free if the anchor was wanted quickly, and she lashed the tiller so she could hand-steer and work the lead at once without the boat running off on her. She reefed the main down a second turn and left the working jib set and took in the staysail, and when she was done the boat had gone from a boat on passage to a boat making a landfall, which is a different boat, and she had done the change herself in an hour with her rope hands.

She called the atoll at eleven.

"Atoll, Atoll, Drift, Drift, main frequency. Junie, this is Nella. Position by noon sight, one hundred and four miles from your south light, bearing two four seven. Wind east-southeast twelve, sea long, barometer rising. I plan to raise the light in the last hour of daylight. I have one question and it is the only one that matters. The passage north of the light — will it take me on the bearing I am on, or must I run past the light and come back to it. Over."

Static. Then Junie's voice, and there was paper being moved behind it, and it was steady, and it had the thing in it that a person's voice has when they have been sitting in a room for three days reading the same water off a chart and have finally been asked what they know.

"Drift, Atoll. I am looking at the harbor board and the two survey sheets I have, and here is what I know. Hold your bearing. Do not run past the light. The passage runs northeast by north for about a mile and a half and it is the only cut in the reef this side of the island, and it is marked by a pair of day beacons, a white post on the outer reef and a white post on the inner. If you see white posts, you are in the cut. If you see nothing but breakers, you are inside the reef's claw and you will go hard aground in less than a quarter mile."

Nella wrote it on the ruled sheet. Then she wrote the rest of what Junie said, which is the part that a sailmaker remembers.

"The bottom sweeps up fast. It goes from deep blue to brown in a boat length near the outer beacons, and if the sun is low behind you it will read black, so do not steer by colour into the sun. Steer by the swell. Off the cut the swell runs long and unbroken and it comes under you clean. In the cut the swell has to break somewhere, and it breaks on the reef tips at both sides. If you hear the swell break on both your hands at once, you are in the middle and you are right. If you hear it break only on one hand, you are on the wrong side of the middle and you must come off to the other hand." A pause. "Do you want me to bring you in on bearings, or do you want to do it yourself. Over."

Nella sat with the microphone for a second and looked at the ruled sheet, and at the lead hanging on the rail, and at the light, which was now a low white column out ahead with the palms behind it, and the whole crossing ran through her hands in that second, the sail and the crack and the buoy and the corridor and the two black nights, and it was all the same work, which was reading the water and holding the line.

"I will bring myself in," she said. "You have given me the cut and the beacons and the swell. That is the chart. I will call you when I am at the outer post and again when I am inside. If you do not hear from me at the outer post, you will know I am in the claw. Over."

"That is a yes," Junie said. "Call me at the outer post. I will be here. I have been here the whole time. Over."

Nella racked the microphone and went up on deck.

The afternoon was the slowest of the crossing, and she was glad of it.

There is a kind of running that is work and a kind that is rest, and after the corridor and the black nights the afternoon gave her the second kind. The trade held at eleven and twelve and a little more toward four o'clock, the Drift moved west-northwest at five and a half and sometimes six, and the swell came under her quarter in long smooth lifts that she did not have to meet with anything but her knees. She sat with the tiller across her thighs and the lead line coiled at her hip and the radio turned up in the cabin, and she let the sun go down behind her right shoulder until it stood an hour above the horizon, and then the sea began to change colour ahead of her the way the sea does within twenty miles of land, going from the pale grey-green of the open Drift to a deeper, warmer blue-green that she had not seen since the Last Harbor.

At four o'clock the atoll was a whole island, with a windward line of surf showing along its southern jaw and the palms standing up behind it, and the light tower was a small white finger and no longer a smudge, and she could see the pale line of the reef running out from the island on both sides, the surf breaking along it in a long slow white that never stopped and never changed its place.

At half past four she called the atoll.

"Atoll, Drift. I have the island and the reef. I can see the surf line. I am twelve miles out, holding two four seven. Tell me when you can see the cut from the tower. Over."

Static, and then Junie's voice came back with something behind it, a smile maybe, or the beginning of one.

"Drift, Atoll. I cannot see the cut from the tower, because the cut is on the far side of the reef from me and I am on the quay and not in the tower. But I have the tower man with me and he has the glasses on you, and he says he has a sail on the bearing two four seven and it is you, and he says you are twelve miles out and your course is good and you should hold it to within six miles and then come off half a point to the south, because the cut is a little south of your line and not a little north. Over."

Half a point south. Nella looked at the bearing on the ruled sheet and at the swell and at the white line of the reef ahead, and it agreed with what she was reading, which was that the cut would be a little south of the line, because the swell was coming in on her starboard bow and the set of it was pushing her north all afternoon, and the current had been doing the same since the first week, and landfall is the same arithmetic as the whole crossing, which is to say it is the same arithmetic as the first week.

"Half a point south," she said. "Understood. I will call at the outer post. Over."

At five o'clock she took the main down entirely, because the half point south required her to come up and the boat had more speed than she needed, and she wanted to be slow and exact at the reef. She ran under working jib alone at three knots, with the swell lifting the boat and the land close enough now that she could see the individual palms and the white sand inside the surf, and she put the tiller in her own hand and did not let go of it again.

The cut showed itself as a break in the white line, which is how a cut always shows itself, and the break was narrow, and there were two white posts in it, one on the outer reef and one behind it and a little to the left, and the swell was breaking on both sides of the break the way Junie had said and running clean through the middle of it the way Junie had said, and Nella lined the boat up on the two posts and put the sea a little on her bow and the current a little on her quarter and came in.

"Atoll, Drift," she said. "I have the outer post bearing two four seven and the inner post behind and to the left. I am going in. Over."

"Drift, Atoll," Junie said. "I have you. I can see you from the quay. You are right on the line. Come in. Over."

She came in at three knots and it felt like thirty. The swell that had been a long slow lift for twenty thousand miles began to shorten under her keel and the sound changed from the hiss of open water to the flat slap of shallow water, and the smell changed, which she had not expected and had not thought about for weeks, the smell of land, sand and green and cut palm and something like a kitchen, coming off the atoll on the last of the afternoon heat, and her hands did not shake on the tiller and her eyes did not leave the two posts, and the lead went out and came back at five fathoms and then four and then five, and the breakers broke on her right hand and on her left hand at the same time, which meant she was in the middle, which was the whole rule.

She called the outer post because she had said she would.

"Atoll, Drift. Outer post abeam, depth four fathoms, swell breaking on both hands. Going in. Over."

"Go in. Over."

The inner post came by on her left and the water went from deep green to brown to a pale sand-bottom green and then the swell stopped under her, stopped all at once, and the boat that had been moving with the ocean for twenty-six days was moving on water that was not moving with anything, and the Drift glided in on her own way and slowed and slowed, and the keel touched the sand of the lagoon and held, and the crossing was over.

Nella sat where she was with the tiller in her hand and looked at her own hand on the tiller and did not move it for a while.

There was a small harbor inside the atoll, with a stone quay and three mooring bollards and a shed with a tin roof, and she rowed the kedge out and dropped it and came alongside the quay in the last of the light, and there was a woman standing at the head of the quay with a hand-crank radio still in one hand and a folded chart under her arm, and it was Junie.

She was younger than the voice, and with the voice Nella had not bothered to make a picture, and now there was one, a short woman with a broad sunburned face and a station cap pushed back and wet-looking eyes, and the two of them stood looking at each other across the two feet of dark water between the rail and the stone and neither of them said anything for a moment.

"Junie," Nella said.

"You are a lot more boat than I thought," Junie said. "I have had your voice for twenty-six days and I thought you were about six feet tall and made of rope. You are made of rope but you are not six feet tall." She held out a hand and Nella took it and stepped up onto the quay, and her legs did not quite know what to do with stone, and she stood there with the radio woman's hand in her own and looked back at her boat lying in the lagoon with the light of the town coming onto her white paint, and something in her chest that had been held tight for three weeks and more let go all at once, and she was tired, and she was glad, and she did not have a word for the rest of it.

"The lamp," Nella said.

"It was in the window the whole time," Junie said. "It is still in the window. I am not going to take it out until you tell me to."

"How many people are on this island?"

"A hundred and sixty, more or less. There is a store, and there is a church, and there is a man named Anker who keeps the light, and there is me, and there is a man on the bench at the end of the quay who has been waiting for you since your first radio call, and I said I would not tell you who he is." Junie looked past her shoulder, up the quay, and her voice went careful. "I did not tell him anything. I said a small boat was coming in with a sailmaker on board who had something for him. He has been sitting there for twenty-six days. He has not said why."

Nella turned and looked down the quay.

There was a bench at the far end of it, in the shadow of the tin shed, and a man was sitting on the bench with his hands on his knees. He was old, and he was thin, and he was looking at the Drift lying in the water with an expression that Nella recognized because she had worn it herself for three weeks, which was the look of a person who has been looking at the same thing for a long time and cannot stop. There was a rope over his shoulder, coiled and old, and a brass sextant case on the bench beside him, and on the wood of the bench back below his hand there was a scar where a name had been cut into it a long time ago, and the name was nearly worn away.

Nella stood where she was and did not go up the quay.

"That is him," she said.

"That is him," Junie said. "I do not know what he wants. I do not know what you have in the box. I know you have been carrying it since the sixteenth day and I know you have not once in ten days told me what was in it." She looked at Nella. "Do you want to sit down first? Or do you want to go to him?"

Nella looked at the man on the bench, and at her boat in the water, and at her own hands, which were the hands of a sailmaker and had been the hands of a sailmaker since she was nineteen, and she thought about the box on the shelf in the cabin and the log inside it and the thirtieth day, and she knew what she was going to do with it, and she knew that the doing of it was not going to be the easy thing and that it was going to be the last of the work.

"The box is on the chart table," she said. "I am going to get it and walk up to that bench and sit down next to him, and then I am going to give him his property. That is the whole of what I am going to do tonight. Tomorrow I will sleep for a very long time, and then I am going to sit down with you over something in a cup and not talk about the weather. That is not the radio talking. That is me."

She went down the two steps to the water and stepped aboard the Drift and down into the cabin, and the cabin was dark and smelled of salt and canvas and her own hands, and the box was on the shelf beside the chart table exactly where she had lashed it on the sixteenth day, and she picked it up, and she stood for a second with it in both hands in the dark and listened to the water lapping the hull of her own boat, which had brought her two thousand miles and then stopped.

Then she went back up onto the quay with the box under her arm and walked up the stone toward the bench at the far end, where an old man with a rope over his shoulder and a brass sextant on the bench beside him was waiting for her, and behind her the lamp in the window at the head of the quay had been lit all day and would be lit all night, and all around the small harbor the ocean was enormous and indifferent and generous at the same time, and the thing she had come to do was the last thing left, and it was not the sea.
