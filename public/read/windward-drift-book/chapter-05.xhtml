The Drifting Buoy

The wind came back at dusk.

It came out of the northeast exactly as it had for two weeks, twelve knots and steady, and it took the Drift on the quarter and pushed her west-northwest, and there was nothing at all in it that said storm. The swell came out of the southwest and kept coming, long and low, and the two of them together made a corkscrew sea that the boat did not like. Nella sat at the tiller because the vane could not be trusted with it. She had never seen the two seas argue before.

She slept in snatches, an hour at a time, lying in the cockpit with a blanket over her and one hand on the tiller, and she woke each time to the same two facts: the sky was still mostly clear overhead, and the swell had grown.

By two in the morning the stars were gone behind a high thin overcast that let a little light through, enough to see the boat by. The swell was six feet now and long, forty seconds between crests, and it was out of the southwest and it did not belong to the wind at all. She had read that once, that a swell is a message from a place you have never been, and that the period tells you how far it has come. Forty seconds was a long way. A storm a long way off, and running toward her, and the thing she could not see yet was the thing she would have to steer by.

She put the reef in the main before dawn. It was work she could do in the dark with her eyes shut, and she did it with her eyes shut that night, sitting on the boom and hauling the cringle back to the gooseneck and tying the nettles in, and it was not fear that made her do her work well. It was habit, and habit was what she had spent twenty-six years buying.

At first light the east was a wall. It stood from the sea to the zenith, grey shot through with yellow, and it had no edge on it anywhere. The barometer read 1006, and the wind was still twelve knots, and the long swell kept lifting her sails until they let their air and then filling them again.

She went below and called the atoll, and the atoll did not answer.

She called again at seven and again at half past. She called a third time on the hour, and the third time she said the atoll's call sign and her own and waited the full minute, and the set gave her back the flat hiss of an empty ocean. She had heard that hiss before, once, when she had tested the spare set on the second frequency, but this was the main set, on the main frequency, at the main hour, and Junie was not there.

Nella racked the microphone and sat on the settee with her hands in her lap and looked at the cabin. Then she did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all night. The storm was three hundred and fifty miles west, making northwest at fifteen knots, and she was making west-northwest at five and a half, which meant that the two of them were closing on each other at twenty knots, which was twelve hours, which was not twenty-four to thirty at all. Junie had not said it that way. Junie had said twenty-four to thirty hours and the sounding of it had been a comfort, and she had spent a day and an evening believing a number the sea had never agreed to.

She did not blame Junie. Junie was a voice on a far atoll reading a ship report off paper. But the arithmetic was hers now, and the arithmetic said the weather was already here, the swell was the proof, the sky was the proof, and the twelve knots of fair wind she was riding was the last of something.

She looked at the barometer. 1005.

The buoy came out of the east at nine in the morning, and she nearly missed it.

It was the swell that showed it to her, lifting it into view and letting it drop again, so that it appeared and vanished and appeared with the sea's own rhythm, and when she first saw it she took it for a wave crest breaking clean. But there was no wind to break it, and at the top of the next lift she saw the shape: a cone, orange gone the colour of old bone, tilted, with a stalk and a flange and a light on top that was not lit.

She had two hours before the sky closed, and she wanted the thing. That was the strange part, and she examined it afterwards for a long time. She was a woman with a storm on her beam and thirty gallons of water and a mainsail with a patch in it, and she wanted a buoy the way a person in a room with a locked door wants a key. She wanted it because it might be a marker, some shipping-lane marker she could use, or it might carry an instrument or a name or a date, and a date was a piece of the world. And because she had not touched a made thing she could hold from another hand in sixteen days. The radio was a voice. The booby was a visitor. The buoy was a thing, with a history in its rust.

She bore away and ran down on it, and it took forty minutes, because it was drifting with the sea and she was going six knots through it. She came up to it on the lee side with the storm jib furled and the main reefed and the tiller lashed, and she stood at the shrouds with the reaching pole and watched it heave.

It was a cone buoy, about four feet at the base and eight feet tall, and it had been in the water a long time. The orange was bleached to bone on the side that faced the sky. The water side was furred green with weed below the waterline, and at the load line a band of barnacles sat packed shoulder to shoulder, little grey volcanoes. The stalk had a lifting ring on top and a light cage, and the light was dead. Lashed to the stalk, above the barnacles, with cord that had gone the colour of the buoy and had bitten into the metal it held, was a box.

Not a container in the modern sense. It was a steel box, about sixteen inches by twelve by eight, the kind a man might have built himself, with a seam down one edge and a hasp and a small padlock rusted to a lump. On the top of the box, in paint that had mostly gone, lettering had been cut with a chisel or a screwdriver, and she read it twice before the letters assembled themselves, and then she read it again.

H. REEVE — WINDWARD DRIFT — DO NOT REMOVE.

The swell lifted the buoy and the box came up out of the trough and hung there and went down again.

Nella stood at the shrouds with the pole in her two hands and looked at the name of the man who had made the route, riding the sea sixteen days from the last harbour, a hundred and thirty miles north of any line he had ever drawn. The name sat there in the rusted paint and it did not mean what she had spent two years believing it meant, and she did not yet know what it did mean, and the sea lifted it again and let it fall.

She got a line on it on the second pass.

It was heavy work and it was work she could do, which was why she did it. She ran down to leeward of the buoy and dropped the main and ran under bare poles at three knots, and at the top of a lift she put a grapnel of her own making, three hooks of half-inch bar lashed to a length of chain, over the rising cone and took the weight on. The chain came taut and the boat slowed and the buoy dragged astern and she paid out line until it was twenty feet back and clear of the rudder, and then she lashed the line to the bow cleat and stood up and breathed.

Then she went aft and got the heavy bolt cutters and went forward again over the coach roof with the boat yawing and bucking and water coming over the bow every third wave, and she hooked one leg through a shroud base and leaned out with the cutters and worked the jaws onto the cord that held the box.

The cord was not cord any more. It had gone to something between rope and stone, and it took her four bites to get through the first strand and then the thing parted all at once and the box dropped onto the buoy's shoulder with a clang she heard above the water, and then a wave took the buoy and the box slid and she had one hand on the hasp and the other in the lifting ring, holding on with everything, and the sea lifted both of them and dropped both of them and she thought for one entire wave that she would lose the box and her fingers with it.

Then the box was in her arms and she was sitting on the coach roof with it in her lap and the buoy dragging astern, and she was laughing, and she had not laughed in sixteen days.

She cut the grapnel line and let the buoy go. It went east, turning slowly on the swell, the last of the orange going out of sight between two of the long grey hills, and she sat with the box in her lap and watched it go and said, out loud, "You had it a long time."

Then she took the box below and set it on the settee and looked at the padlock.

She cut the padlock with the bolt cutters. It took one bite, because the hasp was the only thing that had held up.

The lid was stiff with rust and salt and she worked it with a screwdriver and her weight and it came up with a sound like a bottle being opened, and the smell that came out of it was dry, not wet. That was the first thing she noticed. Dry.

Inside there was a logbook bound in black oilcloth, and two pencils, and a folded paper chart, and a tin of what turned out to be hardtack biscuits gone to stone, and a roll of linen twine, and a small brass compass with a glass face and a needle that swung free and settled. Nella watched the needle settle and checked it against her own compass card, which was nineteen degrees off, and the little brass needle settled in the same wrong place, and she put both hands flat on the table and sat quite still for a moment.

Then she opened the log.

The hand was the same hand that was on her chart. She knew it at once from the little numbers and the neat closings of the letters and the way he had made the crosses of his t's like kissing X's. The entries began in a harbour she had never heard of and moved day by day in small blocks of writing, and each block had the wind and the course and the water used and a sentence or two of what the man thought about the day, and the first half of the book was the book she had read. The weather fair, the wind steady, the current with him, the sea blue as ink. She read her own crossing in it. She had read it three times in the harbour and once a month since.

And then the entries changed.

Day 21. Wind NE 3. Sea slight. The current has left the chart. It is setting me south, not west, and the compass reads 31 degrees off the pole this morning and 44 degrees by noon. I am holding the line by sun and star only and I do not like the northing I am making.

Day 23. No sun. Ran on the dead reckoning and the swell, which is a thing I do not trust. The compass needle turned through a full circle in the afternoon, and stopped, and turned again. I have put the compass in the locker. I am steering by Sirius and by the Cross and by my own watch.

Day 26. The corridor is not where the chart has it. I have run three days on the wind I expected and made westing but no northing worth the name, and the countercurrent south of the Zone has built a wall of foul water that I cannot cross. The wind is fair and the water is wrong, and the two are pulling the boat apart.

There was a gap of four days. Then:

Day 30. I have decided. I am turning back to the harbour I left. The route I advertised does not exist in this weather and this water, and I cannot make it, and to go on would be to kill myself for a book. I am ashamed. I have never been so ashamed. But the sea is not a road and I have been an honest man on the water all my life and I will die one rather than be a liar on land.

Day 31. Beating east. The compass reads nothing. The star fixes put me 40 miles south of my own log line and I set the log line by eye two nights ago so the error is mine as well.

Day 34. I put this box on a buoy that a survey ship of this station left drifting and I have set it to ride the current, because if I fail at the crossing then someone should know the crossing cannot be made and why. The current runs west. Whoever finds this will be west of me. Let them know what the sea did to this summer.

Day 36. Squalls from the south. Took a sea over the stern. The buoy has a light and I have put a new battery in it and I give it to the sea, and if it comes back to me I will know the current loops, and if it does not come back to me it is yours now, whoever you are.

That was all. The book stopped. The last page was a list of the stores he had left when he turned: five gallons of water, four tins, a spare packet of twine.

Nella closed it and sat in the dark cabin with her hand flat on the oilcloth and the boat working under her and the swell lifting her and lowering her, and she did not move for a long time.

She had, once, in the harbour, before all of this, gone to the chandlery in the morning and stood in front of the shelf where they kept the books and taken Reeve's book down and read the last chapter standing up. She remembered the last chapter. Day 51. Landfall at dawn. The atoll lies low and green and the lamp is burning in the window and I have made good my course and my northing and I am here, and the sea allowed it. There had been a photograph at the back of the book, a man of about forty with a beard and a straight look, standing on a quay with a coil of rope over his shoulder, and under the photograph it said Halden Reeve, before his crossing.

She had copied the chart off the endpapers and gone home and pinned it to the bulkhead inside the cabin of the boat she was building with her own hands, and she had spent years learning enough to sail it. Sixteen days ago she had left the Last Harbour at two in the afternoon with a route and a chart and a legend, and the legend had been a man's fifty-one days and a brave line drawn on paper.

The man had not crossed. The man had turned back on the thirtieth day, and written it down, and put the writing in a box, and set the box to sea on a drifting buoy, and gone home and published the crossing he had not made.

Nella's first feeling was not anger. It came later, small and cold, and she put it away for the moment, because there was a storm on her beam and the arithmetic was running. Her first feeling was the one a sailmaker gets when she takes a seam apart and finds the cloth underneath is not the cloth she thought it was. Not rage. That particular clean freezing behind the eyes, which is the beginning of knowing.

She had trusted the route because it had been published. She had trusted the numbers because they were in a book. She had given a living man's name the authority of a fact, and the fact was a wish, and she had sailed two thousand miles into a moving ocean holding a chart that said the ocean stood still.

The chart was not a lie, exactly. The man had sailed it. He had sailed it for thirty days and found the corridor gone out from under him and turned back, and every number in his published account was a true number from a summer that no longer existed, arranged so that the lie lived in the arrangement and not in the digits. She could see how it was done. She was a sailmaker. You can make a sail that looks fair and never once check the seam under the patch.

She looked at her own ruled sheet on the chart table, the one she had drawn on the fifteenth day with her own pencil and her own hand, with the atoll set where Junie had said it was and her fixes wandering across the paper in their loose chain, and the rule at the bottom in her own handwriting: Stars first. Current where I find it. Rain when it comes. Never again into a corridor without looking at the cloth.

She folded Reeve's log shut and put the box on the shelf beside the chart tube. She put the little brass compass in her pocket, because a second compass, even a wrong one, is a second opinion, and one wrong compass and one wrong compass can tell you which way the world is turning.

Then she sat down and looked at the barometer, which read 1004, and listened to the swell, which was no longer long.
