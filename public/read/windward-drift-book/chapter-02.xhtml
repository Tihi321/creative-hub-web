Star Paths

The first night at sea she slept in pieces, as she had planned, twenty minutes at a time with her eyes open between, and between the pieces she lay in the narrow berth and listened to the boat work.

There was a great deal to hear. The boom creaked against its gooseneck in a slow rhythm that matched the swell. A small wave slapped the bow every eight or nine seconds and then ran back along the topsides with a sound like paper being drawn across a table. The backstay hummed at a pitch that rose when the wind did. Somewhere aft, in the lazarette, a tin shifted and settled. She had lashed it. She would lash it again. She lay still and counted instead, and by the time the eastern sky went grey she had counted more sounds than she had names for and had decided she would learn the names.

Dawn came the way it comes at sea, slowly and then all at once, the stars going out one by one in the order of their brightness. She put the kettle on the little stove and made coffee and stood in the hatch with the cup in both hands and watched the sun come up out of a flat clean line of water.

Then she did the morning work. She wrote the entry in the log.

Day 1. Wind NE, force 3, steady. Sea slight, swell from the east, two feet. Course WSW. Distance run, 96 miles. Water used, one gallon. Sky clear. No vessels sighted.

No vessels sighted. She wrote it every day that week and it never stopped being a strange sentence to write, because it meant both I am alone and nothing has gone wrong yet, and those two things had the same eleven letters.

After the log she ate. Bread and salt and a boiled egg from the basket, and a second cup of coffee, and she sat in the cockpit with her back against the coaming while the Drift ran west-southwest on a broad reach at a steady five and a half knots.

The charts lived in a tube under the starboard bunk. Reeve's route was drawn on the working chart in his own careful hand, printed in the book and copied by Nella onto the paper in pencil: a line that ran down from six degrees north to the Zone at four degrees, then turned northwest, then west-northwest to the atoll. Beside the chart, pinned to the bulkhead, was her own sheet of paper, ruled into columns, and the columns were the things she trusted.

Column one was the date.

Column two was the meridian altitude of the sun.

Column three was the latitude that came out of it.

Column four was the bearing of the sun at eight in the morning, taken with the compass and written down quickly, because the sun's bearing at eight in the morning barely changed from day to day, and any large change in the number meant the change was in the compass and not in the sun.

Column five was the run of the log.

Column six was what she had eaten and drunk.

Column seven was blank. It was for anything she had no other place to put.

On the second evening she began the star paths.

She had a planisphere, a flat disc of cardboard with a rotating wheel, and she had made the disc do its job on the kitchen table at home in February, cutting a slot for the latitude and gluing a strip of paper along the horizon line, so that when she turned the wheel to the date and the hour she could see which stars would be where. She had also copied out a list, in small neat letters, of the bright stars and their bearings as they rose and set at her latitude.

The method was old and simple. She could not trust the compass, so she steered by a star.

At dusk, when the first four stars came out, she chose one that was rising low and clear over the bow, and she set her course by holding it a fixed width off the lee side of the mast, and she held it there with the tiller under her arm and her eyes going from star to horizon and back. When the star climbed too high to be any use, she picked the next one, and set the Drift to it. When one set in the west, she moved along to the next.

That first night the sequence was Achernar, then Canopus, then Sirius. She kept them like beads on a string, one star handing her on to the next, and the boat ran straight, and at midnight she lay in the cockpit and looked up at the whole turning sky and understood, for the first time and not as a fact but as a feeling, that the sky was a clock that also told direction.

She wrote in the log, in column seven: Steered by Achernar, Canopus, Sirius, Rigel. Course good. Boat good. Me, awake.

The isolation came in slowly after that, the way weather comes, not with a blow but with a change in the pressure.

She had no company but the boat. She talked to the boat the way she talked to a seam, and the water the way she talked to weather, and once, at the end of a long afternoon, to a booby that came and sat on the end of the boom for an hour.

The booby was a brown bird with a soft white belly, and it had the expression of a person waiting for a bus. It watched her eat. It watched her coil a sheet. When she moved, it lifted one foot and set it down again, politely, as if she had asked it to move and it was doing so.

She talked to it about the current.

That was the other thing that had started. There was nothing wrong with the boat and nothing wrong with the sky and nothing wrong with the food, and the work was the same work each day, and the same work each day was a pleasure, and the pleasure was the problem, because it gave her time to think about the chart.

She had begun on the third day to notice the run of the log was good, and the fix each noon was not.

The way it showed was small. On day two, her noon latitude came out at five degrees forty-one minutes north. On day three, five degrees twelve. On day four, four degrees fifty. The chart's penciled line, at those same longitudes, sat a little north of her. Not much. Eight miles here, eleven there. She made allowance, as a person does, and told herself the difference was her steering and her sights and the drag on the log rotator, and she put the bow up a little to correct for it.

On the fifth morning she had her answer, or part of it, because she took the run of the log and the run of the wind and the two noon latitudes and worked out what the water itself had done to her while she slept.

Set south. A steady set south, and slightly east of south, which could only be a current, and a current running at nearly a knot against the northern edge of her corridor.

She sat in the hatch with the chart on her knees and did the arithmetic twice.

Reeve's chart put the axis of the North Equatorial Current at about four degrees thirty north on this meridian. He had measured it running west at a knot and a half. By her own numbers, the axis lay nearer three degrees forty, and the water along it was running faster than he had found it, closer to one and a half knots — enough that a boat steering west-southwest would find herself, by the end of a week, twelve miles south of where she thought she was and pointing at water she had not meant to enter.

She had not entered it yet. That was the good news, and she wrote the good news down, because good news written down is worth more than good news remembered.

Day 5. Fix by meridian altitude: 4° 48' N. Log, 118 miles. Set south, 0.7 knot. Altered course 20° north of charted line to hold the north edge of the corridor. Wind NE 12 knots. Barometer 1011, falling slowly. Sky clear. No vessels sighted.

Then she went on deck and put the bow up twenty degrees and let the Drift come to her new course, and the boat, who did not care, went exactly as she was told.

The radio call was at eight.

She had got into the habit of being at the set five minutes before, which was not necessary, and which she did anyway, because five minutes of sitting still in the companionway with the microphone in her lap had turned out to be the best part of the day.

"Drift, Drift, this is the atoll. Morning to you. Over."

Junie Park's voice came across two thousand miles of water with a faint hiss under it, like a page being turned in another room. She always said good morning first, before the weather. Nella had noticed that in the first call and had decided it was the most professional thing about her.

"Atoll, Drift. Morning. Over."

"Reading you four by four. Weather, then. Barometer at my end is one thousand and ten, and I have you on a falling trend, which is not much, but it is a trend. Wind northeast, ten to fourteen, in your neighbourhood, and it should hold two more days." A pause; paper moving. "Now. I'm seeing something and I don't want to oversell it. The wind stress curl near the Zone has moved north about a degree since we spoke last. That means the corridor you're aiming for may sit a little higher than the chart says. Over."

Nella looked at her own five days of pencil crosses, wandering south of the penciled line.

"That matches," she said. "I'm being set south about seven-tenths of a knot. I've put the bow up twenty degrees. Over."

"Good," Junie said, and then, in a slightly different voice, the voice she used for things that were not weather: "You're five days out. Over."

"Five days. Over."

"I'll keep the lamp lit. Over."

Nella racked the microphone and sat a moment with the set's red dial glowing at her. Then she went on deck and stood at the mast with one hand on the halyard and watched the sea go by, and it occurred to her that Junie had said you're five days out the way a person says you're doing fine. That was not a weather report. She filed it in column seven.

Day six brought cloud.

It came up from the southeast in a low grey wall with a hard edge on it, and it brought the first rain she had seen since leaving, and by mid-afternoon the sky was shut from horizon to horizon and the sun was a smear of light and the stars were nowhere at all, and she was alone with the compass.

She had taken the bearing of Canopus the night before, when the star was on the meridian, and found the compass reading thirteen degrees east of truth. On day two it had read nine. On day four it had read nineteen. She wrote the number down each time in column four, and the column had become a record of a thing that wandered rather than a thing that grew: nine, then nineteen, then thirteen, the error drifting about its own wrong centre like a boat on a mooring. She had begun to look at that column the way a person looks at a dog that has started growling at nothing.

Now, in the grey half-light, the needle hunted. It swung past its mark twice and then settled somewhere and stayed there, and when she tapped the glass it moved again, and moved back, and settled in a new place, and looked as pleased with itself as a man in a pub who has just explained the tides.

She steered by the wind instead, and by the swell.

The swell was the older and truer instrument. It came in from the east, big and slow and regular, at a period of about eleven seconds, and under it ran a smaller chop from the northeast, the wind's own signature. The two of them together gave her an angle, and the angle told her where west was, because swell does not turn no matter what the compass says. She set the boat to the angle of the swell and held her there, and the Drift went on, and Reeve's chart sat in its tube, and the compass sat on its bulkhead saying nothing she believed.

At four in the afternoon the rain arrived.

She had the rain catcher rigged already, a canvas hopper lashed at the after end of the coachroof with a hose running to a jerry can in the cockpit, but the wind was aft of the beam and most of the water went over the side. So she did what sailmakers do, which is to work with cloth. She eased the mainsheet until the foot of the mainsail sagged and cupped, and the rain ran down the belly of the sail and gathered at the clew, and she sat under it in the cockpit with a bucket and caught it, jug by jug, while the sky fell.

The first of it was salt. She threw the first bucketful overboard and used the second to wash, a shiver of cold across her shoulders and her hair flat to her head. Then she rinsed with the third and let the rest run into the can.

By six the rain had stopped and the sky had broken up into long ragged islands of cloud, and she had four gallons more water than she had had that morning. She sat wet and cold in the cockpit with a mug of tea and did the arithmetic on the water, the way she did it every evening now, the way a sailmaker runs a finger along a seam.

She had left the Last Harbor with sixty gallons in four casks. On the sixth evening she had fifty-one, and the four she had caught that afternoon were counted in. Nine gallons gone in six days, which was a gallon and a half a day and not the gallon a day she had planned, and the difference was spillage and washing and the two cups she had drunk in the night without writing them down. Nine gallons in six days. Sixty days of water had become forty-odd, and the rain would come when it chose and not when she asked.

She drew a line under the numbers and did not love where the line fell.

She wrote in column seven: Rain, 4 gallons. Rinsed. Sky shut all day; used compass, did not trust it. Steered by swell. Swell does not lie. Note to self: learn to sleep with one ear.

On the seventh night the sky cleared as if it had been paid to.

She opened the hatch and the whole of it was there, and it was not the same sky she had left on the quayside, and she had known that it would not be, but knowledge and the thing itself are different animals.

Low in the north, barely above the line of the water and often lost in the haze that sits on a warm sea at night, there was Polaris, four degrees up, no more, a small patient light that gave her latitude without arithmetic. To the south, the Cross stood on one shoulder with its long foot swinging toward the horizon, and beneath it Achernar, and above and around them the rest, Canopus, Sirius, Procyon, the two bright shoulders of Orion going down in the west ahead of her.

She took her sextant out and checked the index error, which was two minutes on the arc, so she subtracted it, and she shot the star Canopus as it crossed, and got a latitude of four degrees thirty-one minutes. Then she shot Polaris, low and steady over the water, and got four degrees thirty-four. Two stars, two instruments of one instrument, two answers within three miles of each other.

She put the pencil on the chart and made the small cross and looked at the line of crosses from six days, bending south away from Reeve's line, and then at the new cross, sitting north of the bend, because she had put the bow up and the bow had paid for itself.

The corridor was still under her. That was the thing to hold to. Reeve had found it in 1971 and drawn it in this place, and she had found it twelve miles to the south of where he had drawn it, and there was a difference between those two facts and she intended to sit with it long enough to understand what it meant.

She wrote the log entry, and then she sat on the hatch and did nothing for a while, which she was learning to do well.

The Drift ran west-northwest at five knots with her sails trimmed and her tiller lashed and the wash of her bow going out into the dark on both sides. The waves passed under her, one after another, in the same rhythm, and the stars turned slowly over her in the same order, and the wind came from the same quarter of the sky with the same pressure in it, and there was a comfort in all of this that Nella had not expected. It was the comfort of a hand on a plane, or a needle through cloth. The sea did not love her, and it did not hate her, and it was doing exactly what it was going to do, and being able to see that was a kind of company.

She was not lonely. That surprised her. What she was, she thought, taking up the pencil, was attentive, and she had spent a good part of her life mixing those two words up.

In the morning she would call Junie and tell her what the water had told her. South axis, faster flow, the corridor shifted north of the chart in one place and south of it in another, the whole thing moving as a thing moves in a season, like a shoal of fish, or the beach at home after a winter of storms. Reeve's line was not wrong. Reeve's line had been right, once, when he drew it.

She pulled the chart out of its tube and looked at it one more time.

Reeve's route was drawn in a fine clear line across a wide blank of paper, and along it, in his neat hand, were little numbers, the current, the wind, the run of each day, and a few words at the turns: light air here; squalls at the Zone; hold north. It looked like a road. It had been printed as a road, sold as a road, and four reprints later it was a road that thousands of people had read about and a handful had sailed.

But a corridor in the middle of an ocean is not a road. It is a river of warmer and cooler air, held in place by the spinning of the earth and the heat of the sun, and it moves, and it does not consult the men who first walk it.

She rolled the chart up and put it away.

"All right," she said aloud, to the boat, and to the water, and to no one. "We'll take it as it is."

Then she put the kettle on and went to bed, and slept two hours at a stretch, then three, and woke at five to a sky going green at the edges, which is what the sky does at that latitude before the sun comes up, and which she had never seen before and would not forget.
