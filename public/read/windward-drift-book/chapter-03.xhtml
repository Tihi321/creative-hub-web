Torn Sails

The eighth morning was the finest morning she had had.

The wind came out of the northeast at twelve knots and held, the sea ran past the hull in long grey ridges with no white on them, and the Drift went west-northwest at five and a half knots with everything drawing and nothing complaining. Nella ate her egg standing up in the hatch. She shot the sun at nine and again at noon and the two altitudes agreed to within four miles, which was as well as she could do on a moving deck with a secondhand sextant, and she wrote the number down and did not fuss over it.

She had begun to know the boat the way you know a room in the dark. The creak of the gooseneck, the papery slap at the bow, the hum in the backstay that told her the wind before she felt it — all of it had names now, and the names had order. That was the thing she had not expected and could not have explained in the harbor: not comfort, exactly, but competence at being here. She knew where every tin was. She knew which of the four water casks she would drink from first and why.

She kept the water ledger on the back of the chart card. Sixty gallons at departure. Fifty-one on the sixth day. Fifty on the eighth morning, and the rain's four already counted in.

A gallon a day, and a gallon a day would take her sixty days, and she had done the sum a hundred times and it always came out the same and always came out too close.

Noon was clear. The sky was a hard blue bowl with one low band of cumulus standing along the southeast, and by one o'clock she had seen what it was.

It was a wall. A long grey line of cloud with a pale hard shelf under it, running from the horizon up toward the zenith, taller than it was wide, moving northwest at the speed of a fast walk. Squall line, she thought, and something in her went up like a bird. Rain. There had been no rain since day six, and here was a whole river of it coming toward her, and the rain catcher was already rigged and the jerry can in the cockpit was half empty, and if she caught a third of what that cloud held she would have ten gallons. Ten gallons was ten days. Ten days was everything.

She put the kettle away. She got the second can out of the lazarette and lashed the bucket where she could reach it. She coiled the hose down from the canvas hopper and wedged the mouth of it in the neck of the can, and then she stood in the cockpit with her hand on the boom and watched the line come, and she was smiling, which she had not done in a week without knowing.

The wind did not fall away in front of it. That was the first thing. It held its twelve knots and then rose, slowly, the way water rises in a bath, and with it came a strange warm smell off the cloud, like iron, like the inside of a kiln, and the sea in front of her went from grey to a strange flat black, and then from black to a smooth oiled skin with a long dimple in it running toward her.

Twelve knots. Fourteen. The boat leaned and picked up her speed and the log line sang, and Nella had her hand on the mainsheet, one turn on the winch, thinking reef, reef, and not having the three seconds it would have taken to do it, because the gust came out of the southeast instead of the northeast and it came flat and hard and at once, and the number was not fourteen and was not twenty.

The mainsail did not flog. That was the worst of it, and the reason it tore. Alarmed sail flogs and spills and forgives you. A sail taken straight from a hard full draw on one tack to a hard full draw on the other, with no warning and no slack anywhere in the system, has nothing to do but carry the load the whole way, and the load went through the cloth like a shout through a house.

She heard the seam go. It was not a bang. It was a long dry rip, a sound like a sheet of paper being torn slowly by a careful person, and it ran from the leech at the clew seam up toward the head for what sounded like six feet, and the leech of the main opened and the cloth divided and the wind came through the gap in a flat white blade of spray.

Then the noise arrived. The whole world was suddenly loud. The sea stood up in short steep pyramids with no shape to them, water came over the weather rail in sheets, the jib backed with a crack like a pistol and the boat rounded up into the wind and lay over on her side.

Blow the halyard.

She did not think it in words. Her hand was on the halyard clutch before her mind had finished the sentence, and she cast it off and let the mainsail run down the mast in a heap, and the sail came down in folds and sluiced half of itself over the boom and into the water, and she was on her knees in the cockpit gathering the cloth in both arms and hauling it inboard by the handful while the boat staggered and the rain arrived.

The rain arrived like gravel. It fell in a moving wall of water that took the visibility down to twenty yards and turned the deck into a river, and the jerry can in the cockpit filled in nine seconds and then went over with a clatter and emptied again, four gallons of fresh water running out the scuppers into the sea.

She heard herself laugh, one hard note, and then she got on with it, because the mainsail was still half over the side and the boat was still on her ear.

Bearing away. That was the first thing. She got the tiller and pushed it down and let the Drift fall off until the jib filled on the new wind and the boat came up out of her crouch and began to run, and the motion went from a stagger to a long downhill slide, and the rain went on for twenty minutes.

The gust lasted eleven.

She ran under the jib all afternoon.

The jib alone would not keep her on a course, quite. It would keep her running, which was a different thing, and running in a straight line on the back of a squall is as much as anyone should ask of a sail and a tiller. The wind fell back to eighteen and then to fifteen and stayed there, and the sea flattened out into a short ugly chop that knocked the bow up every other wave, and the rain stopped, and the sky cleared behind the squall and left a light she had never seen on water, a hard yellow light out of the west with black cloud standing up behind it.

She sailed west-northwest at three knots with her main down and her log line over the stern and her ledger in her lap, and she did the sum she did not want to do.

Twenty minutes at thirty knots with the main up and no way to reduce it, and eleven minutes of gust at the top of it, and now she was making three knots where she had been making five and a half, and she would make three knots until the sail was back up.

She got the mainsail inboard and spread it on the coachroof and had a look.

The seam had let go from the leech inward for about five feet. The twine was gone along that length, not broken so much as worn out — she could see it, the little grey fibres of it still caught in the needle holes on both sides, worn flat, cooked dark by sun and salt over a season she had not sailed and could not account for. The cloth itself was sound. The seam allowance had frayed back a quarter inch on each panel edge, which was ordinary and could be trimmed.

The bolt-rope on the leech had held. That was the good news and she wrote it down. If the rope had gone too she would have been cutting a new leech and roping it by hand at sea with the boat rolling, and that was a three-day job in a workshop let alone here.

She sat with her back against the mast and the sail across her knees and looked at the split seam for a while, the way a tailor looks at a coat. Then she went below and got the bag out.

The bag was flat and oiled canvas and had gone with her onto every boat she had ever worked on and onto two that were not hers. Inside it: three needles in a cork, one of them a heavy one for twine and two for cloth; a paper cone of Dacron twine and a paper cone of linen; a block of beeswax the size of an egg; a brass thimble; a fid of bone worn to the shape of her thumb; a chalk pencil; a small pair of shears; and the palm.

The palm was the old one, the one her first employer had given her when she was twenty and had hands like a boy's, and it was a strap of thick leather that went across the right hand with a steel cup set in the palm of it, and it had gone the shape of her the way a boot does. She put it on. She took a needle and no other tool, and she threaded it with linen twine, and she pulled the linen out through the wax twice, and she held the needle up and looked at it.

Then she laughed again, at nothing, and got to work.

The work took her the rest of that day and most of the night and half of the next.

There is a bench in a sail loft and there is no bench at sea, so she made one. She lashed the sail along the top of the boom in a long flat fold, a dozen turns of light line every four feet, so that the cloth lay out like a table under her hands, and she sat astride the boom with her feet braced on the coachroof and her knees holding the fold, and she sewed with the boat moving under her. That was the thing no one tells you. Every stitch had to be taken twice: once for the cloth, and once for the boat, because the deck would rise and drop and take the needle with it and put it through somewhere else.

The seam was a flat seam, which is to say the two edges of the cloth lay one against the other and were sewn through twice, from both sides, so that the thread locked itself in the middle and could not pull out from either face. She trimmed the frayed allowance back with the shears to a clean quarter inch. She took the two edges and rolled them together between her thumb and forefinger until they lay flat, edge to edge, and then she went along with the needle and the palm, taking a stitch every eighth of an inch, taking it back through the same hole and out and around, drawing the twine snug but not hard, because hard breaks the fibre and slack rubs it, and the whole skill of the trade is the difference between the two.

She worked from the far end toward her hand. You always work toward your hand. It is slower and it is right.

By dark she had the seam closed, five feet of it, one hundred and forty stitches, and the leech was whole again but not strong. A sewn seam in twelve-ounce duck that has already been seamed once and unpicked is a seam you cannot entirely trust, and she had known that when she began, so she had cut a patch before she started. She laid a rectangle of the spare duck over the seam, six inches wider than the split at every edge, with the weave running across the weave of the sail, and she tacked the four corners and then she sewed the whole border down with a double row of stitching and then worked a herringbone across the corners, which is the small zigzag that spreads the load and stops the corner from tearing away from the patch and taking a bite of the sail with it.

Linen twine, because the cloth was cotton and the two of them would shrink and stretch at the same rate in the wet and the dry, and Dacron would not, and a Dacron thread in an old cotton sail saws its way out of the cloth over a season. That was another thing her first employer had told her. She thought about him twice that night, and once she said something out loud to him, and it was not a nice thing, and she felt better afterward.

She finished the patch at half past five in the morning by the light of the binnacle and went below and slept for four hours flat, dead weight, and woke with her right hand cramped into a claw around nothing.

The water found her the next morning, which was how these things came: never alone.

It was the smell that told her first, or the absence of a smell. She pumped the bilge to dry it out, because after a day and a night of rain there was water in the bilge as always — but the pump water ran over her fingers and there was nothing on them, no salt, no slime, no sting. It was clean.

She put her tongue to it. Fresh.

She sat back on her heels in the cabin sole with the pump handle in her hand and understood, in the space of two seconds, a whole arithmetic of loss.

The bilge was fresh because one of her casks was leaking into the bilge instead of onto her hands. It might have been leaking for days. There was no way to know, because a leak in a cask does not announce itself; it goes into the bilge, and the bilge goes over the side with the pump, and no ledger in the world will catch it.

She went through the lazarette like a customs man. The four casks were fifteen-gallon drums of heavy grey plastic with screw bungs and gaskets, lashed two and two against the hull. She took them out one at a time and ran her hands around the base of each and the chime of each, and set each one on the sole and watched. The first was dry. The second was dry. The third, on the forward side, where a stave would have been if it had been wood, had a hairline in the plastic down near the bottom, half an inch long, weeping.

She could not find the end of it. The line was too fine and the water came out too slowly to see. She dried the drum and wrapped it round with a strip of cotton cloth and watched the cloth darken along a line an inch wide, and then she knew.

A gallon and a half gone, she thought, at a guess. Perhaps two. Two days at the rate it was going.

She had put a new gasket in the cap of that cask in the harbor, sat on the quay in the dark with a spanner in her hand, patting herself on the back for her thoroughness. She had never once thought to look at the body of the drum. Nobody ever does. Hoses and gaskets and caps, and the thing itself, the one piece with no moving part, is the piece you never look at.

There was no room in the other three casks. There was a little room in the jerry cans, but not much, and not nearly ten gallons.

So she did the only thing that would work, which was nothing at all, exactly inverted.

She got the funnel and decanted eight gallons of the leaking cask into the emptiest of the jerry cans, which took twenty minutes of trickling and sloshing in a sea that had come up again in the night, and once the drum was light she turned it on its head and lashed it back against the hull upside down, bung down, hairline up. The crack sat three inches above the waterline now, the water sat below the crack, and the drum would not weep so long as she kept it less than full. It was a stupid fix and a good one, and it would last until the crack crept.

She did the arithmetic standing up, and it came out at what she already knew.

Forty-eight gallons. At a quart a day she could drink a hundred and ninety-two days. At three quarts she could drink sixty-four. And she had been drinking three quarts, near enough, with the wash and the cooking and the two cups she took in the night without writing them down, and three quarts a day was not sixty-four days, it was the number she had been avoiding since the second week. She stopped washing her face. She reused the cooking water for the coffee. The rain's four gallons were already half gone.

She wrote in column seven: Fourth cask cracked at the chime, inverted, holding. Loss about two gallons. Water now 48. Ration 3 quarts. Nothing else to say about it.

She almost missed the call.

It was a quarter past eight when she remembered, which was the latest she had been to the set in eight days, and she came up the companionway with her hands black to the wrists from the twine and her hair rank and her right hand still cramped, and she slid into the hatch and turned the dial and pressed the microphone and said, "Atoll, Drift. Sorry. Over."

The speaker hissed. Then: "Drift, this is the atoll. Two minutes late. I was about to start worrying in an unprofessional way. Over."

"Rough night," Nella said. "Over."

"Tell me when you're ready. Weather first or the other thing first? Over."

"The other thing first," Nella said, and surprised herself. She told it flat and short, the way you report damage to an insurance man. Squall line off the southeast at one in the afternoon, gust thirty knots or better, no time to reef, main seam split five feet from the clew, sail down, running under the jib for a day and a half. Then the cask. Then the forty-eight gallons. Then, because she had come this far, "I'm making three knots while I've got no main up and I'll be lucky to hoist by nightfall. Over."

There was a pause on the other end. Nella had learned that Junie's pauses were not dead air. They were thinking.

"That matches what I've got, which I don't like," Junie said. "The Convergence Zone has shifted north. About a degree in three days, and it's still going. That squall line you met is the leading edge of it. You're going to see more of them, and they'll come out of the southeast with no barometer change to warn you, which is the kind of thing that gets people hurt. Over."

"How far north. Over."

"Four and a half, going on five. Which puts you on the southern edge of the Zone instead of the middle of the corridor." A pause. "I'll say the honest thing, Drift. Reeve's book puts the corridor holding through the whole season and I don't believe it holds through this one. The curl has moved twice since you left. It's not wandering. It's being dragged. Over."

Nella looked at the chart tube under the starboard bunk. Then she looked at the sea, which was the same hard grey it had been all morning and had not one thing in it that could be called a road.

"Say what you'd do," she said. "Over."

"I'd hold north of the chart. Two degrees north, maybe three. Take the squalls on the nose instead of the beam, and keep your water in the drum." Junie's voice came through the hiss with the same steadiness it always had, the steadiness that Nella had at first taken for training and now took for something else. "That's not an order. It's what I'd do. Over."

"Understood. Over."

"And Drift—" A pause, shorter, the other kind. "You're nine days out. You sound like you've been sewing for two of them. Over."

"Some of them," Nella said, and left the set keyed a moment before she racked the microphone.

She sat in the hatch a while after that. Then she went and got the spare set out of its oilcloth in the port locker, the little one, and turned it on and dialled it to the second frequency and pressed the key and said, "Test, test, this is Drift on the alternate." Nothing came back but the hiss of an empty ocean, and that was what she expected, and she turned it off and wrapped it up and put it back.

Then she went forward and hoisted the mainsail.

It went up ugly and it went up slow and it went up whole. The patch stood out from the rest of the sail like a scar on a face, six inches of new grey cloth framed in three rows of thread, and when the wind filled it she watched the patch in the light of the afternoon, waiting for the first pull, watching the cloth travel over the seam and out to the edges where the herringbone held the corners.

The leech stood true. The sail drew. The Drift leaned and shook off her slow crawl and came back up to five knots and then five and a half, and the wash went out along her bow again, and the sound of the water under her went from a plod to a hiss.

Nella sat at the tiller with the sheet in her hand and looked at the sail and let herself be a sailmaker for a minute, because that minute was owed her. The patch was not pretty. It was stronger than the seam it covered. It would outlast the panel around it, probably; a new piece of cloth and a new piece of twine beside twelve-ounce duck that had been twelve years in the sun on somebody's boat before it ever came to her. She had repaired a sail the way she would have repaired it in a loft, which is to say she had not made it as good as new, because nothing does that, but she had made it as good as the day it was cut.

She turned the boat two degrees north of the charted line. Then three.

There was a way to think about the chart, and she found it while the sun went down behind her shoulder. Reeve's line was a seam. Somebody had sewn it once, in his own conditions, with his own tools, and it had held for a while, and the cloth on either side of it had gone on moving and shrinking and taking the weather, the way cloth does, until the seam was the weakest part of the sail. You could stand in a loft and curse the man who sewed it. It would not help you. What you did was cut it back and sew a new one, taking your stitches into the cloth that was still sound, working toward your hand, and then you carried the extra cloth with you and you sewed again when it went.

The wind came up out of the northeast at fifteen knots and held.

She ate dry bread and a quarter of an egg and drank three-quarters of a pint and wrote the entry, and in column seven she wrote: Main repaired and set. Course altered 3° north of Reeve. Water 48. Wind on the quarter. Boat good. Me, tired.

Then she added, in smaller letters, because there was nothing else to do with it:

Sewn by hand. Holds.
