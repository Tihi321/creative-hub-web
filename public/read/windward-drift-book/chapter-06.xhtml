New Course

She gave herself an hour to think, and the hour was the hardest work of the day.

The swell was no longer long. It had shortened to twenty seconds and steepened, and the sea was building out of the southwest in a way that matched nothing in the sky, and the wind still came twelve knots out of the northeast as if it had not been told. She took the main down to the third reef and ran under the storm jib alone, and the Drift went west-northwest at four knots with her shoulder down and her deck wet, and Nella sat at the chart table with the pencil and the pad and the little brass compass in front of her and did the thing she had been circling since she opened the box.

She drew the ocean.

Not Reeve's ocean. Her own. She took the ruled sheet she had started on the fifteenth day and pinned it flat, and she put her fixes on it from the log — every noon sight, every star fix, every morning and evening bearing she had written down for sixteen days — and she joined them with a line that wandered. It wandered south at first, the way Reeve's corridor ran. Then, after the eighth day, it wandered north. It went north and west and north again, and the wandering was not error. The wandering was the current. The wandering was the shape of a thing that moved.

She sat back and looked at it for a while without the pencil in her hand. It was the first time she had looked at her own track all together in one place, and it did not look like a course. It looked like a question asked over and over, with the answers drifting north as the days went by. Every little cross on the paper was a morning or an evening out of a cockpit that smelled of salt and canvas and the boil of a paraffin stove, and every cross was farther north than the one before it, and she read the line the way she read a sail when she spread it on the loft floor: not for the flaws, but for the grain.

She found the grain. It ran north of his line, on the north side of the wandering, and the wandering bent with the water, and the water bent with the season, and the season was hers.

She looked at the line and she looked at Reeve's printed chart and she laid them side by side and the two of them told her what she needed.

His corridor ran down the middle of the map, west-southwest and then northwest. Hers ran on the north side of it, two degrees above, and then bent west. The difference was not one degree or two. The difference between his paper and her water was the difference between a road and a river.

She sharpened the pencil. The knife had a short blade and a wooden handle gone dark at the grip, and she drew it two or three times across the lead with the fine strokes she used on a pencil at the loft, and the shavings came off in a thin curl and lay on the chart like a curl of hair. The cabin smelled of the pencil lead and the paraffin and the wet wool of her sweater, and the boat worked under her and the swell lifted the pencil on the paper so that she had to put her left hand flat to hold the sheet down. She was aware of all of it, the way she was aware of everything in a small boat, and she was aware that it was the first time in sixteen days that she had been at work on something that was not waiting to be done to her.

She drew a new line.

She fixed her position first, the old way, sun and horizon and the running of the log, and then she checked it against the swell, which was running one nine five, and against the wind, which was running two two five, and the three of them agreed within fifteen miles, which was good enough. Then she drew a line north of northwest, two points north of the line she had been holding, and it went up the page toward a place where the chart said deep water and her own sightings said a countercurrent, and she named the line in her own head before she named it on paper. North of the Zone. North of his corridor. North, because in this season the water that ran west against the wind was running in the north, and the water that ran west against the wind was the water that would carry her.

She wrote the new course at the top of the sheet in her own hand, in small letters, the way she wrote on a sail: Course, NW by N, magnetic not to be trusted, hold by star and by swell. Corridor, north of Reeve's line, along the countercurrent. Rule: stars first, current where I find it. Water, thirty gallons. If the rain comes, take it. If the storm comes, run it.

She put the pencil down and looked at it and it looked like nothing. It looked like a line on a piece of paper drawn by a woman alone in a small boat. That was the whole of it, and the whole of it was enough, and she sat there for a minute and felt the strange light thing in her chest that she had not felt since the harbor, which was not hope exactly. It was the feeling of a decision that was entirely her own.

She thought about the harbor, and about the pin in the bulkhead, and about the man in the photograph at the back of the book — a man of about forty with a beard and a coil of rope over his shoulder, standing on a quay that no longer existed. Sixteen days ago she had left with his chart for a compass and his name for a weather report, and she had sailed his line and corrected it and sailed it again, always correcting, never choosing. There was a word for what she had been doing, and she did not like the word, and she put it away. She had a course of her own on the paper now, and it did not need anybody's name on it, and that was the end of the matter.

She worked the boat north all that afternoon.

It was the first time since she left the harbor that she had chosen a course rather than accepted one. She had come into the Drift on a charted line and corrected it here and there, twenty degrees north there, three degrees north again, and every correction had been a correction of somebody else's paper. Now there was no paper under her but her own. She trimmed the storm jib to a course of north-northwest and put the vane on and let it steer, and it held well enough in the swell, and the Drift went north with her deck awash and the grey wall standing off her quarter.

The wall did not come. That was the thing that kept her attention all afternoon. It stood in the west and it did not advance and it did not recede, and the barometer went 1004 and 1003 and back to 1004, and the swell stopped shortening and began to lengthen again, which meant the storm had stopped running at her and started running past her, off her bow, and the water between her and it was the water she could use.

She read the swell the way she read a hand. Period and direction and the shape of the crest, and the conclusion that came out of them, and the conclusion was the storm had never been coming for her at all. It had been going west on its own errand, and she had been in the wrong lane when it passed, and now she was in the right one. She said so out loud, standing at the tiller, because there was nobody to say it to.

"You were never coming for me," she told the water. "You just came." And the water went on being the water, and she found that she did not mind the answer.

She had read the charts enough to know what a wind corridor is on a day like that. The Zone drags the wind with it. Where the Zone sits, the trades on the south side blow one way and the trades on the north side blow another, and between them the wind dies and the water runs. The corridor is the seam between two winds. Reeve had found his in the middle of the Zone in his summer, and his summer was four decades gone, and the Zone was not in the middle any more. It had moved north, the way Junie had told her and the way her own sightings had told her, and it had dragged the seam with it. The seam was north of where she stood. North of where he had ever looked.

She rigged the spare halliard as a topping lift and got the storm jib down at dusk and bent on the working jib with numb hands, because the wind was going light on the north side and she would need the cloth. She laid the storm jib along the coach roof and folded it three times and stowed it in its bag, and while she worked she put her lips against the back of her hand to warm them and tasted salt. The main stayed at the third reef; she would not shake it out until she saw what the morning was. When she went below at full dark the barometer sat at 1005 and the wind had gone to ten knots from the north, and she ate a cold supper standing up — hard biscuit and a mouthful of tinned fish and one cup of water, which was the ration, which she drank slowly, and which she did not count afterwards because she had done counting and was done with it — and slept four hours in her clothes.

In the morning the sea had changed.

It came as a colour first. For sixteen days the Drift had been pale grey-green with the long yellow streaks of weed, and now, on the port bow, there was a band of water that was darker and smoother than the water around it, running north of west, and the band had a line on its southern edge where the two waters met and did not mix. She had read about that line. She had drawn that line, once, in a book in the harbor, and never believed she would see it.

She stood at the weather shrouds with one hand on the wire and looked at it for a long quiet minute. It was a seam. She had spent twenty-six years looking at seams. There is the seam you sew and there is the seam the cloth makes on its own where one bolt meets the next, and the second kind is the one you have to respect, because the cloth has an opinion about it and the opinion does not change to suit you. It ran north of west across the whole of the eastern horizon and it went out of sight behind her shoulder, and on this side of it the water was the old water and on the other side the water was new.

She put her helm over and ran into the band.

The Drift came alive under her like a boat that had been waiting. The water in the band was running west-northwest at a knot, maybe a knot and a half, and the wind was out of the north and light, and the two of them together laid the boat on a course north of his line by almost two points, with a sea that had gone smooth and a sky that was clearing to the west for the first time in three days. She shook the second reef out of the main and set the working jib, and the boat went five and a half knots as if the storm had been a story she had told herself in the dark.

She went below and called the atoll.

"Atoll, Atoll, Drift, Drift, on the main frequency, do you read me. Over."

And the atoll answered.

"Drift, Atoll. I read you." A pause. The voice was hoarse and young and it came through with a rush of static that was not the set, it was the speaker, breathing. "Nella, I am so sorry. Over."

"Atoll, the set. Over."

"The whole station went dark," Junie said. "Lightning took the mast night before last and I have been on the battery and the hand-crank and I did not have the range. I have had the set on since midnight and I have been calling you every half hour since six. Nella, I did not have the range. Over."

Nella sat with the microphone in her hand and looked at the cabin, at the box on the shelf, at Reeve's log and her own ruled sheet, and she understood that the atoll had been there the whole time, and the silence had been wires. She thought of the thing she had felt at nine in the morning when the set gave her back the empty ocean. She had felt alone. She had decided, standing at the chart table with a pencil in her hand, that being alone was what she had. And the atoll had been sending up half-hourly calls into the static the whole time, and the reason they did not arrive was a mast and a battery, which is to say, weather.

She noticed that she was not angry, and she noticed it the way she noticed a shift in the wind, without measuring it. The atoll had not failed her. The weather had taken the atoll's voice and held it for two days and given it back. That was the kind of thing the Drift did. It took the things you counted on and gave them back when it was ready, and the trick was not to decide, in the meantime, that they had gone for good.

"I am two hundred miles north of the line and I am on a new course," she said. "Over."

There was a pause that was not dead air. It was the pause of a person writing. Nella had learned to count the pauses, the way she had learned to count them all through the crossing, and this one she did not have to count. It was a different kind of silence. It was the silence of paper.

Then there was a third voice on the frequency, and it was not Junie's.

"Drift, this is Atoll, secondary set. Stand by for an older hand," Junie said. And then, lower and slower and with a grain in it that was not the static, another man spoke.

"Drift, this is Atoll. I answer to an old call sign and it is not important. Junie has handed me the set because I know this water and she says you have left the line. Read me your course. Over."

She read him her course.

He was old, she could tell by the voice, but there was a steadiness under the hoarseness that was good and she liked it. He asked her the same questions a surveyor asks. Position, twice, with the run of the log. Wind by the cardinal and by the degree. Swell by the period and the direction. Barometer, and the trend of it over three hours, and the sea state on both sides of the band she was in. She gave him everything Junie could not have used, and he took it and wrote it and did not tell her what she already knew, and when she had finished he asked for the one number he had not asked for yet, which was the water temperature on either side of the band, and she dipped her finger and told him, and he went quiet for a moment as if that were the piece he had been waiting for.

He did not hurry. He asked for a number, and then there was a period of paper, and then he asked for the next number, and Nella stood in the companionway with the microphone and gave him each one as he asked it, and the two of them worked through her position and her wind and her water and her swell like two people going over a loft floor together, checking a sail, one holding the cloth and one marking the seam. It was the first time since she left the harbor that somebody had wanted her numbers for a reason she could respect. Junie had wanted them because she cared. This man wanted them because he knew what to do with them.

"Drift, stand by. I am going to read you the wind." A rustle of paper. "The stress curl has moved two degrees north since the fifteenth and it has moved west with it. The countercurrent on the north side of the Zone has built — that is the band you are in, and I am glad to hear it is there. The North Equatorial Current on the south side is running strong and narrow, below four north. The Zone is sitting at six north now, or a little above, and it has been moving up a degree in three days and it is still going."

He stopped, and Nella waited.

"I surveyed this water before your chart was ever printed," he said, "and I will give you what I have because you have earned the reading of it. The corridor is narrow. You will have nineteen, maybe twenty miles. Twenty miles wide. The temperature of the water tells you when you are in it — one degree, no more, cold on the north side, warm on the south. If you find cold water on your port hand you are out of it, and the wind will go light and the current will shear you, and you will lose a day getting back." A pause, paper moving. "The corridor runs north of any line that has been published. I have watched it walk north for twenty seasons and it will not walk back. Whatever you are carrying, carry it as a survey and not as a scripture. Take your own soundings. The water you are standing in is the only authority there is. Over."

"Atoll, Drift. Understood. Over."

"There is one more thing, and then I will let you sail," he said. "The Zone is moving north and it is moving on a schedule nobody has written down. My numbers will not hold another season. Watch it. Not the paper. The Zone. Watch where it is, and the day the countercurrent dies and the warmth goes out of the band, come north again. There will be a corridor north of the one you are in in another ten years. It will not be on the chart. There never was a chart for this one either. Over."

"Understood," Nella said. "Drift out."

She put the microphone down and sat with the last of it. She thought about the phrase he had used: the day the countercurrent dies and the warmth goes out of the band, come north again. It was a rule for somebody who intended to make the crossing more than once. It was a rule for a woman who would be alive in ten years and would still be sailing. And the old man on the atoll had given it to her without being asked, the way a sailmaker hands down a stitch. She had never seen his face and she did not know his name, and she had the sense, hearing the surveyor's flatness in his voice, that he had been on this water longer than the book had been in print and knew it better than the man who wrote it.

She went on deck in a good wind with a band of dark water under her keel and the box on the shelf behind her, and she looked aft at the water where the storm had been and forward at the water where she was going, and she found that she was not thinking about the paper at all.

She was thinking about the morning.

She was thinking about the light on the water at the northern edge of the band, and the colour of it, and the way the band had a seam that held, and the fact that she had looked at it and known what it was without having to go below and check a chart. That was the whole of it. She had known. The knowing did not come out of a book or out of an old man's voice on the radio. It came out of sixteen days of mornings, each one of them logged, each one of them a small careful reading of wind and swell and sky until the readings were not readings any more, they were the way she saw the world. She had set out with a charted route and a living woman's voice, and what she had now was neither. What she had was her own eyes and her own hands and her own log, and the log was honest, and that was the only weather report that mattered in the end.

The booby came back in the afternoon. It came out of the west and circled the mast twice and settled on the pulpit rail, and it did not beg and it did not leave. It sat with its beak tucked and its eye on the water, and Nella sat at the tiller and watched it and did not speak to it. After a while the bird lifted and went west on its own errand, the same direction she was going, and she watched it until it was a fleck on the band of dark water. She had stopped talking to the booby some days ago, out of a feeling she could not name. It had stopped being the one other living thing in the ocean and become a bird on its way somewhere, and she had stopped needing it to be anything else.

By mid-afternoon the band ran thin and she found the cold water on her port hand.

It showed in the log first. The log line went slack and the speed fell from five and a half to five, and then to four. The water beside the boat changed colour at a line as sharp as a seam, darker on her port hand, and she put a finger in her mouth and dipped it and it was the old water she had been in for sixteen days, and on the other side of the line was the band and the band was one shade warmer and the wind there was two points freer. She dipped the finger twice more, once on each side of the line, and the difference was there both times and it was the difference the old man had told her to look for. One degree. No more.

She had drifted out of her corridor on the northern edge, where the countercurrent met the old current and did not meet it kindly, and the old water had taken her keel and begun to shear her south.

She bore up and ran back two miles through the shear, close-hauled on the jib and the reefed main, and took the short choppy sea of the shear line on her bow, which was a wet and unpleasant hour. The bow went up and came down and put green water over the coach roof twice, and she stood at the tiller with her hat pulled down and her feet braced and did not move for the whole of it, because the tiller was the only thing she had and it wanted all of her. She thought nothing at all during that hour. That was the curious part of it, and she noticed it even while it was happening. There was no fear in it, and no calculation, and no memory. There was just the boat and the water and the tiller and the two miles to be made against the chop.

When she found the band again the sea went smooth and the wind came fair and she let the boat go and it went six knots, and she wrote in the log at the top of the next page, in her own hand, the first entry of the new course:

Day 17. Lost the band on the north edge in the cold water. Two miles back. Corridor is narrow, about nineteen miles across as he said, and the line is a hard one, not a gradual one. Rule holds. Water thirty gallons. Wind north, ten, fair. Sea smooth in the band. Running west-northwest of his line by one point seven.

She closed the log and put it on the shelf next to Reeve's and looked at the two books side by side: his oilcloth and her ruled paper, his thirty-six days and her seventeen, his ending at the stern of a boat with squalls out of the south, and hers at six knots on a west-northwest course with the wind fair. And she understood, looking at them, that she was no longer sailing his crossing at all. She had left his crossing somewhere behind, in the cold water on the north edge, and the thing she was on now had no lines on any chart and no days in any book but her own, and it was going where she was taking it, and it would arrive or it would not, and either way the decision was hers.

She reached up and touched the box on the shelf, lightly, with two fingers, the way you touch a bolt of cloth to see which way the nap runs. She did not open it. She did not need to. It sat there in its steel frame with the letters chiselled on the lid, and it would sit there until she made the atoll, and then it would go ashore, and it would go back to the man it belonged to, and the man would have to look at it. That was a thing she would have to do, and she did not know yet how it would go. But she knew that it would happen on land, and that it would happen after she arrived, and that arriving was a separate problem from this one, and the water in front of her was the only problem she had.

She went back on deck. The band ran west-northwest under the bow, dark and smooth and twenty miles wide, and the wind was north at ten and the sky was clear to the west and the atoll was still fourteen hundred miles away, which was a number she had not written down and did not intend to. She did not have the word for the feeling, and she did not try to find one. She just stood at the tiller and let the boat run, with the dark band under her and the grey wall gone into the west and the atoll somewhere ahead and the lamp, she hoped, still burning; and she kept her own counsel, and she kept her own course, and the Drift went west-northwest at six knots through a sea that had never been on paper.
