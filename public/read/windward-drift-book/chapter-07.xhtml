The Unseasonal Storm

The band held through the night and into the next day, and for a while it was the best sailing of the crossing.

She ran west-northwest at six knots with the wind on the starboard quarter, north at ten and then twelve, and the sea in the band was smooth and dark and ran with her as though it had been made to carry her. She slept in two-hour pieces, and each time she came on deck the band was under the bow, and the log line was singing, and the noon fix put her another hundred and fifty miles along. Whatever else it was, the corridor the old man had given her was real, and it was running, and it would have taken her all the way if the weather had let it.

On the eighteenth day the wind went light before dawn and by eight in the morning it was gone.

Not fallen. Gone. The sea in the band flattened to long low swells that came from everywhere and from nowhere, and the sails hung and slapped and then stopped slapping, and the Drift lay on a sea like a plate on a table, and the sun came up white and hard and there was no cloud anywhere in the sky except the cloud that ought not to have been there.

It was a bank of it, low and grey and massed, sitting off the port bow to the south and west, and she stood in the rigging with one arm around a shroud and looked at it for a long time. It had the wrong shape. It did not have the flat underside of a trade-wind cloud, and it did not have the high anvil of a squall, and it was not moving with the wind because there was no wind. It sat on the sea in a long wall that went from the southwest across to the west and faded off into haze at the north, and it had the look of something that was growing rather than travelling, the way a bruise grows.

She went below and got the barometer. 1009 and steady. She checked it again at nine and it was 1008. She checked it at ten and it was 1007, and the first long swell came through the flat sea from the southwest and lifted the boat and put her down again, and Nella understood the whole of it in that lift and that set-down the way she understood a length of cloth when she had it on the loft floor.

There was a storm out there. It had been out there for two days, building on water that was warmer than it should have been for the season, and it had eaten the wind for hundreds of miles around it, and the calm she was sitting in was not weather. The calm was the hole in the middle of the weather. It was the space the storm had cleared for itself before it began to move.

She called the atoll.

"Atoll, Atoll, Drift, Drift, on the main frequency. Over."

Nothing.

She waited a minute and called again, and then a third time, and the third time she got a voice out of the static that was not Junie's and was not the old man's and was not anything she could use. It came in pieces, like a sentence shouted through a wall. "Drift—" and then a long hiss, and then "—moving—" and then nothing at all.

She racked the microphone and did the arithmetic the way she had learned to do it, on her own, with the pencil. The barometer had fallen a millibar an hour for three hours. The swell was running two two five and the period was eleven seconds and shortening, which meant the storm was not far and coming. The storm was between her and the atoll, between her and Junie, between her and every voice she had, and the calm would hold her exactly where she was until the storm came over her or until she found a way around it.

She sat with the pencil and looked at her own ruled sheet and did not look at Reeve's chart, because she had used up Reeve's chart as a guide some days ago and there was nothing left on it but dead ink.

The old man's voice came back to her, slow and hoarse, the way it had come across two thousand miles of water when Junie first handed him the set. Watch the zone, he had said. Not the paper. The water. She had written that in her log and ruled a sheet by it, and now the paper she was watching was a wall of cloud on the wrong side of the season, and the water under her keel was telling her what the wall was doing before the barometer did.

The first thing she did was to take the calm seriously.

A calm is not nothing. A calm in the wrong place, with a storm on three sides of it, is a trap with the door closing, and the door was the wind. If she sat in the middle and waited, the storm would arrive and take her, and a small sloop caught in the open by a system that had eaten its own wind would be rolled or dismasted or both. If she tried to go south and outrun it, she would run into the storm's southern arm, which was the worst of it. If she tried to go west, she would go straight in. The only door that was open was the one that was always open and never marked, and it was somewhere on the north edge of the wall, where the storm's wind ran one way and the trades ran the other and between them, for a short time, there was a corridor of running wind.

She took her bearings and drew the wall.

She drew what she could see: the mass of cloud from southwest to west, the eastern edge of it, the way the haze thickened toward the north. Then she took her sextant and shot the sun, twice, and worked out a position, and put it on the sheet, and looked at where the storm's centre had to be to make the swell she was feeling. Two two five. That put the centre somewhere to her south and west, a hundred and fifty miles or more, and that put her north of the storm's track, and that was the single piece of luck she had. She was on the north side. The corridor would be on the north side. The door, if there was one, was not behind her and not to the south. It was ahead and to her right hand, somewhere along the line where the wall met the trade wind, and she had to find it before the storm overran it.

She started the boat moving. There was no wind, so she put out every scrap of light canvas she had and used the swell to make the boat work, and by noon she had worked a slow mile to the north and a mile to the west, and the swell was running two four zero now, and the wall was visibly closer, and the top of it had begun to feather into a single long cloud that lay southeast to northwest and pointed, like a finger, at the north.

That was the door. She did not know it yet, but she wrote it down, and writing it down made her watch it, and watching it for two hours made her sure. The cloud finger pointed north-northwest, and at the end of it, where it thinned into haze, the sky was not flat grey. There was a line of darker blue under it, and under that line the light was different. Wind makes a different light on water. Every sailor knows the look of it, the long soft riffles that run with the wind, and there was a band of it out there to the northwest, and it was moving, and it was her door.

She had maybe six hours. The barometer said eight and the barometer was falling faster now, and the wall said six, and since she had learned weeks ago that the barometer lies when the sea is telling the truth, she took the sea's number and she took six.

She spent the next two hours getting the boat ready, and she did it the way she did everything, in order, without hurrying and without stopping.

The main came down and stayed down. She had run the third reef through the storm that had passed her by, but this was a different thing. This was wind that would come out of the corridor at thirty, maybe forty, running with a sea that had no fetch to build but plenty of storm swell to steepen. She bent the storm jib on and hanked it down and then bent the second storm jib — the small one she had cut down from the old working jib in the harbor — and hanked that too, so that if the first blew she would have the second already on the stay.

She went over the rigging wire by wire. Shrouds, turnbuckles, the pins, the cotter pins, the spreader boots, the two bolts at the masthead that held the whole thing up. She looked at each one and shook it and put her hand on it, and where a cotter pin was bent or worn she took it out and put in a new one, and she had nine spares in the tin box and she used four. She taped over the shackle pins and the turnbuckles with rigging tape so nothing would shake loose or chafe a sail, and she checked the tape to make sure it went the right way round the wire, the way she had done a thousand times on other people's boats in other people's harbors.

She lashed the tiller for the run and set the vane on the quarter, because she would need both hands and both feet for the rigging and the sails and could not stand steering. Then she packed the watertight bucket fresh and added the little brass compass, since the storm would come out of the north and the north was where she was going. First-aid tin. Flares. Hand-bearing compass. Spare radio. She lashed the bucket to the mast.

Then she filled the water sack and hung it in the cockpit, and she ate standing up — a full biscuit, a piece of the tinned fish, one cup of water, no ration, because a long run needs a fed body — and she changed into dry wool and put the second sweater in the bucket.

She finished an hour before she had to, and she used that hour to go forward to the bow and stand there, holding the pulpit with both hands, and read the wall.

From the bow the door was clear.

The storm's whole western face ran from the south-southwest up to the north, a wall of cloud that went from dark slate at the bottom to grey at the top, and the wall was turning. The wind that had eaten the trades for two days was now beginning to heave the whole thing north, and as it came, the finger of cloud at the top bent east, and the band of running wind on the north edge widened and then narrowed again and widened again, breathing with the storm's edge. The door was there. It was real. It was not a hole in the wall — it was a gate in the wall, and the gate was moving, and the two winds that had made it were fighting over water that had no name on any chart she carried.

She went aft and took in the staysail and set only the working jib, then let it draw on the first breath of wind — a soft air out of the northwest, five knots, no more — and the Drift began to move. She sheeted the jib in and the boat picked up to two knots, and the band of riffles was five miles off and coming at her, and the wall behind it was coming at her too, and the two of them would reach her at nearly the same time.

She called the atoll once more, the last call she would make for a while, because she did not know what the next two nights would do to the mast or the set or her.

"Atoll, Atoll, Drift, Drift, main frequency. Junie, this is Nella. I have found a door in the storm and I am going to run it. Two nights, maybe three. If you do not hear from me by the twenty-second, do not worry." She paused. "Do not worry, Junie. I will call you when I come out the other side. Over."

The static hissed. And then, very faint and very far, a piece of a voice came through it. "Drift—" and a long wash of it, and then "—go—" and then "—the lamp—" and then nothing.

She held the microphone a moment and then put it down and lashed the set. It was enough. It was not what she would have chosen, but it was what the sky had left her, and it was enough to know that somewhere under that static was a woman in a radio room with a lamp in the window.

She turned and faced forward.

The band of wind reached her first. It came in from the northwest, cool and steady and real, and the jib went taut and the Drift leaned and began to run, and the wall came behind it, and the corridor opened in front of her like a seam pulled straight, and she took the boat into it and did not look back.

The first night was wind. The second night was worse.

She wrote nothing down during either of them, because there was no time and no free hand, and what she remembered afterwards she remembered the way you remember a long fall — in pieces, and in the body rather than the mind.

The wind came out of the corridor at thirty-five knots and stayed there, and the sea that built under it was not the storm's sea, it was the corridor's own sea, short and steep and close, running north-northwest under a sky that was boiling on one side and clear on the other. She kept the small storm jib on the stay and never touched it, and the Drift went before the wind at six and seven and eight knots and put her shoulder down and stayed there, and Nella sat in the cockpit with the tiller between her legs and the vane only a help and not a hand, and the black water went by the rail all night, and the wind screamed in the rigging in a single note that changed only when she altered course.

She took the helm in two-hour watches and let the vane steer in the hours between, and she slept in the cockpit for forty minutes at a stretch, curled in the lee of the coaming with her arms through the safety line, and each time she woke the sky had changed a small degree and the wall had gained on her or fallen back and the corridor had narrowed or widened, and each time she corrected the course by the stars when she could see them and by the swell when she could not.

The swell ran north-northwest. The wind ran north-northwest. She held the boat between them, and as long as the two agreed she was in the door. Twice in the night the wind went east on her and she had to bear up to find it again, and once the swell went round to due north and the wind stayed west of it, and she put the helm down and ran west of north for an hour until the swell came round to meet her, and when it did she felt the corridor come back under her keel the way you feel a shoal come back under the lead — by the change in the way the boat worked.

The second night the wind rose. It was forty and then it was more, and the sea went from short and steep to short and violent, and she shortened down to the smallest cloth she had and put the boat before it and rode. There was a moment, an hour before dawn, when the whole wall seemed to come down on her and the rain came out of it in sheets so thick that the bow was a memory, and in that hour she could see nothing and hear nothing but the water, and she steered entirely by the feel of the helm in her hands and the heel of the boat under her and the deep growl of the swell on her quarter.

She was not afraid. She noticed that, the way she had noticed it in the shear, and it was not courage, and she did not pretend it was. It was the narrowness of the work. There was no room in her for fear, because fear is about what might happen and she had only the one second she was in, and the one second she was in wanted both her hands and her whole attention. The wind was the wind. It was neither hostile nor generous. It was simply enormous, and it was running, and she was running with it, and the only thing that mattered was whether she held the line.

Dawn came grey and hard, and the rain stopped, and she saw the corridor again. It had narrowed to something like four miles, and the wall was on her starboard hand, close, and on her port hand the sky was clearing to the east, and the corridor ran northwest between them like a seam between two bolts of different cloth. She held the line. She held it all that second day, and the wind came down slowly from forty to thirty and then to twenty-five, and the sea with it, and by the second dusk she had put the corridor and the storm's north edge together into one long run that took her out of the wall's reach and into open water on the far side.

She wrote the log at dusk on the second day, sitting in the cockpit with the pencil and the wet page.

Day 20. Ran the corridor. Two nights. Storm passed south and west of the track, north edge held, corridor opened northwest and closed behind me. Wind was thirty-five to forty-five at the height, sea short and steep. Lost the corridor twice and found it by the swell. No damage. Mainsail stays down. Water twenty-eight gallons. Position two degrees north of the line. Running northwest.

She closed the log. Her hands were so tired that holding the pencil had been work, and she sat with her back against the mast for a long while and watched the last of the storm go off into the west, a long grey curtain that no longer had anything to do with her. The sea was still high and the boat was still working, but the wind had gone down and the sky over her head was clear and there were stars coming out, and she read them the way she had read them every clear night of the crossing, the old familiar ones, and fixed her position from the two she could see through the gap in the clouds.

She thought about the door and about the six hours and about the two nights, and about the arithmetic of it, which was this: she had no idea whether she would have been safe if she had stayed in the calm, because there was no way to run the experiment twice. But she knew what she had read. She knew the swell and she knew the cloud and she knew the light on the water, and she had made the decision on her own, with no voice on the radio to confirm it and no chart to check it against, and she had been right. And the being right was the smallest part of it. The important part was something else, and it came to her now, sitting against the mast with her tired hands in her lap.

The important part was that she had been alone, and it had not been a lonely thing. It had been an attentive thing. Every reading of the water, every lift of the swell on her quarter, every look at the cloud had been a small act of care, and to care for something — to watch it that closely, to want to get it right — is not isolation. It is the same careful attention she gave to a sail on the loft floor, and it was not about her at all, it was about the thing she was trying to understand.

She sat with that for a while and did not say anything out loud, because there was no one to say it to and no need.

Then the radio crackled, and a voice came out of it.

"Drift, Atoll. Drift, do you read me. Over."

She got up and went below, steadying herself against everything, and picked up the microphone.

"Atoll, Drift," she said. "I read you. I came out the other side. Over."

There was a pause on the line, and then the voice that came through it was a young woman's voice, warm and clear, and it was very tired, and it was smiling.

"I know," Junie said. "I have been calling you every half hour for two days. Your lamp is still on. Over."

Nella laughed, and it hurt, and she did not care.

"Understood," she said. "Drift out."

She left the set keyed a moment longer than she needed to, the way you hold a hand after you have finished shaking it, and then she let it go.

Outside the dark had come, and the swell was lifting and setting the little boat with a long slow motion, and ahead and to the west-northwest was the atoll, and the lamp, and the rest of the distance. She went up on deck and took the tiller, and she pointed the Drift northwest into the clearing sky, and she kept her own counsel, and she kept her own course, and the wind blew fair.
