Running the Corridor

She came out of it on the morning of the twenty-second day, and for a while there was nothing to do but look.

The storm was behind her and to the south and west, a long grey curtain that had drawn itself up off the sea and stood along the horizon like the wall of a room she had just left. The corridor she had run was closed. The wind had come down to twenty knots and then to eighteen and then to a steady fifteen out of the west-northwest, and the Drift was running before it with the small storm jib set and the tiller vane working and no hand on the helm, and the whole boat was quiet in the way a boat goes quiet when a long strain comes off it.

She slept for four hours in the morning and woke at noon and could not believe the sky.

It was the first clear noon in days. There was not a cloud in it except the high white streaks the trades make when they are set, and the sun stood almost overhead and burned the colour back into the sea, and the sea had gone from the grey-black of the corridor to the pale grey-green she had come to know as the Drift's own water, the water with the long yellow weed-streaks in it. She sat in the cockpit and ate a full biscuit and drank two cups of water instead of one, because her body asked for it and the arithmetic would be what it would be, and she looked at her hands.

They were not her hands. The palms had gone to rope, three blisters broken and healed into callus, one split across the base of the left thumb where the sheet had taken her skin off in the second night. Her forearms were rope too, hard and thin. She had not looked at her face since before the gate.

She drew the log out from under the dodger and wrote the entry, and the pencil moved badly in her hand and she did not care about that either.

Day 22. Clear. Wind WNW, 15. Sea moderating. Ran the corridor two nights. Position by star fix at dawn: two and a half degrees north of Reeve's line, ninety miles west of the atoll's meridian. Running NW under storm jib. Water twenty-eight gallons. Rig sound. No damage found forward — will check again when the sea lies down. The storm is behind me. The atoll is ahead.

Then she went forward, because the sea would let her, and she went over the rig the way she had gone over it before the run.

It took her most of the afternoon, and it was the old work, the sailmaker's work, and she found what she always found.

The port shroud had chafed a strand of its serving where the storm jib's sheet had borne against it in the second night, and she parceled and served it fresh with tarred twine and her palm and her needle, sitting in the bow with her legs braced against the pulpit, and the little boat lifting under her with the long swell. The storm jib's luff rope had pulled two stitches at the head. She sewed them with linen twine doubled, the way she sewed everything, flat and close, and the cloth was sound around them. The mainsail she left in its gaskets, because it had taken no strain at all down on the boom, and the working jib she hanked back on the stay and left furled. The masthead bolts were tight. The turnbuckles had not moved. The tape she had run the day before the gate was worn through in one place and she retaped it, and when she was done she sat in the bow with the spray coming over her and let the whole thing be true.

She did not take out Reeve's chart. She had put it under the bunk on the fifteenth day and she had not wanted it since, and the reason she left it there now was not anger. It was that the chart had done its work and was finished, the way a sail is finished when the seam under the patch has failed and you have cut the cloth back and sewn new cloth into sound cloth. She took out her own ruled sheet instead and laid it on the cabin table in the light of a clear afternoon, and she drew her noon position on it, and looked at what the line was now.

The line ran northwest by north. It had run that way since the sixth day, when the current first showed her its grain. It was a line of her own sightings and her own steering, and the atoll was not on Reeve's line anymore, because the countercurrent had carried her north and west of where any chart put her, and if she held northwest by north another few days the atoll would come up off the starboard bow. That was the whole of the navigation, and it had been the whole of the navigation since the first week, and she had done it without a working compass. She sat with the pencil in her rope hand and understood, quietly and without any heat in it, that the crossing she would arrive by was not the crossing she had sailed out of the harbor to make. She had left the Last Harbor to repeat a man's route. She would arrive at the atoll by a course that was not on his chart and would not be on any chart. She would arrive by it because she had read the water and kept the line and trusted the reading, and there was no one to tell and no one's name to put on it.

She sat with that a while. It was not a wound and it was not a triumph. It was the plain shape of the thing, and she found that she could hold it without wanting to put it down.

The wind freshened at dusk, which is the way the trades often work, and by dark it was eighteen and then twenty, and the sea was lying down from the storm into a long hard-running swell out of the west, and the Drift began to run in earnest.

She went up on deck and changed down to the working jib and hanked the storm jib into the bag on the stay and sheeted the working jib flat, and with the wind on the quarter and the swell behind her the little boat picked up six knots and then seven and held there, and the log line sang, and the white water on her quarter rose and broke and went by the rail in a long hiss that was the best sound in the world. She set the vane and lashed the tiller and sat on the aft deck with the stars coming out over her head and did not touch a thing.

That was the night she understood the thing she had been trying to name since the first morning out of the harbor, and it came to her not as a thought about her life but as a fact about the water.

The ocean did not care about her. That was the first half of it, and she had known it since the first week. It did not care about her sails or her water or her hands. It ran west because it ran west. It had made this storm out of water warmer than it should have been for the season, and the storm had eaten the trades for hundreds of miles and then gone off to the south and west to spend itself on empty sea, and it had done all of that without any reference to a small sloop with a sailmaker on board and a route inherited from a dead man's published page. The ocean was not kind and it was not cruel. It was enormous and it was indifferent, and on a night like this, when it ran with you, the indifference was the truest thing about it and there was no comfort to be drawn from it, and Nella had made her peace with that weeks ago.

And then there was the other half, and it was the part she had not been able to say.

The ocean was indifferent and it was generous at the same time, and the two were not a contradiction. The storm had eaten the wind, and the eating of the wind had made the calm, and the calm had made the corridor, and the corridor had run her west-northwest at six and seven and eight knots for two nights, and none of it had been for her. No one had built that corridor for her. It was not in her chart and it would not be in any chart. It was simply a place where two winds had fought for a while over water, and a woman who read the swell had found the place and used it, and the using of it had cost the sea nothing. The generosity of the ocean was not a gift. It was the fact that the ocean was so large that somewhere in it, always, at any hour, there was wind running the way a person needed it to run, if the person could find it. The sea would not bring it to you. The sea did not know your name. But it was so large that the wind was in it somewhere, and the whole work of a crossing was to be the kind of sailor who could find the running wind and run it.

She sat with that for a long while on the aft deck with the wake going away behind her in the starlight, and it was not a sad thing and it was not a happy thing. It was the way she felt after a long seam pulled straight on the loft floor, the cloth running true under her hand and no praise in the world and no need for any.

Then the radio crackled and the voice came out of it, and it was warm.

"Drift, Atoll. Drift, how is the sea. Over."

She went below and took the microphone, and her hand was steadier on the set than it had been on the pencil at noon.

"Atoll, Drift. Sea's lying down, wind's WNW at twenty, running northwest by north at seven knots. Rig sound. Over."

There was a pause on the line, and then Junie's voice came back, and it had something in it that Nella had not heard in it before, which was the thing a person sounds like when they have been holding their own weight for a long time and can put it down.

"Seven knots," Junie said. "That is very good. Hold that line, Nella." A paper sound. "Let me read it to you properly. You are two hundred and six miles from the atoll's south light, bearing two four five, and the north edge of your track is drifting with you, and if you hold northwest by north and no more north than that, you will raise us on the third day from this noon. Over."

Two hundred and six miles. Three days. Nella wrote it on the ruled sheet and looked at it, and the line she had drawn was ninety miles west of where she had put it at noon, which was the current, which was always the current. She had been running three miles an hour with the wind and two with the water and it had carried her west, and the atoll was not three days northwest, it was three days west-northwest, and that was the difference between a plan and a fix, and she was glad of it, because a plan would have left her a hundred and forty miles off the light and a fix had told her the truth.

"Understood," she said. "Bearing two four five, two hundred and six miles, three days. Hold northwest by north. Over."

"Hold northwest by north," Junie said. "I have the lamp in the window. It has been in the window since the tenth day. Over."

Nella looked at the set for a moment and did not answer straight away, and then she did.

"Junie," she said, and stopped, because there was no rule for the next thing and no protocol, and she said it anyway. "You ran on the battery and the hand-crank when the lightning took the mast, and you called me every half hour for two days, and you kept the lamp lit through a storm that took the trades for a week. That is not a weather service. I do not know what it is. Thank you. Over."

The static hissed for a second and then Junie's voice came out of it, and the warmth in it had gone a little unsteady.

"It is the job," she said, and then, "no. It is not the job. I am sitting here with a lamp in the window and a hand-crank radio and I am the only person for two thousand miles who knows where you are, and I am not going to be the one who puts the lamp out. That is all. Hold northwest by north. Atoll out."

The set clicked. Nella sat with the microphone in her lap and looked at the ruled sheet with the fix on it and the bearing on it, and the boat ran on in the dark with the vane steering and the wake going away behind her, and out to the west-northwest, two hundred and six miles away, there was an atoll with a woman on it and a lamp in a window, and the two of them had made a crossing between them.

The wind came up in the second night, and it came up fast.

She had been running seven knots at dusk and she was running eight at ten and the log line was singing higher, and out of the darkness ahead and to the port bow there was a low bank of cloud coming out of the northwest that the stars had not been saying anything about at dusk. She went up on deck and stood by the mast with her hand on the rig and read it the way she had learned to read everything, and it had the wrong shape, and behind it the wind was going round to the northwest and freshening.

It was the storm. It had not spent itself. It had turned, the way a system does when it finds the warm water again, and it had come round to the north and was reaching back for the corridor she had run, one last arm of it, one last push before it died.

She had not expected it. She had put it behind her on the morning of the twenty-second day, and it had been behind her, and now it was on her port bow and closing, and the arithmetic was simple. She had a hundred and thirty miles to run to the atoll's south light and the storm's north edge was coming back on her at whatever speed the trades gave it and she had perhaps one night to make the distance before the wall came over her track.

She took the helm herself off the vane, because the vane would hold a course and she needed to run the fastest course, and they were not always the same line. She laid the Drift off a quarter point to the south of northwest by north, which would put the wind a little more astern and let the working jib draw full and put her down the leading face of the swell, and the little boat picked up a half knot and then a knot, and the log line hit nine, and Nella sat with the tiller between her knees and the wind coming over her left shoulder and the black water going by the rail all night, and she did with the last night what she had done with the first two, which was to hold the line and read the water and not think about what might happen, only what was.

The sea built under the returning wind and the swell came short and steep out of the west-northwest and ran with her, and the Drift surfed the leading faces and put her shoulder down and shipped water over the bow and threw it off, and the whole small boat was singing, and Nella never left the helm. She shortened down to the storm jib at midnight when the wind hit twenty-eight, and she held the storm jib all night and never had to go smaller, and the wind held between twenty-five and thirty-two and the storm's wall held off to the north and west and never closed, and at half past four in the morning the wind began to come down the way it does at the end of a blow, and at five the sky ahead went pale, and at half past five she saw the light.

It was a small light, low on the horizon, almost lost in the last of the morning haze, and it did not flinch and it did not move, and it was exactly where it had said it would be, bearing two four five, and it was the atoll's south light. The whole crossing had been sailing toward it since the first afternoon out of the Last Harbor, and she had not known it, and now she knew it, and the boat went on toward it under the last of the night wind with the sea running green and clean under her bow.

She did not shout and she did not cry. She took up the microphone and called the atoll.

"Atoll, Atoll, Drift, Drift," she said. "I have the south light. I have your south light bearing two four five. Wind's coming down. I am one hundred and thirty miles out and running. Over."

And out of the static, warm and clear and very tired and very glad, the voice came back.

"Drift, Atoll," Junie said. "I see you. I have you on the bearing sweep. Come in careful. The reef is four miles off the light and the passage is north of it, and I will bring you in. There is a man on the quay who has been waiting for you for a long time and I do not know what to tell him yet. Over."

Nella held the microphone and looked at the light and the pale morning rising over the atoll, and the water running green and clean under the bow, and the ruled sheet on the cabin table with her own line on it and the bearing on it, and the steel box on the shelf with the letters chiselled on the lid, which belonged to a man who had turned back on the thirtieth day and was not dead and did not know she was coming.

"Tell him nothing yet," she said. "I am bringing him something. Drift out."

She put the microphone down and racked it, and took the tiller and pointed the boat at the light, and the wind blew fair behind her, and the ocean was enormous and indifferent and generous all at once, and the last run of the crossing had begun.
