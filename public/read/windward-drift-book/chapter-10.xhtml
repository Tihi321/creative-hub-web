The Return

The bench was wood, and it had been rained on and sunned on for a long time, and when Nella sat down on the far end of it the wood gave a little and held. The old man did not turn his head. He was looking at the Drift, where she lay in the dark water with her mast working a slow circle against the lamp on the quay.

She put the box on the bench between them.

It was a steel box about two feet long and a foot wide and eight inches deep, dull as a stone and stippled all over with a fine orange freckle of rust that had dried and gone matte after twenty days in her cabin. The lid still carried the stencilled letters she had cut the padlock off to open. H. REEVE. WINDWARD DRIFT. DO NOT REMOVE.

He looked at the box for a while. He did not look at her.

"You found it," he said. His voice was hoarse and slow, and the words came one at a time, the way they come from a man who has not had much occasion to use them. "I put it on the survey buoy with two anchors and eighty feet of line so it would hold station through a spring tide. I gave it a hundred and ten days of drift left in her. That was the arithmetic. It was 1971 and I thought it would run aground or be picked up inside two seasons."

"It was south of the zone," Nella said. "On a long curve. It had fouled itself in weed, and the weed had grown around the mooring swivel, and the swivel had frozen. It was not going anywhere. It would have stayed there, going round in the same water, for another forty years."

"Forty-one," he said. He said it without heat. "It is forty-one years since I set that buoy adrift. I have been counting it."

She sat beside him and worked out what to say and found that she had already worked it out, on the way up the quay, with the box under her arm. She had spent the whole crossing building an argument and now that she was here the argument was not the shape of the thing.

"I opened it," she said.

"You would have had to."

"I read the log."

He nodded, and the nod was small, and it was not the nod of a man who is being accused. He turned his head at last and looked at the box, and then at her, and his face was old the way driftwood is old, weathered down to whatever was underneath and holding to it.

"Then you know," he said. "Thirty days out. I put the helm over on the thirtieth day and ran back east-northeast and I was in the Last Harbor on the thirty-ninth. I wrote the crossing in the winter and it was reprinted four times and I never corrected it and I never went back to sea. That is the whole of it. There is nothing else in the box, is there."

"There is the brass compass," she said. "There is a chart and there is twine and there is the last of the hardtack, which is not worth anything to anybody. There is the log. That is all. I did not open the hardtack. I did not need to. I had my own."

"The compass," he said. "What did it read."

"Nineteen degrees east of where it should have read," Nella said. "The same as mine. The same as every compass in the Drift. You wrote it in the log. Thirty-one, then forty-four, then spinning. Mine never spun, but it was wrong by the same amount for the whole of the way across, which is worse than spinning, because spinning tells you to stop believing it."

He was quiet for a while. Out beyond the reef the swell was breaking on the coral in the long slow white she had watched all afternoon, and it was still breaking there, at the same rate, in the same place, the way it had been doing since before anyone had a name for this piece of water.

"I stopped believing it," he said. "It took me thirty days."

"It took me nineteen," she said. "And I had your chart the whole way and I was reading it wrong."

He made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a cough.

"There is a thing I have wondered for forty-one years," he said, "sitting on this bench, and I am going to ask it and then I am going to stop asking. Did you follow my line. The whole way. Did you follow the line in the book."

"I followed it for five days," Nella said. "Then it stopped being the same water. The current had moved south of the arrows by most of a degree and I put the helm over and held north of them. Then the wind stress curl moved north and dragged the corridor with it, and by the tenth day your arrows were pointing into water that had gone the other way. I hauled a buoy aboard on the sixteenth day and read your log, and after that I drew my own chart." She laid her hand flat on the lid of the box. "I am not telling you this because you were wrong to write the book. I am telling you because you asked, and because if you had not written the book I would not have left the harbor at all. I went out on your route. There would have been no route without it."

"It was not a route," he said. "It was a summer."

"Yes," she said. "That is what I found out."

The lamplight from the head of the quay came far enough up the stone to lie across the bench in a long yellow shape, and Nella watched a moth work at the edge of it. The old man had not touched the box. His hands were on his knees, and the rope was over his shoulder, and the brass sextant case sat on the bench on his other side.

"Tell me about the crossing," he said. "Not the course. I have read every course. Tell me what the water was doing."

So she told him. She told him about the last harbor at four in the morning, and the cask that cracked at the chime and then at the waterline, and the mainsail that split five feet from the clew in a squall that came with no warning, and the day and a half under the jib while she sewed a flat seam with a herringbone patch over it and the needle going in and out of wet cloth with her fingers stiff. She told him about the booby that came on the fourteenth morning and walked the length of the boom on its flat feet and left at noon and did not come back. She told him about the steel box lashed to the stalk of the buoy with the weed grown thick around the mooring swivel, and how she had cut the padlock with a hacksaw and laid everything out on the chart table one piece at a time and put the twine and the hardtack back and kept the compass in her pocket because a second wrong opinion was better than none.

She told him about the log, and reading it, and finding that his true numbers were true numbers and that the book had been those same numbers arranged into a lie he had never gone back to correct. She told him she had not been angry. She told him the feeling had been the clean cold of a sailmaker opening a seam and finding the wrong cloth underneath, which is a particular kind of cold, and that she had folded the log and gone on deck because there was nothing else to be.

She told him about the long nights and the fish and the rice and the bread when the bread went the way bread goes, and about how she stopped talking to the booby when the booby did not come back, and about the Zone, and what it did.

"The zone," he said. "Where did you put it."

"Six north," Nella said. "And a quarter, and moving up about a degree in three days. It has been walking north for forty years, and the corridor walks with it. Your corridor is forty years south and thirty miles wide and gone. The one I ran was nineteen to twenty and marked by a single degree of water temperature, and the man who told me that told me his own numbers would not hold another season."

He was very quiet.

"Nineteen miles," he said. "Mine was a hundred and ten. A hundred and ten miles wide. You could not miss it. That is why the book sold. You could not miss it and you could not get lost and that is the only reason I was brave enough to write it down." He looked at her. "You ran a corridor nineteen miles wide."

"By hand and by eye for two nights," she said. "The second night I ran it on the cloud finger and the light on the water."

"I would not have found it," he said.

"No," she said. "You would not have. That is not a reproach. It did not exist when you were here."

He reached down and put his hand flat on the lid of the box, and his hand was spotted and the tendons stood up on the back of it, and he did not open it.

"I am going to tell you a thing I have never said to a living person," he said. "On the thirtieth day I was sitting where I am sitting now, in a manner of speaking, in the cockpit of a steel cutter, and the wind had gone south of where the book says it went, and my compass was reading forty-four degrees east and I was calling it an instrument fault. I had a radio and I had an engine and I had a crate of tinned beef and I had every advantage a man could want out there and I was not one day lost. And I put the helm over not because I could not find my way but because I had begun to understand that the water did not know who I was. It did not care about the book. It did not care about the crossing. It did not care whether I made it or turned back, and the second I understood that I turned back, because a man who cannot be seen cannot be brave, and I wanted to be brave more than I wanted to be at sea." He took his hand off the lid. "There. That is what is in me. You can carry it back out with you if you want."

Nella sat with that.

"I know what that is," she said. "I felt it for the first time on the eleventh day. The current was setting me north and the chart said south and there was nobody out there who was ever going to see me be right or wrong, and I sat in the cockpit and held the tiller and thought: this is the whole thing, this is the entire test, and I am going to fail it or I am not, and there is no one to witness either way." She turned her hands over in the lamplight. "And then I understood that I had got it backwards. There was somebody. There was Junie, calling twice a day on a radio she was winding by hand, and there was me, listening for her at the top of the hour, and the attention was the thing. That is what I found out. Not that I could do it alone. That I was never doing it alone."

The old man looked at her for a long moment.

"I did not have that," he said. "I had a radio. There was no one on the other end of it who was winding anything by hand."

"No," she said. "There wasn't." She stood up. "The log is yours. The chart is yours. The hardtack is nobody's. I am going to sit down with Junie tomorrow, because I have not had a meal in a chair in twenty-six days, and then I am going to sleep for a very long time, and then I am going to fix the Drift, because her third reef has chafed through the leech and it is not going to fix itself." She hesitated. "And if you want to know what the water is doing now, you can ask me. I am going to be here for a while. I have not decided how long. I do not have to decide."

"I have been sitting on this bench for twenty-six days," he said, "because I heard the first radio call from a woman who said she was going to follow the route, and I wanted to be the one who told her what I could not tell her over a printed page. And then you did the route without me, and I have been sitting here ever since not knowing how to put down forty-one years of not saying anything." He looked at the box. "You did the last thing for me. I am going to go home and open this, and I am going to be quiet for a while, and then I am going to come down here, and if you are still here, I might ask you what the water is doing. That is all."

He stood. It took him a while, and he got the rope over his shoulder and the sextant case in his hand, and he did not offer her the box and she did not offer to carry it, because it was his to carry and he had earned the carrying.

"The name on the bench," Nella said. "The one worn away."

"It is mine," he said. "They cut it in forty-one years ago, when the book sold. It is nearly gone. That is all right. A name on a bench is not a crossing."

He went up the quay with the box under his arm and the sextant case in his hand, and his shadow went ahead of him into the lamplight and then out of it, and Nella stood at the bench and watched until he turned off between the shed and the store and was gone.

Junie was waiting at the head of the quay with the lamp still in the window behind her and her cap pushed back, and she had two mugs of something hot in her hands.

"He went home," Junie said. "He has not gone home since you called from the last harbor. He has slept out here every night. Anker has been bringing him tea." She held out a mug. "It is not good tea. It is island tea. There is nothing else."

Nella took the mug and sat down on the steps at the head of the quay because sitting on a step was as far as her legs were prepared to go, and she drank the tea, which was hot and weak and tasted of dust and sugar, and it was the best thing that had been in her mouth since the Last Harbor.

"Did you tell him anything," she said.

"No," Junie said. "You told me not to, and I said I would not, and I did not, for twenty-six days, and I have never once in my life been asked to keep something that long and I will tell you now it was harder than the radio." She sat down on the step above and put her mug on her knee. "What was in the box, Nella. He has gone home with it. I am the only person on this island who does not know."

Nella looked out at the lagoon. The Drift lay where she had left her, off the quay, with her mast making the same slow circle against the light, and the surf was breaking on both jaws of the reef the way it had all evening, and beyond the reef the Drift ran out black and enormous to the edges of everything.

"A log," she said. "His crossing. The one he wrote down and then wrote again for the book. He turned back on the thirtieth day and he never crossed, and he set the log and the compass and the last of his hardtack adrift on a survey buoy because he wanted whoever found it to know the route could not be made, and no one found it for forty-one years, and I found it on the sixteenth day and I read it and I drew my own chart and I came in on that." She drank some more of the tea. "He asked me if I had followed his line. I said I had followed it for five days and then never again. He did not mind. He said it had been a summer, and I said that was what I had found out."

Junie was quiet for a minute, and then she said, "He has been sitting on that bench since your first call because he thought a boat was coming that would finish what he started."

"And a boat came," Nella said. "It just did not do what he expected. It did what the water was doing instead."

She finished the tea and stood up. The lamp in the window was lit behind them both, and it had been lit for twenty-six days, and Junie had wound a hand-crank every half hour through a lightning strike and a storm and the long empty middle of a crossing she was not on.

"You can take the lamp out of the window now," Nella said. "You can. I am ashore."

"No," Junie said. "I am not going to take it out. There is a man on another island somewhere who is going to sail this and you are going to be the one talking to him, and I want it in the window when you do." She picked up both mugs. "I will leave it in. Someone will need it. It is not doing anything sitting in a drawer."

Nella stood at the head of the quay for a while after Junie had gone up to the station, and looked back at the ocean, which was enormous and indifferent, and which had given her wind and had taken her water and had set her the whole test of the thing and had let her come through it, and she understood that she had crossed it and that she had never once crossed it alone. Her hands smelled of rope and salt and the tea. She could hear the surf on the reef, breaking in the same long rhythm, on both hands at once, which meant she was in the middle, which had been the whole rule and which she had used coming in, and which she found now that she was going to keep using for whatever she did next.

She looked at the lamp in the window above her and then at the black water beyond the reef, and she turned and walked up the quay, and her own footsteps on the stone were the only sound she was making, and that was all right, because the lamp was lit behind her and she knew exactly where she was.
