The Lost Cask

The crack crept the way cracks in that kind of plastic do, slowly and then not slowly, along the grain of the moulding, and Nella found the new half-inch of it on the eleventh morning when she went through the lazarette with her head down and her hands out like a blind woman. The drum was upside down, bung down, as she had lashed it. The hairline had run past the waterline. There was a dark bloom on the cloth wrapped around it, an inch wide and widening, and the sole under it was wet, and the wet was fresh.

She put her tongue to her finger. Fresh water. She had been pumping fresh water over the side for two days without knowing, and her ledger said thirty-one gallons and her ledger was a fiction.

She sat on the lazarette hatch a while. Then she did the thing properly, because doing it properly was all she had left of the harbor and its clean moorings and its cat on the sill.

She emptied the drum completely into the two emptiest jerry cans, which took half an hour of patient trickling through the funnel while the boat rolled down the long grey hills of the western Drift, and when the drum was light she took it out and held it up in the light from the hatch and looked at the crack in the plastic. It looked like nothing at all, a thread, a scratch, the kind of thing you would walk past in a chandlery. She could not even see the end of it.

There was no repair. That was the truth and she said it out loud to the drum. There was no cloth that would hold it, no tape that would hold it, no wax that would hold it against the working of the hull and the sea. Plastic does not take a seam. You could melt it and fuse it and a sailmaker at sea is not a plastics man and there was nothing aboard to melt anything with except a small spirit stove she did not intend to burn the boat with.

So she retired the drum. She rinsed it and let it dry in the sun and lashed it empty in the forward corner of the lazarette as a monument, and the water she had saved from it she counted twice.

The sum came out at thirty gallons, or a little over. She wrote the number in the ledger and looked at it for a long time and then wrote it again in column seven, which was the column for things that would not go away.

Thirty gallons. At a quart a day she could drink a hundred and twenty days. At three quarts she could drink forty. And she had been drinking three quarts, near enough, with the wash and the cooking and the two cups she took in the night without writing them down, and three quarts a day was not a hundred and twenty days, it was forty, and forty was not the sixty she had carried and not the fifty-one she had left the harbor with and not enough to be comfortable about. The rain catcher needed rain.

The next four days had no rain.

The sky stayed a hard hot blue from the eleventh morning to the fifteenth, and the sun came up each day out of a clean horizon and went down into a clean horizon, and the only clouds were the little fair-weather cumulus that marched across the southeast in the afternoon and dissolved by five, and Nella watched every one of them the way you watch a stranger's face for a sign.

She rigged the catcher aft of the cockpit where the wind came over the quarter and could blow the rain into the canvas hopper, and she left it rigged day and night, because to take it down was to admit something. It was a canvas apron about four feet by six, seized to the backstay and the lee shrouds, with the hose led down to the jerry can under the tiller. In the squall of the eighth day it had filled a can in nine seconds. In fair weather it was a grey triangle of cloth, and nothing more.

She ate dry bread and a quarter tin of fish and drank her quart, and she stopped washing, and she began to notice things she had not noticed before, which is what a thin margin does to a person. She noticed that the boat's fresh water line in the bilge was a lie, because the leak had stopped. She noticed that her own skin smelled of salt and twine. She noticed that she had begun to count the swallows in a cup of water, which is not a thing a person does when they are easy in themselves.

On the twelfth day she shot the sun at local noon and worked the sights and plotted the fix, and the pencil cross came out nine miles north and three east of where her dead reckoning had put her, which was not alarming in itself. Nine miles is a good day's work with a secondhand sextant. But she had been holding a course three degrees north of Reeve's line for four days, and the current she was steering through should have been setting her south, and the fix said north, and the two things did not agree.

She checked her arithmetic twice. Three times. She checked the index error on the sextant, which was two minutes off the arc and she had known that since the harbor. She ran the sum backwards from the three o'clock sun line to the noon line and got the run east and north, and it stood.

The current was not setting her south. It was setting her north, and it was faster than Reeve's chart said it was, and the whole geometry of the thing was wrong by a number she could see with her own eyes.

She got the chart out of its tube and laid it on the cabin sole with the corners held down by a tin and a knife and the logbook. Reeve's neat hand was all over it. The current arrows were drawn in blue ink and labelled in little numbers, seven-tenths of a knot and eight-tenths of a knot, setting west and a point south. She had copied those numbers into her own ledger in the harbor, sitting on the quay, and she had trusted them the way you trust a bridge when you can see the far side.

She put her finger on the pencil line and ran it along the chart, and her finger came to the turn at the Zone and the little words in his hand: hold north.

He had held north. That was the thing. In his book the whole crossing had been a matter of holding north, and the holding had been easy, and the wind had been fair, and the current had stayed where it was written. She had read the passage three times and could nearly recite it. Day fourteen: the wind comes round and holds, and I have made good my northing, and the sea here is as blue as ink.

The sea here was not blue as ink. The sea here was a pale grey-green with long yellow streaks of weed in it, and the current was setting her north, and the wind had come round on the fourteenth day like a story with the right ending in it, which was either a gift or a coincidence, and she had learned in the harbor that you must not mistake one for the other.

She sat on the sole a long while with the chart in her lap. She was not angry. Anger would have been easy and it was not there. What was there was a slow cold thing under the ribs, the feeling you get when you find a seam you sewed yourself has gone, and you look at the needle and know it did exactly what you told it and you told it wrong.

She called the atoll at half past eight.

"Atoll, Drift. Over."

"Drift, this is the atoll. Reading you four by four, a little flat. Everything all right? Over."

She had decided to tell it in order. She told it in order. The cask, the crack run past the waterline, the drum retired, thirty gallons, a quart a day, no rain in four days. Then the light, the fix, the nine miles, the current setting north. Then the chart, and the numbers on the chart, and the little words in his hand.

Junie did not answer at once. Nella had learned to count the pauses. This one ran long, the way a person pauses when they are reading something off paper.

"Drift, I'm going to read you a few things," Junie said at last. "You've got a pencil? Over."

"Got one. Over."

"Wind stress curl, last six days. I've got it from the pilot charts and the tanker reports, and it's coarse, but here it is. The curl has moved north about a degree and a half since you left, and it's moved west with it. The countercurrent south of the Zone has strengthened — that's your northing, that's what's setting you up. The North Equatorial Current on the south side has weakened, so the set on the southern edge of the corridor is south and weak, and the set where you are is north and strong. Over."

"Which means Reeve's arrow. Over."

"Reeve's arrow was right for a summer in 1971. It's a different summer." A pause, short. "I can't give you his current any more than I can give you his wind. The man wrote down what he saw. What he saw is gone. Over."

Nella looked across the cabin at the chart tube under the starboard bunk, and at the pencil crosses on the chart, her own crosses, wandering in a loose chain across a broad blank of paper, and the neat blue arrows underneath them.

"So the route isn't safe. Over."

"The route is a route. It was one man's route one time." Junie's voice came through the hiss with the same steadiness it always had, but the steadiness had a different weight in it now, and Nella could hear the weight. "Here's the thing I don't like, Drift. I've got a ship report from a bulk carrier two days west of you, and they've logged a southwesterly swell with no local wind on it. No wind, and swell out of the southwest. That's a storm somewhere and it's not where it says it should be for the season, and it's not moving the way it says it should either. Over."

"How far. Over."

"Four hundred miles. Give or take." A pause. "I'd like you to think about water and weather at the same time, because that's what you've got. Over."

Nella did. It was not a hard sum. A storm would bring rain, and rain was water, and rain was also a wall of wind out of a direction she could not predict, and she had a mainsail with a patch in it and a boat she had built with her own hands and no engine, and the two things that could kill her were thirst and a sea she could not read.

"I'll think about it out loud," she said. "Over."

"Do that. I'll be here. Over."

She racked the microphone and sat a while in the dark cabin with the set's little dial glowing red at her and the sea running under the hull, and then she went on deck and stood at the shrouds and looked at the sky.

It was clear. Orion stood over the port bow, and Canopus was low and bright in the south, and the Southern Cross hung below it, four stars and a fifth of a kind, tilted like a kite. She had come to know them the way she knew the cabin, and she had begun to use them the way Reeve must have, and she found that she could hold a course by Canopus and the mast truck and never look at the compass once.

Which was the trouble. The compass was nine degrees off when she left the harbor and nineteen degrees off on the eighth day and she had stopped reading it, and now she had a set of stars and a log and a chart, and the chart said one thing and the stars said another, and there was no third voice.

She turned in early and lay in the bunk with her hand on the cloth of the mainsail, which was stowed against the hull beside her head like a friend, and she thought about Reeve, and about what it means to publish a crossing.

The booby came back on the fourteenth morning.

She heard it before she saw it, a hoarse croak away to windward, and then it came in low over the stern, brown and white with a yellow bill and a look of settled disappointment, and it made a circuit of the mast and set itself down on the pulpit rail with its feet turned in and its head down and its eye on her.

"Hello," Nella said.

The booby did not answer. It had answered politely in the harbor and in the first week, by which she meant it had not answered, and she had gone on speaking to it anyway, because a sailmaker alone at sea keeps a column in her log for the living. It had ridden the pulpit rail for two hours on the fifth day and gone. Now it was back, and it sat, and it looked at the water, and once it yawned with its bill wide, and she took that for company.

She went forward and sat down on the coachroof a dozen feet behind it and looked at it for a while. It looked at the sea. There was fish under the boat, a thin scatter of them the color of the water, and every so often the booby would tilt its head and pick one out, and every time it did she felt something click in her own chest, some old gear of noticing that had gone rusty in the years of the loft.

She had spent twenty-six years with a needle in her hand. You can look at a sail for a whole day and not see it, and you can look at it for one second at the right moment and see the one seam that is going. Nella had always been good at the second kind of looking. On the Drift the second kind of looking was no longer a craft. It was the whole of her.

The booby stayed until noon and then went, east, without ceremony, and she watched it go until it was a speck and then a nothing, and then she turned to the ledger and wrote in column seven: Compass 19° and I don't read it. Stars hold. Fix disagrees with chart by 9. Junie: storm 400 west, swell no wind. Water 30. Ration one quart.

She had eaten a quarter of the tin of fish for supper, which left three-quarters, and there was one onion left and half a sack of rice and the tinned beef she did not like, and the sum of it all was that she had water for forty days at the ration she had set and food for longer, and the rain had not come.

She worked the boat that day like a woman with a job.

That was the use she had for a boat. When there was nothing you could do about the water and the chart and the storm, you could do something about the boat, and the boat would answer, and the answering was worth more than the thing done. She went up the mast in the bosun's chair to the spreaders and put a hand on the shroud terminals, and they were tight and bright and salt, and the mast was sound. She greased the gooseneck and the halyard sheaves. She took the slack out of the topping lift. She checked every seizing on every turnbuckle with a knife tip and a thumb, and two of them she cut off and replaced with new line, because a seizing takes the wear and it is a fool who does not spend two feet of line to save a rig.

She looked at the tiller pilot — the wind vane, which is what it really was, a little four-foot vane behind the transom with a trim tab on the rudder, and which had steered the Drift for nine of her fourteen days while Nella slept or sewed or shot the sun. It was simple and it was beautiful and it worked, and she had built it herself in a shop with a sketch she had made on a napkin, and she stood at the transom and looked at it a while and said, out loud, "You're the best thing on this boat."

Then she went below and got out the sail bag and cut a spare patch for the jib, because there was weather coming and a sailmaker who waits for the tear is a sailmaker who is late.

On the fifteenth morning the barometer fell two millibars in four hours.

It was a small thing, two millibars, the kind of fall you see on any ordinary day, but she had been logging it every four hours for two weeks and she knew how it behaved on this ocean. It had sat at 1012 for seven days and 1011 for four more and had moved at a crawl, and now it was 1009 and falling and the sky at six in the morning was the color of a bruise.

The wind was still northeast at twelve knots. The sea was the same long grey ridges it had been for a week. If you had come on deck fresh from the harbor that morning you would have said it was a fine day, and Nella stood in the companionway with the mug in her hand and watched the east and did not believe it.

She went below and called the atoll early.

"Atoll, Drift. Over."

"Drift, this is the atoll. Go ahead. Over."

"Barometer 1009, falling, two millibars in four hours. Wind still northeast twelve. Sea still long. Sky east is wrong."

"Copy that." A pause and the sound of a pencil. "The carrier is still reporting from the southwest and the swell's gone up — they log three meters now, long period. That's a storm making its run. Drift, I'm going to say the uncomfortable thing. It's not tracking with the season. It's making northwest at fifteen knots, three hundred and fifty miles from you, and it may not pass north of you the way these things usually do. It's coming across your line. Over."

"How long. Over."

"Twenty-four hours, maybe thirty, if it keeps its speed."

Nella looked out the hatch at the blue-grey morning and at the sea that had not one thing wrong with it and the sky that had.

"Then I'll spend today," she said. "Over."

She spent it.

She put a double lashing on the cracked cask, and on the two full ones. She rigged the storm jib and left it hanked on the inner forestay ready to set. She took the mainsail down and unshackled it and stowed it in the deep locker with the patch facing up, because if she had to drop it in a blow she wanted it folded so the seam ran along the boom instead of across it. She filled the cockpit lockers with the things that would float out of them and took the lid off the watertight bucket and packed it with a first-aid tin and two flares and a hand bearing compass and the spare set, wrapped in oilcloth, and lashed it to the mast.

She cleaned the cabin. That was not for the storm; that was for herself. She took everything loose off every shelf and stowed it in the low lockers and set the floorboards back down and put a third lashing on the toolbox, and then she sat on the sole in the tidy cabin with her knees up and looked at the chart tube under the starboard bunk, and she took the chart out one last time and laid it on her knees.

Reeve's line crossed the paper west-southwest and turned northwest at the Zone and ran west-northwest to the atoll. His little numbers. His little words. Light air here. Squalls at the Zone. Hold north.

She had trusted that line the way you trust the bank that holds your money, and the line had been drawn by a man alone on the sea with a sextant and a wristwatch forty years before she was born, and the man had been telling the truth as far as he could see it, and the truth he could see was not the truth she was sailing through. The corridor had moved. The current had moved. The storm was not in its season. The chart was a good seam in a sail that had gone around her, a seam she had sewn her own crossing to without ever once putting her hand on the cloth underneath and feeling how thin it had got.

She had carried the chart two thousand miles. She did not tear it up. She rolled it and put it back in the tube and put the tube back under the bunk, because a sailmaker does not throw away a chart she may need again, and because the man who drew it had crossed an ocean once and earned the right to be wrong slowly.

But she took a fresh sheet from the log and ruled it with her own pencil and her own fingers. She drew the atoll, where Junie had told her it was, and she drew the Drift, and she marked her noon fix of the twelfth day and the sun line of the fourteenth and the run she had made good over four days, and she plotted the courses she could sail and the waters she could do it in, and under it all she wrote, in her own hand, the one thing that would keep her alive: Stars first. Current where I find it. Rain when it comes. Never again into a corridor without looking at the cloth.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the sheet. The line she had drawn was hers, from her own fixes and her own steering, and it ran north of his, and it ran where the water she had felt with her own keel was running, and there was no printed arrow on it anywhere. It was not much to look at. It was a ruled sheet with a pencil line on it, and it was enough.

The light went on the fifteenth day the way it goes on a day before weather, too bright and too quiet, the sea like glass to the horizon and the horizon like a wall, and she went on deck with her sheet in her hand and stood at the shrouds in the hush and looked east with the booby's eye, the eye that notices.

The swell was already there. It came out of the southwest, long and low and slow, and it lifted the Drift and lowered her and lifted her and it had no white on it anywhere, and there was not one breath of wind to make it. And over the bow, low on the eastern rim, the sky was turning over like a page.
