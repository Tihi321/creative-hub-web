Leaves, Not Roots

The tea was not the medicine she had come for.

Naledi had known that before she brewed the first pot, and she went on knowing it through the second and the third, and she wrote it in the clinic book in her own hand every morning so that no one, later, could say she had been confused about what she was doing. The root carried the compound at full strength. The leaf carried it at a quarter, and a quarter was a thing you could dose, measure, and fail with.

She had set the dose with Toma over two evenings at the stove, the two of them leaning on the clinic table with the brass scale between them and the leaves spread on a sheet of waxed paper, sorting the good from the rain-marked, the broad ones from the narrow. Twenty leaves to the litre was too strong; it made the smallest children vomit and brought Emeka's brother out in a sweat that had nothing to do with the fever. Fifteen was where they stopped. Fifteen leaves, steeped covered, not boiled, for the length of time it took to say twenty Our Fathers slowly, which was how Toma measured it, and which Naledi recorded as eight minutes, twice tested, once with Ruth counting.

"You are writing down that a prayer is eight minutes," Toma said.

"I am writing down that eight minutes is what the prayer comes to when you say it slow."

"That is a different sentence."

"It is the same sentence with the working shown."

He looked at the page for a while. Then he took the pencil from her, which he had never done before, and drew a small mark beside the number — a leaf with its stem, which was the old mark for the plant — and handed the pencil back.

"Now it is a sentence a person can use when you are not standing beside the pot," he said.

Essa was the measure of it, because Essa was the worst.

Naledi took the first night, and Toma took the second, and Nuru Banda took the hours between, and none of them slept properly through either. The fever had been at forty for four days and had begun to do the thing that frightened Naledi most about fevers in children: it had stopped being a number and become a country, and the child had moved into it. Essa talked to the ceiling about moths. She asked the wall where the water had gone. She held Naledi's sleeve through one long stretch of the dark without knowing whose sleeve it was, and let go only when Nuru came with the sponge and the enamel basin and made her let go.

Naledi dosed her at four in the afternoon and again at ten, the way they had set it, and wrote the temperature at the same times every time, and did not read anything into a single number. That was the discipline of it. A half degree was not a cure; a half degree was a fact and you wrote it down and waited for the next one. She had spent a career learning to distrust the single reading, and never more than now, when she wanted one of them to mean something.

On the first night, Essa's temperature went from forty point one to thirty-nine point seven and stayed there, and Naledi sat with her back against the blackboard and her notebook open on her knee and thought about the western edge of the grove, yellowing where she had lifted the root, and then remembered that Renn's men had gone through the gate and were not coming back this season, and still could not make her shoulders drop.

On the second night, it went from thirty-nine point seven to thirty-eight point six by midnight, and Essa surfaced, briefly, the way a swimmer surfaces.

"Doctor."

"Yes."

"Your hands are cold."

"They are."

"That is good," Essa said, in the tone of one delivering a considered medical opinion, and went under again.

Naledi wrote thirty-eight point six. She wrote the time. She wrote, in the margin, in letters she intended to keep and show to Nuru in the morning: five children on the tea, three falling, one flat, one rising. She did not write the word she wanted to write.

On the third morning at six, Nuru took the temperature and read it out in her flat nurse's voice from the far end of the schoolroom, so that everyone else in the room could hear it too, which was her way.

"Thirty-seven point five. Rash is going off the chest and coming nowhere else." And then, breaking her own rule and putting the number in a sentence: "She is out of it."

Essa was awake. She was propped against Nuru's rolled coat with the blanket to her chin, and she was looking at the row of pallets down the long floor, at the other children, one by one, doing what she did when she was well, which was count.

"How many are still sick," she said.

Naledi counted for her, the way she would count a transect. "Nine out of seventeen on the tea have fallen below thirty-eight. Four are flat. Two are rising slowly. Two were never bad enough to matter. Everyone who was bad has come down from where they were. Nobody has gone up."

Essa considered this with the flat seriousness of a person verifying a figure they intend to use later.

"Write that down," she said. "All of it. With the times. I want to see it."

"You are twelve."

"I am nearly thirteen and I am the only one here who can spell temperature."

The rest of the children followed, and the following was not quick and was not even, and Naledi refused from the first to call it a cure. Two children held at thirty-nine for a whole day and made Nuru walk to the end of the room and back twice before she would say anything. One, a small girl called Adanna, went up again on the fourth morning, and Naledi sat with the book open under the lamp and went back through every dose she had written, and found nothing wrong with the dose, and had to say so out loud to Nuru so that they would both have heard it.

"It is not the tea. The tea is doing what it does. She was the weakest of them when she came in and the fever has a longer shape in her."

"Then what do we do."

"We wait, and we keep the water going, and we give her the tea and not more of it."

She was wrong about Adanna, as it turned out — wrong in the way that matters, because the child's fever did break on the sixth night, and Naledi had spent two days bracing the ward for the other outcome and had to be told to sit down when it came. It was the boy with the hoe who told her, coming straight off the lane at midnight with his coat over his head, standing in the doorway and saying it in the flat way the villagers said everything important, as if the sentence were about somebody else.

"Adanna is sweating and the nurse says the number is down and Nuru said to come and tell you and I came the short way and I did."

Naledi stood in the doorway for a moment in the wet dark, looking past him up the lane toward the schoolroom, and then she understood what he had done, which was to run the whole distance in the rain, not for his own family, not for any of the children who were his, because none of them were, and had come to find the person whose job it was to write it down.

"Go and sit by the stove," she said. "There is tea in the pot and it is not the medicine, it is the ordinary kind, and you will drink it and then you will sleep on the mat by the door and go home in the morning."

He went. She stood in the doorway a while longer and listened to the water going down the channel she had opened with Toma, which had a lower note in it now that the lake was falling, and then she went in and wrote the number in the book, and under it, because it was the third morning in a row that she had written a number like this one, she wrote: no child in this valley has died since Chidi.

The village did not celebrate, because the village was wet through and tired to the bone and had buried a child eleven weeks before, and the whole of it understood that a fever that lifts can come back. What they did was quieter than that and harder to describe, and Naledi did not have a word for it until much later.

They came to the schoolroom in the evenings. That was all. It began on the fourth night after Essa's fever broke, when Baro turned up at the stove with a pot of his own, which was unheard of, and said he had brought it because the schoolroom had ten children in it and Nuru could not cook for ten and run a clinic at once. The next night it was Ruth Achieng with yams, and the night after that Amara Nkemdirim with a pot of groundnut stew that she put on the schoolroom stove and told everyone to eat while she went round the pallets with a spoon, and after that it was simply what happened, every evening, in the wet dark, at the end of the world.

Half the household heads who had signed Renn's contract came, and half did not, and the ones who came sat with the ones who had voted no, because there was nowhere else to sit. Toma sat at the stove end with his back to the fire, and Naledi sat where the light was good over the book, and the villagers brought food to the door and stayed and ate it and went home wet, and the thanks at the end of it, when it came, were given to both of them at once and to neither of them alone.

Baro said it, on the sixth evening, standing at the door with his coat on and his lamp in his hand, looking back down the long room at the children.

"To the healer," he said, "and to the botanist. The two of you brewed the same pot."

Toma did not answer him. Toma was the man who answered a question with a question and left a thank-you sitting where it was, and he came over to where Naledi was sitting and put his hand flat on the page of the clinic book, on the leaf mark he had drawn beside the eight minutes, and left it there a moment, and took it away, and said the only thing he had said about it in eleven weeks.

"You asked the leaf before the root," he said. "And the small ones. So now the leaf is the medicine and the root is still in the ground, and the root will be in the ground when I am not, and that is a better sentence than mine." He turned and went out into the rain before she could answer, which was also his way, and she sat with the book and the leaf mark and did not try.

Renn's claim died in a machine somewhere north of the river, and it died quietly, and Naledi did not hear about it for a fortnight.

What she heard first was that his porters had come out through the canyon and gone down the valley, and then that the town at the road-end had seen one man in a wet coat and one man on a stretcher being loaded onto the back of the one truck that could reach that far, and then, in a letter that came up with the truck man on his next run with the salt and the fuel, that the company had closed its field ledger for the season. The letter was from a clerk she had never met, at a laboratory she had never seen, and it was short and written in the flat paper voice that Naledi recognised because she had once been inside it.

We are advised that no living material and no usable samples reached the laboratory from the current season's work. In the absence of material the company cannot proceed to the trial phase and has closed the relevant field account. The matter of any existing agreement with the village is for the company's legal office and we are not able to advise on it. We hold no further instruction.

She read it twice at the stove, and then once more a day later, to see whether it said anything different in daylight. It did not. She gave it to Baro to read aloud at the schoolroom, which he did, standing, in his good coat, to the thirteen household heads and the ones who had not been coming, in the flat voice he used for figures, having read it through to himself first. When he finished there was a silence in which nobody said the obvious thing, which was that a paper can be signed in one room and cancelled in another, and that the signature — fifteen of them, in the book by the shop, all in ink — had bought the village nothing at all except a wet season of trouble and a drowned road. Baro folded the letter and put it in his breast pocket.

"There is no material," he said. "There is no trial. There is a grove on the slope with the moths in it, and there are seventeen children in this room who are going to live, and the man who offered us the paper is somewhere down the river with one boot." He looked round at them all, and then at Toma, and then at Naledi at the end of the bench, and said the last thing slowly, as if it were arithmetic, because it was. "I will not ask anyone to say they were wrong. We were all of us doing the best arithmetic we had. But this is a village with no road and one clinic and now a plant that grows back, and I think from tonight we should put the paper in the box with the other papers and get on."

Nobody argued. There was nobody left in the room who had argued for eleven weeks.

The letter from the Board of Field Credentials came on the truck the same day, and Naledi put it on the shelf above the clinic table and did not open it for three days.

She had known what was in it. She had written to them herself, in a flat hand, days before the fire, asking for an emergency survey order under the 1972 survey, and she had paid the truck man to carry it to the town-hall post and had watched him go down the riverbed in the first rain, and had understood before he was out of sight that a letter from a woman whose credentials were suspended, addressed to the board that had suspended her, was a letter that was going nowhere useful. She had written it anyway, the way you set a broken bone even when you know the child will run on it again next week. The letter that came back had the board's crest on the corner, and the paper was thick, and there was more than one sheet of it.

She did not open it on the first day because the schoolroom needed shifting and the ward had to come out of the corner by the blackboard and the pallets had to go back to the houses they came from, and by evening she was too tired to hold a knife.

She did not open it on the second day because the truck man came up with the salt and the letter about the company and the question of what to do with the four households who had voted no and shut their doors, which had become a question about whether a village can be whole again if it needs every pair of hands it has — and the answer the village found, on the second evening, was that it is easier to share a stove than to keep a grudge, and the last of the four doors came open over a pot of Nuru's tea and a discussion about the yam harvest.

She did not open it on the third day because Toma asked her to walk the contact line with him to count the moths, which they had begun to do every few days since the fire, and they counted them along the old wall and the north lane and the western edge where the litter had burned, and the count was up on the last time they had done it, and Toma did not say anything about it, and she did not either, and the two of them walked down at dusk in the mist with the copper smell in it and did not need to say the number out loud to agree about what it meant.

On the fourth morning she took the letter off the shelf and sat down at the clinic table with the knife and opened it cleanly along the fold.

She read it once, sitting, and then she stood up and read it again standing in the doorway, in the grey light with the rain coming off the roof in a steady line. The panel had reviewed her case in the light of the emergency survey request and the field reports forwarded by the truck man. It noted that her work in the Iron Basin had identified a therapeutic compound, preserved a unique symbiotic system, and mitigated a public health crisis in a recognised community. It voted six-to-one to restore her field credentials with full collecting privileges, effective immediately, and to append a commendation. It invited her to return for a formal hearing at the start of the next academic term.

She folded it and put it in her coat pocket and walked down the lane to the schoolroom, where a girl with a record book had been left in charge of a word she was not to write down yet.

Essa was sitting up with the blanket round her shoulders and the book on her knees, copying the fever numbers out of Nuru's clinic book into her own, in her careful separate letters, with the times against them, because she had said she wanted to see it and now she was seeing it.

"Doctor," she said, without looking up. "Are you going to answer them?"

Naledi took the letter out of her pocket and looked at it. It gave her back what had been taken. It did not mention the years of silence, the form, the locked door, the corridor where two former colleagues still crossed to avoid her. It did not mention the valley, or the pot, or the leaf mark beside the eight minutes.

"Not today," she said. "Write your numbers."

Essa wrote her numbers. Naledi sat down at the end of the long floor with her back to the stove and read the letter one more time in the firelight, and then folded it and put it away and left it in her pocket, where it stayed through the yam harvest and the last of the rain, a paper about a door, which could wait, because outside the door there was a slope, and on the slope the western edge was mending, and the moths were on it again, and the root was in the ground, and all of that would still be there whether a board in a city three days' travel away put its name to it or not.
