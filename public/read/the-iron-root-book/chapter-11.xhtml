The Diversion

The rain came back before dawn, the way it had come back every morning for three weeks, drumming on the schoolroom roof and running in the lane. Naledi had learned to hear the difference between rain that would pass and rain that would stay. This one stayed. By the time the council gathered at the wall of the shop, the lake had swallowed the bottom two steps.

Baro came last, as he always did, wearing a coat over his sleeping shirt and carrying a hurricane lamp that smoked. Fifteen household heads stood behind him. Four of them had voted against the contract. Every one of them had a child on the schoolroom floor.

“Doctor,” Baro said. “Show us.”

She did not make a speech. She took them along the western shore, past the place where the footpath went under, to the mouth of the old channel. The stones of the wall stood four courses high and black with wet, and the western water lay against them, three metres wide, running fast and brown and full of leaf litter from the burnt slope above.

Naledi put her hand flat on the water and showed them the level against her wrist.

“Yesterday this was at my knuckles,” she said. “At dark it was at my fingers. By tomorrow night it will be over the wall and into the grove, and the grove is on the only high ground the root mat has got. The mat can take a wet season. It has taken fifty of them. It cannot take a wet season with the litter gone from above it and the west fork pushing full. If it floods, the mat rots from underneath and the moths go. That is the whole sentence.”

She turned and pointed down the shore, to the low ground where the water ran out toward the break in the ridge.

“That water has to go somewhere. There is a channel under our feet that used to take it. Four hundred metres, four metres of fall, built by people who laid these stones by hand. It is choked with brush and gravel and two generations of rubbish. If you clear it, the western water goes down it, past the grove, and out onto the flat ground below.”

Silence, except for the water.

“And the flat ground is Renn’s camp,” Baro said.

“Yes.”

“The camp where he sleeps. The camp with his nine porters and his samples and his road.”

“Yes.”

Baro looked at the stones for a long time. The lamp shook in his hand and he set it down on the wall.

“I signed,” he said. “Fifteen of us signed. I read it aloud twice and I signed it. The paper says the company has rights to the material in this basin, and the material in this basin is what that water is about to take, and if we send it there we are taking it. That is not mud. That is a hundred thousand of something and a man’s name on it.” His voice went loud, then flat. “I am not going to stand in front of this village and say I destroyed what we sold.”

“You did not sell the water,” Naledi said.

“I sold everything in the paper. The paper is broad.”

Toma had been standing back with the hoe over his shoulder. He had not said a word since the shop, and Naledi had stopped expecting him to. He stepped forward now into the lamplight, and he spoke to Baro and not to the others.

“Old Baro. When I was fourteen I was there for the four roots my grandfather’s generation pulled out of that mat, and the last of them was in my own two hands, and I have carried it fifty years, and I will not carry it another month. So hear this from a man who did the wrong thing once. The valley keeps those who keep it.”

“That is a proverb,” Baro said. “I have four grandchildren.”

“It is not a proverb. It is arithmetic. The grove kept us the leaf. The leaf has kept your two and seventeen besides. You want a number — the number is seventeen children on leaves from a plant that floods tonight if you do nothing. The paper does not know where the water goes. The paper has never been here in a wet season. You have.”

Baro looked at the mud, at the water, at the stones he had known all his life.

“If I say clear it,” he said, “whose house does the water come into?”

“Nobody’s,” Naledi said. “That is the point. It goes down the old cut and out onto the low ground. The channel was built so that the water and the village never met. We are not making a new thing. We are putting back a thing that was taken.”

He stood with his hands on the wall, and then he turned and went out into the rain, and they heard him begin to call the households by their full names, one after another, down the lane and up the lane: Ruth Achieng! Daniel Osei and your two boys! Amara Nkemdirim! First light, at the old wall, with hoes!

Thirty came, with hoes and crowbars and two machetes and a handsaw and every bucket in the village. Four of them were the ones who had voted no and shut their doors, and they came without being called in the end. The boy with the hoe was there, and Ruth, and Amara, and Emeka’s father, who had not been seen at a meeting since Chidi was buried. Baro counted them aloud and stopped counting.

The rain did not stop. It came down straight and grey out of a low sky, and the lake stood at the bottom step of the shop and did not move, which was its own kind of clock.

Naledi put them where the work would keep them alive. Two on the bank with ropes around their waists and the others on the flat with the hoes, cutting the brush out of the old cut from the top down, so the water could not back up behind a jam. She kept Toma close, because the channel turned twice under the standing rock and a man working blind at the bottom of a cut in rising water is a man who drowns looking for the way out.

They worked from before first light. She could not see the channel. She had walked it twice in two days and drawn it once in her notebook, and now she stood on the bank with the notebook open under her coat and directed by sound.

“Ruth, the water is turning left at your feet. Feel it? Left, and then it drops. Take the gravel out there and it will find it.”

“The gravel is two feet deep!”

“Then take a foot and let it do the rest.”

There was a way to hear it: the note of the stream changed as the cut opened. A blocked channel whispered and slapped and came back on itself. An open one ran with a lower sound, a long note, like a bow drawn slowly.

“They are not fighting the water,” Naledi said to Toma. “They are letting it tell them where to dig.”

Toma said nothing. He was watching the western fork, which had risen another hand.

They cut for six hours. The rain went harder at midday and the lake came up the lane as far as the schoolroom steps. Nuru came down twice with tea in a pot and took away the two men whose hands had split, and brought them back with cloth wrapped around them. The boy with the hoe cut a channel thirty centimetres wide on his own and had to be pulled out of it when it filled.

By the fourth hour the water in the top of the cut had begun to move. By the fifth it was moving down the channel with a voice, a real one, and Naledi stood up straight for the first time and felt the sound of it in her chest.

“It is open,” she said. “Not all of it. The bottom third is still holding. But it is open enough.”

Baro came up the bank, slipping, and stood beside her, and they both listened.

“That is the south end,” he said. “That is the sound of the south end.”

“It is.”

“It will be at the camp in twenty minutes.”

“Yes.”

He was quiet. Then he said, “I will be the one who tells him. I said I would stand in the door and tell him, and I will.” And he went down the bank and called four men to come with him, and went, and did not look back.

Ruth and Amara and the others kept working the last gravel out of the bottom of the cut because the water was asking the channel to open wider and Naledi was not going to argue with it. She stood with the notebook closed in her hand and listened to the note get longer.

Then the western branch shifted. She heard it happen, the way you hear a rope go slack, and the brown water that had been leaning against the old stones turned and leaned the other way, and went down.

It did not roar. That was the thing she would remember. After the two-metre flood in the upper channel she had expected a roar. This was a long low pull, a hundred small sounds of water finding a way it had not used in fifty years, and the whole surface of the west fork dropping, and the old stones of the wall standing clear for the first time in three weeks. Leaves and sticks and one flopping silver fish went past her feet and into the cut and away.

She put her hand on the wall and felt it hum.

They did not go down to watch. That was Toma’s word and it held. Nobody followed Baro’s four men, and nobody went along the ridge to look at the flat ground, because there is no way to watch a thing like that from a bank and stay a person you want to be. So they waited in the downpour at the old wall and listened to what the water did for twenty minutes, and they heard it arrive. They heard a shout, and then several, and then the sound of many things at once that were not people — the tearing of a tarpaulin, a stack of boxes going over, wood and water and a road going out in one long wet rush.

Then the noise went away down the valley, and the rain kept coming, and the west fork ran clean and low in its bed past the old wall.

Baro came back alone at dark. He was wet through and there was mud on him to the waist and he had a hand-shaped bruise on his arm that he would not explain until the following week. He stood in front of the council wall with his coat dripping and the thirteen remaining household heads standing in the rain in front of him, and he gave his report in the flat voice he used for figures.

“Camp is gone. Tents flat, boxes burst, the samples in the water. The road is a ditch from the bend down to the riverbed. Three of the porters are up on the shelf and Renn is with them. Nobody is hurt bad. Renn has his foot on a stake and his coat is in the river.”

“Did he speak to you?” Naledi said.

“He asked me what the water was. I told him it was the same water that was always going to come. He asked me whether the village had opened the channel. I said yes. He asked me whether I understood the paper. I said I understood the paper and the paper did not know where the water goes.” Baro wiped his face with the flat of his hand. “Then he sat down on a rock and did not say anything else, and I came back.”

The porters came up through the canyon and out at first light, three and then four at a time, cold and quiet, carrying what they could carry in their two hands. They went past the shop without stopping. By mid-morning nine of them had gone through the gate and were somewhere down the riverbed on the way to the town, and there was nothing in the village that could have stopped them and nothing in the village that tried.

Renn came last. He walked slowly with a stick, coatless, his one good boot and one wet sock, and the foreman with the broken foot came behind him on a stretcher carried by the two men who had stayed. He stopped at the wall by the shop in the rain, with the lake up to the second step behind him, and he looked up the slope at the grove, which they could not see for mist but which every one of them could have pointed to.

Baro stood in the door of the shop. He had put on his good coat for it.

Renn said, “I have signed copies.”

“You have four.”

“Three are in the river. One is in my bag.” He held it up in two fingers. “The material it describes is still standing up there. I can be back with a court order by the middle of the dry season, or I can be back with a different paper and a longer conversation. I would rather the second.”

Baro said, “The village will hear any paper you bring, out loud, in the schoolroom, with witnesses, the same as last time.”

“That is more than I expected,” Renn said.

He stood a moment longer. Naledi was standing at the end of the wall with her notebook under her coat. He turned and looked at her, and for the first time in all of it he said something that was not a piece of paper.

“Dr Okonjo. Your compound is in the leaves. I read your evidence and I did not dispute it. Did you check the leaf for the second compound, or only the first?”

“Only the first so far.”

“You will,” he said. “It is in there. It will be a better paper than mine.” And then he turned and went, slow, after the stretcher and the men, into the mist at the canyon mouth, and was gone.

The expedition went through the gate before noon. Toma walked down to the waterline at the foot of the lane and stood for a while and watched the last two men go, and then he came back up and sat on the wall of the shop with his feet in the water, which nobody had seen him do before.

Naledi went up to the schoolroom.

The fire was banked and the floor was dry and the whole long room smelled of steam, wet wool and the sharp green of the tea in the big pot on the stove. Naledi had made it herself that morning, fifteen leaves to the litre, the strength she and Toma had settled on with the notebook: no more, because more made the smallest ones sick, and no less, because less did not move the temperature.

At the far end, in the corner by the blackboard, Essa was sitting up.

Not far up. Propped on Nuru’s rolled coat with a blanket round her shoulders, and her hair flat with sweat and her face still grey and thin, and her hands in her lap doing nothing. But sitting, and looking at the door, and when she saw Naledi she lifted her chin the way she did when she had a question ready.

Nuru met Naledi at the stove and spoke in a low voice with the number in it first, the way she always did.

“Thirty-seven point nine at six this morning. Thirty-seven point four an hour ago. The rash is fading off her chest. She asked for water twice and drank it and kept it, and she did not know your name at dark last night and she knew it at midnight.”

Naledi stood looking down the long room at the child, and the room did not spin and the floor did not move, and she would remember that afterwards: that it arrived as a fact and nothing more, the way the leaf count arrives, or the flood line.

Essa said, “Doctor.”

“Yes.”

“Papa is sitting in the water.”

“He is.”

“Why is Papa sitting in the water?”

“Because he can,” Naledi said.

Essa thought about that. Her eyes went to the window, where the rain ran down the glass in sheets, and then to the door, and then back to Naledi, and the question she had been holding ready came out quite steady.

“Did we win?”

Naledi sat down on the floor beside her, because her legs had opinions of their own by then, and pulled the blanket straight over the child’s feet.

“There was no win,” she said. “The grove is standing and you are sitting up and the company has gone home. That is three facts, and none of them cancels the others. Tomorrow you will write them down for me, and the day after that you will write them down for Nuru, and then we will start on the plot.”

Essa frowned at her. “That is not how Papa answers.”

“No.”

“Papa answers shorter.” She took a slow breath and let it out, and laid her head back on the coat, and closed her eyes, and said, from somewhere on the way down into sleep, in the tone of a person correcting a record, “Write ‘did not win’. Write ‘standing’.”

Outside, the rain kept on. The lake stood at the second step and did not rise. Up the slope, past the mist and the burnt western litter and the old wall with its clean-cut water running through, the mat lay under the host trees with the moths in it, keeping, as it had kept before there was a village to need it. It would need tending. It would need counting and watching and a hundred days of somebody’s patience. But the accounting, for tonight, balanced: grove, child, and the water in the wrong man’s road.
