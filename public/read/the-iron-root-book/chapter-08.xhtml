A Price in Paper

They went up before first light, and it was Toma who carried the crowbar and Naledi who carried the lamp, and they were on the old stone channel above the grove by the time the first grey came into the mist.

The channel had been cut by hand, long ago, along the contact where the limestone met the clay. It ran from the western shoulder of the grove down toward the lower valley, and its whole length had been built to do one thing: take the water that came off the high seep and carry it away from the trees. Someone had known that. Someone had dug it with iron tools and kept it clear for a hundred years, and then the families had gone and the brush had come and the channel had filled with leaf litter and stone and the roots of everything that grows in wet ground.

Naledi put the lamp on a shelf of rock and they worked.

It did not take long to learn the channel's temper. The lower end was shallow and wide and easy; they cleared it in an hour, Toma with the crowbar levering out a jammed slab, Naledi dragging brush downhill with both hands. The upper end was the trouble. There the channel was narrow, and the clay had slid into it, and every bar of stone they prised loose brought a run of grey mud after it, so that the work went the way all such work goes, two shovels of mud for one shovel of channel.

At the top the water stood against the blockage in a quiet brown wall, thirty centimetres deep and holding, the way water holds when it has been holding for years. Naledi put her hand in it and felt the cold of it and the direction of it, which was not the direction of the grove.

'When this opens,' she said, 'it goes west. Away from the root mat. Down onto the low ground.'

Toma leaned on the crowbar and looked at the brown water and then at the trees below, whose lower branches were still in the mist.

'It will find the lowest ground,' he said. 'It always does. That is the whole of what I know about water and it has never once been wrong.'

They had the channel open by mid-morning, not wide, but open end to end, a working cut with a fall on it, and they stood at the top and watched the water take the first metre of it and stop, because there was no flood yet to push it through. It would sit there until the rain came. That was the trade. They walked up to the grove and checked the west edge, where the mat ran thin toward the rock, and Naledi counted the yellowed patch she had made and found it no larger than it had been three days before, and the moths were back on the trunk of the old westward tree, folded flat and pale as bark. She stood and looked at that for longer than she meant to.

'It is mending,' Toma said.

'It is not dying, which is not the same as mending.' She wrote the count in the notebook anyway, and the time, and the condition of the western edge, because a number written down twice is worth more than a number remembered once.

They came down to the village at noon, and the schoolroom door was open, and there were papers on the teacher's table and men standing around it the way men stand around a thing that has been put in front of them and is waiting.

Renn had been busy while they were on the channel.

He had done it properly. There were four copies of the contract, one for the council, one for the company, one for the shop, where anyone could read it, and one that he had written out in a fair round hand and left on the clinic table for Nuru Banda, which Naledi found a kindness and distrusted for exactly that reason. He had set up the schoolroom as a place of business: the teacher's table at the front, benches in two rows, the permit and the map pinned to the wall behind him on the school's own notice board, and the school's map of the world, which showed the world in eight flat colours and had the Iron Basin marked on it as nothing at all.

He did not stand at the front when the council came in. He sat at the end of the front bench with his hat on his knee, and he let Baro take the teacher's chair, and he answered every question from the bench like a man who had been called in to advise and not to rule. That was the thing about him, Naledi thought, watching from the back with Toma beside her. He did not want the room. He wanted the paper, and the paper did not need the room at all.

Baro opened the meeting the way he opened every meeting, by reading out what had been decided and what had not, and then he held up the contract and read the parts that mattered out loud, slowly, moving his finger under the words. He read them twice.

The company would hold the rights to all Iron Root material in the Iron Basin, root, leaf, stem, seed and fibre, and to any compound derived from it. The company would pay the village a share of the net revenue from any medicine produced from that material, two and a half parts in a hundred, in perpetuity. The company would fund a clinic nurse's post for five years. The company would build a foot track from the canyon mouth to the village, wide enough for a hand-cart, maintained at company cost. The company's men would take no material from the basin without the council's written consent, visit by visit. The village would not sell Iron Root material to any other party for the life of the agreement.

'That is the whole of it,' Baro said. 'Read it yourself if you want. Nuru has read it and says it is what it says. Doctor Okonjo, the council has given you the floor before it votes, because that is how we do things here, and because half this room owes you a debt already, and I am one of them.' He said that last part flatly and without looking at her. 'Say what you have to say.'

Naledi stood up.

She had thought about this on the walk down, and she had decided not to argue the paper. You cannot argue a paper. You can only put a fact beside it and let the room see both.

'I am not going to tell you what the contract says,' she said. 'Baro has read it and Nuru has read it. I am going to tell you what happens to a wild plant when you move it, because that is the plan on the table — a hundred leaves now, and living material later, and a site, and a road. Renn's men have not said it out loud in this room, but the second visit is to take living material. Nothing else makes commercial sense. A company does not build a road to a slope to pick leaves off it. Ask him. I will wait.'

Nobody moved. Renn looked at her with the pleasant interest of a man who has been handed a point in his own favour and is deciding whether to accept it.

'She is right,' he said mildly. 'The second phase of the plan is living material. I would have said so within the hour. It is not a secret, Dr Okonjo, it is a sequence, and a sequence is not a plot.'

'Then hear the sequence,' Naledi said.

She had the notebook out, but she did not read from it. She had learned that much in the valley. Numbers spoken from a book are a woman's hobby; numbers spoken from the ground are a claim.

'I have moved plants all my working life. Here is what that means, and it means the same thing for a seedling, a shrub or a mat like the one on that slope. First: the plant has a root system sized to the place it grew up in. It has spent its whole life reaching for water exactly where the water is, and it has spent nothing on reaching anywhere else. When you lift it, you cut the small roots that do the drinking. The big root comes up with the plant and looks like the plant, and looks like a plant can be moved, and it is a lie. The small roots are the part that drinks and you cannot see them, and you have cut them by lifting, and you have cut them whether you meant to or not, and the plant is now standing in new ground with a mouth and no hands.

'Second: two-thirds of what you take out of the ground does not survive the first season, and most of what dies, dies in the first three weeks, and it dies of thirst while standing in wet soil, because it cannot drink. That is what the trade calls transplant shock. It is not a disease. It is a plant with nothing to drink and no way to say so. Some species take it and some do not. You do not find out which kind you have until you have killed the first several hundred of them trying, and I do not have several hundred of that slope to spend.

'Third: even the ones that live are not finished. A plant raised under the trees on that slope has never seen open sky in its whole life. The mist, the shade of the host trees, the wet air off the seep, that is not scenery, that is a room, and a plant built for that room will burn if you carry it out into the sun in one move. So you harden it off. You shade it, you lift the shade an hour a week, you water it twice a day in the first month and once a day in the second and let it go thirsty by a day in the third, and you watch it the way Nuru watches a child, and you do that for a season, and at the end of the season you find out whether you have a plant or a stick. The whole sequence, from lifting to a plant that can stand on its own, is eight months at the shortest, and for a mat like that one it may be longer than a man's patience. So when the paper in Baro's hand says material, the room should understand what material means. It means lifting living root out of that slope, and lifting living root there kills the mat at the cut, and this time it does not stop at the edge, because it will not be four seconds, it will be a shovel.

'That is my evidence. It is not a paper with a red drop on it. It is a plant, and a man cannot stand on the bank and look at it with his own eyes the way he can look at a ditch, and I will not pretend the arithmetic is different from what it is.'

She sat down. The room was quiet, and the quiet went on for a while, and then Renn spoke, and he spoke as smoothly as ever, and this time he did not bother pretending that he was on the bench and not running the meeting.

'Everything she says is true,' he said. 'I want to start with that. Transplant shock, root disturbance, hardening off — I have a nursery in the town with eight hectares of glass in it and a man who does nothing but harden off wild material from one wet season to the next, and he would tell you the same numbers to the week. Dr Okonjo is a good botanist and she is telling you the truth.

'And then I have to tell you the other truth, which is that none of it applies to what I actually need from this valley.

'I do not need a nursery of this plant. I do not need it to live in the town, or in a field, or anywhere else. I need enough living material to carry a compound legally and to prove where it came from. That is a patent. That is a legal instrument and a laboratory instrument, and it needs material the way a courtroom needs a witness, not the way a farm needs a crop. A living stem, held in the cold, is enough. Two kilos of clean root, cut, packed and carried down in a cold box, will keep the compound stable and identifiable for months, and it will not need to grow in the box and it will not need to be hardened off in the box and it does not have to survive six weeks in the wet for my purposes, only that one, and only long enough to be tested and registered.

'Now — Dr Okonjo will tell you, and she is right, that taking root kills the mat at the cut. She saw it in her own hands and she is not lying. But the cut does not kill the stand and the stand does not vanish overnight. The western host tree and the mat under it, the eastern edge where it is already yellow — the material for a patent can be taken from the eastern edge, where it is yellowing anyway, from a patch that no longer carries moths, in months when the water is low, with a line drawn around the small area I want and a count kept by Dr Okonjo's own hand, and a written term in the paper that no second place is ever opened. One patch. Not the grove. No cut in the living mat, except at the edge, once, to the limit of the paper. I will put all of that in writing before the council, today, and sign it with my name in front of you all, and if I break it the village can tear the paper in half. I am asking for two kilos of material and a road to move it over. The leaf tea stays where it is. The girl stays on the tea. Nobody in this room has to choose between my material and Dr Okonjo's tea, because the tea is made from what the tree grows every season and what I take is from the edge of a mat that is already dying, and both of those sentences are true at once and a reasonable man can hold both of them in one hand.'

Naledi sat very still.

He had done the thing that could not be argued with, and he had done it in front of the room, and what he said about the eastern edge had one true word in it and that word, yellowing, had come out of her own mouth at the grove and been measured in her own notebook. She could not call him a liar in Baro's hearing and be believed. She could only say what she knew: that a patch measured once is not a patch of a known size; that yellow and dead are not the same, and the eastern edge had carried moths on it two days ago; and that the boundary between a mat and the rest of a mat is a line drawn on a map, not a line in the ground, because in the ground those roots join up and the plant is one living thing, and no paper can draw a line that the plant has not drawn.

'A statement in a paper that no cut is made in the living mat is a sentence about a place where no paper goes,' she said. 'You have been in the room all morning and you know exactly what I mean. Every term you read cuts a line on a map and the map is not the ground. The ground has no eastern edge. The ground has root, and the root goes west, and the western host tree is older than the rest, and it remembers the last four cuts.'

'Then come and mark the line,' Renn said, as gently as he had ever said anything, and she saw the trap door open under her feet and go shut again, because he had offered it for the second time, in front of the whole room and in front of Baro, and there is only one way to refuse an offer like that in a village of thirty households with a sick child in half of them.

Baro read the room the way he read every room. He had his hands on his knees and his eyes going along the benches, and Naledi, from the back, watched him count his own secret ballot in the faces of eleven household heads who had put their names on a letter two months back because their children were sick and there was no other paper in the valley with a doctor's name on it.

'I will say the last thing,' Baro said. 'Then the council votes, and I will vote with it, whatever it decides.' He stood. He was an old man and his knees made a sound when he came off the chair, and he did not hide it. 'I have two grandchildren on Nuru's list. Chidi Emeka died at four years. Nobody in this room needs me to say that, and I am saying it because a man should say the thing he is about to decide on, out loud, in front of his neighbours, so that he cannot go home later and pretend he was somebody else. When the card came in from the dry riverbed I took it and I read it and there was no phone on this side of the canyon and no way to answer it and it went into my coat for a month, and then Toma's granddaughter went down, and I took the card out of the coat and said in the lane that we should send for the company man. I said we should send for him, and it was not for the valley and it was not for the council, it was for the two on that list, and everybody here knew it and nobody said a word against me, because you are my neighbours and you are the kind of neighbours that let a man do a thing like that and keep his face.' He put his hand on the paper. 'I am still that man. I have not changed overnight. Go home and think about it, and come back at dusk and vote, and if you vote to sign, sign, and we will all drink tea, and if you vote against, I will read out the vote and the answer will be no and I will stand in the door and tell the company man myself. That is what the council is for. It is not for being right. It is for being the sort of thing a village can stand behind. You cannot get one paper back from being signed. You cannot get a root back into the ground. That is what is on this table and that is all, and I am not going to tell you which way to vote, and I am going to have to sit down for this knee.'

The vote was at dusk, by the wall of the shop, and it was taken the way the village took things: a voice for each household head, one by one, in front of everyone, and Baro's voice went last so that no one could weigh it. Fifteen household heads had a case with a sick child in it. All fifteen said yes. Baro said yes, eleventh of the yeses, in a flat voice that did not move. There were four noes out of twelve who had nothing on the list, and the four noes went home and shut their doors. Two household heads did not come. The rest of the village, which is to say the wives, the grandmothers, the four families who had moved their mats into Toma's yard, and the children, stood outside the wall and listened, and said nothing, and went home.

Renn took the paper and signed it at the shop table by lamplight, and he signed all four copies so that the shop and the clinic and the council and the company each had one under their own hands, and he thanked the village in the careful, equal way he did everything, and then he said the thing that Naledi and Toma had known since noon and had not said out loud to each other.

'My men begin the road at first light,' he said. 'We will need to open a line through the brush on the eastern shoulder to get the hand-cart up. We will burn the standing litter on that line rather than cut it, because cut brush on a wet slope has to be dragged and burnt is quicker, and the burn will be kept small and on wet ground and I will have two men on the burn line and water carried up. Pending the site, of course, and the site east edge only. Dr Okonjo, I would still be glad to have you with us when we mark it.'

The village celebrated that night, because a village does.

Nobody put it that way and nobody thought about it that hard. There was a barrel of cane spirit in the shop and there was a fire in the lane where there was always a fire, and the household heads who had put down a yes brought their stools and their lamps and sat around it, and after a while the wives came, and the boys came with wood, and Renn's porters came and stood at the edge of the light with their bowls and were handed food and were pulled in by the arm like any stranger, and the fire went up and the rain held off and there was a while there when nobody in the valley was sick and nobody was being asked to decide anything.

Somebody had a drum. Two of the porters, who were from the coast and had walked two days into a valley they could not name, taught the older children a clapping game in a language the children did not know, and the children learned it at once, the way children do, so that the wrong words came out perfect, and their grandmothers laughed. Essa sat in a blanket on a stool with her feet up and was put in charge of the pot because she could reach it from where she sat, and she stirred it with the long spoon and told the smaller ones off for running near the fire, and once, when Naledi looked over, the girl was singing the clapping song under her breath to the pot, getting the wrong words right.

Renn came to the fire and stood. He was good at that too. He did not dance and he did not put himself forward and he did not leave early, and he accepted the cup when it came to him and drank from it and did not pretend to like it more than he did, and he asked Baro, in a voice the whole fire could hear, about the old channel that ran west, and Baro said what it was and where it went, and Renn said that a channel was a good thing for a village to have and he would have his men look at it on the way up, and Toma, across the fire with his stick on his knees, did not look up.

Naledi did not dance and did not drink much. She sat at the outer edge of the fire in the dark where the light stopped, with the notebook in her lap unopened, and watched the village be at ease, and did not wish it ill. That was the hard part. She could not find the place in herself where she wanted them to be afraid; what she wanted was for the paper on the shop table to become a lie on its own, and paper does not do that. Paper waits.

Toma came and sat on the end of the bench in the dark, far enough beyond the firelight that his face was nothing but a line.

'You are not at the fire,' he said.

'You are not either.'

'No. I am old and I am sick of being right, so I am at the edge. You are young and you are sick of being right, and you are also at the edge. That is a thing worth noticing.' His stick tapped once on the ground. 'They are happy tonight.'

'They should be. A company is paying a nurse's wage for five years and building a track. Their daughters are out of the fever and their children are at the fire. If I were Baro I do not know that I could have voted the other way either, and I have been sitting here for an hour trying to find the place in the arithmetic where he was wrong, and I cannot, and that is the part I have not said to anybody yet. It is not wrong. If what Renn says about a cut is true, then it is a small wrong for a large right and I have no ground to stand on at all. If what Renn says about a cut is untrue then he is lying about two kilos and he is lying in a way a paper cannot catch, and either way I am a woman who has spent this whole valley's patience telling it not to sign.' She turned a page without reading it and turned it back. 'Toma, when your grandfather's generation took the four roots — did they think they were wrong?'

Toma was a long time answering, which she had known he would be, and she did not fill the wait.

'No,' he said at last. 'That is the part I have never said to you, and I have been carrying it since the night in the cave. They did not think they were wrong. They thought they were trading. There was a fever on this side of the canyon that year and the man on the other side of it had a boat and an offer and a road out, and the four families that took the root took it the way a man takes the last tin of paraffin before the wet — because there was a winter coming and a fire to keep, and they said the same thing to each other that your man said in the room today. Not the whole grove. Not the living stand. Four roots, cut clean, from the edge, where the water was low and the yellow was already standing, and a document with a figure on it in the hand of the man who came up the canyon nine wet seasons ago and did not come back out. Do you hear it? He came up the canyon nine wet seasons ago and did not come back out, and he was not a better man than the man at your fire and he was not a worse one, and it took him one day to use the same words about a cut from the edge that Renn has been using since noon, and it did not cost him a day of sleep. The arithmetic is not what went wrong with him, doctor. The arithmetic was correct. It is the paper, and how a man sits down at a fire and signs his neighbours' hill away from them one small true sentence at a time, and the fact that a man can do that and sleep, and that the ones who do not sleep are all old and sitting at the edge.'

Naledi looked out at the fire. He was right and she knew he was right and there was nothing to say and the fact sat between them in the dark like a stone.

'You still think it will be different with Renn,' he said.

'I do not know whether it will be different with Renn. I think it can be. I have been in this profession for twenty years and I have watched men with permits do the wrong thing in twenty ways, and I have also watched the paper do the protecting, once, at a site in the delta where the bound was written by the man who stood to gain from it and it held for eleven years after he retired because the bound was a fair one and he was an honest man and he outlived his own money.' She shut the notebook. 'What I know is that I have one part of it I have not used yet, and it is not the tea, and it is not the ditch. It is the survey. This basin was surveyed in 1972 and never again and the only record is in a cabinet in the university's annex, and a survey record is the one kind of paper that can stand against a contract, because it is a paper about the place and not about a deal. If the stand is on the record as a single registered biological entity with one host species and one range, then no contract signed in a village lane can grant a right that contradicts it, and Renn can go and argue his two kilos and his edge with the survey in front of his own lawyer. It is an old paper and it is weak and it has holes in it and every one of those things is true, and it is the only door I have left that opens on the other side of the canyon. I am writing to the board tonight and asking for an emergency survey order. I will walk it down to the canyon mouth at first light and leave it with the truck man at the dry riverbed and pay him to carry it to the town and pay him again if it is answered.'

'And if it is not answered?'

'Then it is not answered.'

'And if it is answered slowly?'

'Then it is answered slowly.'

'And by the time it is answered, the road is up and the cut is made and the paper has been signed at two fires,' Toma said, and got up, because the fire was going down and the drums had stopped and the children were being carried home.

'Yes,' Naledi said, to his back, in the dark, because there was no other true thing to say.

She wrote the letter in the clinic, by Nuru's lamp, on the last two leaves of the writing pad that Nuru kept for public business, and she wrote it three times.

The first one argued. She read it back and saw her own hand going up and down the page the way it had gone up and down when she was twenty-five, and she burnt it in the tin.

The second one pleaded. She read that one back too and it was worse, because it was true and it was smart and it made the board into people who could be moved by a good sentence, and she had once been a person who believed that, and she remembered exactly how it had ended.

The third one she wrote flat, in the register of a woman writing a note about a delivery.

She set out the stand, its position, its single host species, its dependency on a pollinating moth, the probable legal status of the range under the 1972 survey, and the presence of the stand on no mapped land register. She did not name Renn. She did not mention her own suspension or her permit or her credentials. She did not ask to be reinstated or permitted or paid. She asked for one thing: an emergency order to survey and to declare, under the board's existing procedure, on the ground that a stand of the species was imminently at risk of being removed by a party acting under a village contract, and that no other person in the profession had standing on the ground and the board therefore had to. She gave the dates. She gave the flood line. She asked them to send a name and a date, and to send the surveyor on foot, and to send the acknowledgement to the nurse at Sorrow's Gate. She signed her name and her registration number and did not sign it Doctor, because the board had said not to, and she had written out the number from memory and it had never once left her.

Then she read it through twice for the facts, and once for the tone, and folded it, and sealed it, and wrote on the outside, in her clearest hand: To the Registrar, Board of Field Credentials — acknowledgement requested, and gave it to Nuru to put with the medicines, because Nuru's bag went down to the dry riverbed once a fortnight and her own hands were bleeding.

'You have put no complaint in it,' Nuru said, reading the outside and not the in. 'They suspended you and you have been in this valley three weeks with no permit and you have put no complaint in it, and I have watched you write it, and you did not put one word in it about the Kalu matter at all.'

'There is nothing that can be said about it that would help. They know what they did. A letter is not the place. If the board says yes I will have got what I came for, and if it says no then the complaint on file will not be worth the flour it is written in, and it will be true and it will be there.'

'You have not opened their letter,' Nuru said, and went on writing in her book, and that was that.

They went down to the canyon mouth on the second morning, and they did not go alone, because word had it that Baro's grandchildren's names had gone on the list of the households that voted at the wall, and Baro's household had voted yes with the rest, and the four who had voted no had one sick child between them and had voted no anyway, and Renn's road had begun at the eastern shoulder that morning with a party of eight, and Renn had gone up with them and had walked the western channel on the way and looked at the fall of the ground and asked Baro's son the name of the stream where it met the lower valley.

The dry riverbed was a plain of grey stone with the truck tracks baked into it and the truck man's hut at the top of it, and the truck man had a bed and a radio and a crate of paraffin and was not there, and they sat and waited and the mist came down over the canyon mouth and it was cold.

Naledi walked the first flood line while they waited. She measured it again with the string, out of habit, and got the same number she had got on the way in, the 2.4 metre mark above the present water, and stood with her hand on the canyon wall and looked at the second line, at 3.1, which she had not measured with the same care, because on the way in it had been a curiosity and now it was a number with a stake on it. Three point one metres. The footbridge at the village was two point two metres above the present water on her own levelling, and the whole lower valley downstream of the grove's western shoulder was lower than that, and the old channel she and Toma had cleared ran the whole way between, and if a flood came to 3.1 in the canyon it would stand in the lower valley and the girl's house and the shop and the schoolroom were at 2.5 and 2.6 and the four families camped in Toma's yard were at 2.7.

She wrote the figures down with the date and the time and did not say anything about them to Toma, because it was a thing to think about and not a thing to say out loud at the mouth of a canyon where the other person was already carrying enough.

The truck man came down the riverbed at dusk and took the letter and put it in the tin box under the seat that was for public business, and said he would carry it to the town hall post on the next run and would not take a coin now and would take one when he got back if it was answered.

'And if it is not?' Naledi said.

'Then I bring you the outside and nothing inside,' the truck man said, 'and you owe me nothing, and you will know.'

They were three hours up the riverbed on the way home when it began to rain. Not the fine wet mist of the valley mouth and not the wind-laden rain of the cliffs — real rain, high rain, rain that came down out of the northeast with the whole weight of the sky behind it, the kind that fills the upper seeps in an hour, and it came with a wind that had no place in the middle of the dry season, and within half an hour the riverbed under their feet, which had been grey stone, was grey water the depth of a hand.

They stood in it in the dark with their coats over their heads and looked at one another.

Naledi got out the notebook and wrote the date in her wet hand, at the top of a new page, and under it: rain; northeast; heavy; began at the mouth, not in the valley. Then she put the notebook away under her coat closer to her chest, and they walked up out of the flood with the water at their ankles and the dark coming down, and the rain went on the whole way home, and everyone in the lower valley who heard it stop and start on the roofs that night knew that the wet season had come early, in a valley where the wet season was the one thing no one had ever been able to argue with.
