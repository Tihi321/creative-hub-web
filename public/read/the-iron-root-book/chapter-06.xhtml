What the Moths Keep

They left the village at first light, before the mist had lifted off the river, and Toma did not take the path up the wet channel. He took the long way, along the contact where the limestone met the clay, and Naledi understood the choice without needing it explained: the channel was the road the water used, and the water had been using it hard.

They climbed for three hours. Naledi counted her paces for the first thousand and then let the count go, because the slope broke it up, and because there was more to read than there was to number. The ground changed under her boots in stages. First the wet leaf litter of the lower slope, soft and giving, full of the smell of crushed green. Then the stone of the shelf, swept clean, with silt only in the hollows. Then, above the shelf, a band of shattered rock with thin soil in the cracks, and in that soil a low scrub that she did not know, and in the scrub the ordinary small traffic of the valley, birds and insects and a thin brown snake that crossed their path and went into a crevice with the unhurried confidence of an animal that had never been chased.

The rain held off. The clouds stayed low and grey and did not break, and the air was so wet that her shirt felt damp without any rain falling on it, and the copper smell came and went with the wind, stronger on the rises, weaker in the hollows.

At the top of the shattered rock the scrub ended, and the ground became something else. Naledi stopped and looked at it for a while before she stepped onto it.

It was a root mat.

She had seen the pale dusting from the shelf the day before and had not been able to name it. Now she was standing at the edge of the thing itself, and the naming came easily, because it was not a mystery once you were on it. The ground above the flood line was not soil. It was a woven mat of roots, pale grey-brown, cable-thick at the thickest, hair-thin at the thinnest, running over and around and between the stones of the slope in a way that made the stones secondary, incidental, like the bones inside a hand. The mat was deep enough that a boot did not sink through it. It was shallow enough that the shape of the rock beneath showed through it in the places where the roots ran thin.

And over the mat, in a low drifting layer that moved with the smallest air, there was dust. Pale, fine, almost white in the grey light. The same dust she had found on the flood-brought branch below the village. The same dust she had seen from the shelf. It was not dust at all, she thought, pressing the heel of her hand down and lifting it to look. It was scales. Tiny, flat, overlapping, the kind of thing that comes off a wing.

Toma stood at the edge of the mat and did not walk on it.

‘This is as far as I go,’ he said.

‘You have been here before.’

‘A boy. Once.’ He planted his stick in the rock at his feet and leaned on it. ‘I do not walk on the mat. The mat is the plant. Put your weight on a root in the wrong season and you are standing on the thing you came for.’

Naledi looked at the ground she was standing on and then at the ground ahead of her, and chose the path with the most stone and the least root, and went forward slowly, one foot and then the other, and Toma stayed where he was and watched her go.

The host trees began thirty paces in.

They were not tall and they were not old, except for one, and Naledi saw the one first because it was the only one that stood above the rest and the only one whose lower trunk had the dark stain that comes with years. She counted the others from where she stood. Twenty-nine trunks, none of them thicker than her forearm, pale grey bark, small dark leaves in a thin canopy that let the grey light straight through it. The ground beneath them was entirely root mat. Nothing else grew there. No moss, no ferns, no seedlings of anything. The mat had the floor to itself, and the host trees rose out of it at intervals, and between one trunk and the next, in the pale dust of the surface, there were small round holes, dozens of them, each about the width of a thumb.

Naledi crouched beside the nearest hole and put one finger in it. It was not deep. It turned a corner almost at once. She lay down on the mat — carefully, spreading her weight, though she was not sure it helped — and put her eye to the hole and waited for her vision to adjust to the dark inside it.

Something in there was moving. Small, pale, segmented. She could not see it properly and did not try to dig it out. She lay there with her cheek against the root mat and her eye at the hole and her hands flat, and she thought about what she was looking at. A nest. A nursery. The mat was layered like a fine bale of hay, and inside its layers, in the dry air the mat held, there were small warm chambers, and the chambers held small pale young, and the whole floor of the grove was a house.

She got up slowly and went back to the edge of the mat where the first host tree stood.

The moths came out at dusk, and Naledi had not expected them, and so she was not ready, and that was the only reason she almost missed it.

She had spent the afternoon on the mat with her notebook and her sample bags, working in the small careful way she had been trained to work. She had measured the mat's depth at six points and recorded the figures. She had taken three leaf samples from three separate host trees, one leaf each, wrapped in waxed paper and labelled. She had found one place on the eastern edge of the stand where the root mat had yellowed above a break, and she had looked at that for a long time, because a break in a root mat is not a natural thing.

And then the light began to go, and Toma, from the edge of the mat where he had stayed all day, called her back, and she stood up and turned and saw the air above the grove fill with wings.

They came out of the mat itself.

For a moment she thought it was smoke, the way the pale dust lifted in one long soft exhalation from the whole floor of the stand at once, from every hole and every crevice of every root. And then the smoke resolved into bodies, hundreds of them, thousands perhaps, and the bodies had wings, and the wings were pale and powdery, and the air over the grove became a slow drifting snow of moths rising from their daytime chambers into the cooling dark.

Naledi stood still and watched. She had not stood still on purpose. The stillness had happened to her.

The moths did not fly away. They settled again, most of them, on the low trunks of the host trees and on the upper surface of the root mat, and they sat there with wings half-open and did nothing at all. The grove was covered in them, in rows, like a field planted in beige.

She turned her head very slowly to find Toma.

‘Now,’ he said. ‘Look at the mat where you lifted the root.’

She looked. She had pulled one dead twig from the litter, and had lifted one short section of root to see how it lay, and had put it back. It had taken four seconds. She had put it back with care. And she looked at the place where she had lifted it, and saw the yellowing.

It was not the same yellowing as the eastern edge. It was fresher, greener-yellow, the colour of a leaf in the first week of leaving a tree. It had spread up the length of the lifted root from the point she had held it, and outward into the adjacent mat by an inch or more. And in that patch, in a bare circle about the length of her arm, there were no moths. None. The pale rows ended at the edge of the patch as if a line had been drawn, and beyond the line there were moths, and inside it there was root mat and pale dust and nothing alive.

Naledi stood at the edge of the patch and looked at it and understood something that had been assembling in her hand for four days and had finally finished.

‘It is the moths,’ she said.

Toma did not answer. He did not need to.

‘The mat is the plant,’ she said, still to the patch. ‘The mat is the plant, and the plant is one thing, and the thing is the whole floor, not the root. The trunk is not the plant. The host tree is not the plant. The host tree is the stand, and the moths are the part that keeps the stand alive, and I have been looking for a root to take down to the village for a week, and there is no root to take. There is no root that is not the whole mat. Cut one and the mat bleeds where you cut it, and the moths know before I do, and they leave, and the leaving is the killing.’ She stopped and looked at her hands. ‘I lifted it for four seconds. I put it back flat. I did not cut it. And it is dying where I touched it, and the moths have already written it off, and by the morning that patch will be the colour of the eastern edge, and by next season the mat will have re-knitted around it or the mat will not have re-knitted around it, and I do not know which, and I cannot know which, because I have just done to this grove the thing that killed the last one.’

She sat down in the wet leaf litter at the edge of the mat. The sitting was not dignified. It was the kind of sitting that happens when the legs do it without asking.

Toma came and sat beside her, on the rock, where the mat did not run, and for a while he said nothing. The moths were settling on the trees and on the mat and the light was nearly gone, and the pale rows were visible as a faint grey now, and the patch where she had lifted the root was a darker smudge on the pale ground, like a thumbprint on a page.

‘I have done the same thing,’ he said.

‘I know. You told me. A boy. You pulled a root and watched the grove die for it.’

‘No.’ He shook his head. ‘That was a truer thing than the one I told you. I gave you the shape of it before, and it was the true shape, but it was not the whole of it. I was there for four. Four roots, on the west side, in one afternoon, and I was fourteen and I was strong and the men wanted a boy to hold the lantern and to reach in under the mat where the shovels could not go. My grandfather cut the first. The others cut the rest. But the fourth — the last one — was mine. I put my arm in under the mat and closed my hand on the root and pulled it out, and there was a moth on it, and I shook the moth off onto the ground and gave the root to my grandfather, and he said good.’ He looked at the patch. ‘A grove takes thirty years to come back. I gave it thirty years. That is what I have been doing with the rest of my life, and now there is a woman from the lowlands sitting in my valley doing it again in four seconds with a notebook, and I have brought her here myself, and it is still my arithmetic and not yours. Do not take all of it. You did not know. You have only just found out, and I have known for fifty years, and I am the one who brought you up here to stand on the mat. The hand at the end of the arm is mine as much as yours.’

Naledi looked at him. It was too dark to see his face clearly and she did not try. She thought that he had just done the thing she was least able to do, which was to hand her a share of his own blame and hand her none of the reason to feel it.

‘We are going to lose the patch,’ she said.

‘Most likely.’

‘Can we do anything?’

‘You can stop walking on the mat,’ Toma said. ‘That is what you can do. And you can write down what you have seen here, so that whoever comes next knows before they touch it. The next one will not be me, and it will not be you, and it will be someone who has not spent the day with the moths, and they will need the page.’

They made a low camp at the edge of the mat, on stone, in the lee of the big western host tree, and Naledi took out her sample bags and the small kit she had brought up from the village and did what she could by lamplight.

She had three leaves, one from each of three host trees. She put the first leaf on a clean square of waxed paper and worked at it with the flat of her knife, crushing a corner of the leaf against the paper, pressing the green juice out of it and onto the paper and letting it dry, and then holding the paper to the lamp and looking at it through the small eyeglass she used for seed.

The colour of the dried juice was a dull green-brown, unremarkable. But she was not looking for the colour. She was looking for the compound she had come to the valley for, the one the fever answered to, and she had read the old notes on it, and the compound had a habit she knew. It went brown when it dried. And then, when a drop of the weak acid from her kit was put on it, it went a particular shade of red. It was a crude test. It was the test you used when you had no laboratory and no microscope and a sick child in a village below.

She put the drop on the dried juice of the first leaf.

It went red.

She sat back on her heels and looked at the little red-brown stain on the paper and did not say anything, because she was working, and the working had not finished.

She did the same thing with the second leaf, from a different tree, and got the same red.

She did the same thing with the third leaf, from a third tree, and got the same red, only weaker, a shade lighter, a thinner edge to it.

She sat on the stone for a while and looked out at the grove in the lamplight, at the pale ghost-rows of the moths on the trunks, and she thought very carefully, because a result is a thing you have to think carefully about before you say it out loud.

The compound was in the leaves. It was in every leaf she had tested, from every tree. It was weaker than she expected. The old notes said the compound was strongest in the root, and now she could see why: the root was the reservoir, the deep store, the place where the plant had put the most of it. The leaves had less. The leaves had enough, though. The leaves had enough to be worth brewing, if a child were sick and there was no other way, and if the leaf was taken the way the old families took it, one leaf and not the tree, one leaf and not the root, in the season when the tree could spare it.

She said it out loud, because Toma was awake beside her and because saying a thing out loud is the only way to know if it stands up.

‘The leaf will do it,’ she said. ‘Not as strong. Strong enough. The leaf will do it, and the leaf is the part that comes back. I came up here for the root, and the root cannot be taken, and I have been trying to understand for a week why the old families here took the leaf and not the root, and now I know, and it is not a superstition and it is not a rule for the sake of a rule, and it is not even a mercy to the tree. It is arithmetic. The root is the plant. The leaf is the plant's work, and the work comes back every season, and the root does not. Take four roots and the whole floor dies and dies slow and takes the moths with it, and the fever gets worse, and the fever was worse that autumn because the grove was sick. I have been looking at this wrong the whole time. I have been looking for the plant that cures the fever, and I have found the plant that cures the fever, and there is nothing wrong with it that needs curing. The valley is not sick. The valley is what makes the medicine, and everything I have seen here, the moths and the mat and the tree and the leaf, is one living thing, and I have spent the day walking on it, and I am sitting on the edge of it right now deciding whether I am going to save the child or save the grove, and the answer is that I do not have to choose, and the reason I do not have to choose is that I came up here yesterday looking for the wrong part of the plant for the wrong reason, and I am looking at the right part of it now, and the right part of it grows back.’

She stopped.

Toma had not moved. At the edge of his voice there was something she had not heard in it before, and it took her a moment to name it, and when she named it she found it was not gratitude. It was recognition.

‘That is the sentence,’ he said.

‘What?’

‘The one you came up the valley for. You have been holding it for four days and not saying it. Say it to the council, in the morning. Say it the way you have just said it. Baro will sign nothing and they will throw the paper on the fire, and if they do not, then I will go to the fire myself and burn the paper for them.’

She did not sleep well.

She lay on the stone at the edge of the mat with her coat over her and the lamp low, and she listened to the moths. She could not hear them, exactly, but there was a sound, a low papery sound, like a cloth being moved in another room, and she understood after an hour that it was the moths themselves, thousands of them on the trunks and the mat and the undersides of the leaves, moving their wings a fraction of an inch now and then, and the sum of all those fractions was a sound, and the sound was the grove breathing.

She woke once in the night and looked at the patch where she had lifted the root, and in the dark it was a black hole in the pale rows of wings, a place where the moths would not sit, and she thought about the arithmetic, and about the four roots of Toma's grandfather and the season of worse fever that came after. And she understood what the arithmetic was actually called. It was not arithmetic. Arithmetic does not have a season and does not come back on you. It was something closer to debt. You take a part of a thing that lives, and you do not know what the thing will do to live without it, and the thing does something, and the something arrives in the village below as a fever, and nobody in the village connects the fever to the root, because the root was taken three seasons ago and the fever is now. That was the whole shape of the trap, and she had nearly walked into it herself, and Toma had carried the knowledge of the trap for fifty years without spending it, and Baro had been about to sign a paper that would spring it again.

In the morning she would go down. She would go down with the leaves in their waxed paper and the page in her notebook, and she would stand in front of the council and say the sentence, and if they asked her for proof she would show them the patch in the grove, and if they asked her why she had lifted the root she would tell them the truth, that she had lifted it to see how it lay, and that she had not known, and that not knowing was not the same as not being responsible, and she would not offer it as an excuse.

And then she would brew the tea. She would brew it with the leaves, weak, measured, one leaf to a cup, the way the old families brewed it, and she would give it to the children, and she would watch them for two days and if the fever did not break she would have to think again, and if it did break, she would have the thing she had come for, and she would have it without taking anything out of the ground.

She sat up before dawn on the stone at the edge of the mat, with the wet leaf litter at her hip and the moths beginning to fold their wings back into the mat, and the grey light coming up over the canyon mouth, and the copper smell arriving with the wind off the morning valley, and she thought: the valley did not need me to cure it. The valley needed me to stop. And I have been trying to fix things by adding to them for so long that I did not recognise the fixing when it arrived, and it arrived as an old man with a stick telling me to ask the leaf before the root, and I have spent four days not understanding the simplest sentence in the valley, and this morning I understand it, and this morning I am going down to the village to brew tea for a girl who has been sick for six days, and I have never in my life been so certain of the next two days and so certain of nothing after them.

Toma woke. He did not speak. He stood up, worked the stiffness out of his leg, took his stick, and turned his face downslope, which was his way of saying everything that needed saying.

Naledi gathered the waxed papers and the notebook and the pencil and the lamp and put them in the bag, and she took one more look at the patch at the edge of the mat, and then she stepped off the mat and back onto the stone, and she followed him down.

The eastern edge of the stand was still yellow. The patch she had made was still dark. The moths had not come back to either, and the mat at the western edge, around the one old host tree, was pale and breathing and whole, and would be whole for exactly as long as nobody came up the valley with a saw.
