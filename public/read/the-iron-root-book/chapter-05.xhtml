The Night Stalker

The cave was not where Naledi would have chosen to spend a night. It was a shallow overhang on the east side of the shelf, dry at the back and wet at the mouth, no deeper than three tall men lying end to end and not as wide as a room in the village. The floor was a mix of fine silt and small flat stones, and the silt, when she put her palm flat on it, was dry to the second knuckle.

Toma found it before dark. He did not look for a cave. He looked at the slope above them and followed a line of moss that stopped where the rock overhung, and then he walked along the base of the overhang with his stick held out at arm's length and his head down, and when the stick touched nothing for four steps he said, "Here," and went in.

They moved what they needed up from the shelf: the two lamps, the food bag, the cord, the waxed notebook. The rain had settled into a steady, vertical thing, not hard, but the kind of rain that in the valley meant hours. Naledi sat with her back against the rock and her boots off and her socks wrung out and hung on her knee, and ate two handfuls of cold rice from the bag Toma had brought, and said nothing, because they had both been in the rain all day and the saying of nothing was part of the drying.

Just before full dark, she went out to the mouth of the cave and looked down.

The new channel below the shelf was still moving. The water had dropped perhaps a foot since the flood, but the bed was wider and stonier and it had cut a fresh bank on the far side that had not been there that morning. The upper basin floor lay under a shallow sheet of brown water in the low places, from the contact line down to the trees, and a shallow sheet of brown water was exactly what she had spent two days mapping and one night thinking about. Children stood in it. Children played in it. Children drank from the pools it left behind when it drained. She noted that down, because it was the shape of an answer, even if it was not the answer yet.

When she turned back into the cave Toma had already lain down, rolled in his coat, with his stick along his side, facing the entrance. He did not sleep with his back to a door. She had noticed that at the canyon gate and had not asked about it, and she did not ask about it now.

She lay down on the other side of the cave with her head on the waxed bag, and she pulled her socks on wet, because dry socks at night in a wet place are a story you tell yourself, and she put out one lamp and left the small one burning low between them, and she looked at the rock ceiling until she no longer needed to look at it.

She did not know what woke her.

That was the first thing she noticed, later, when she went back over it: not the sound, because there was no sound yet, but the stopping of her own sleep, the way you wake when a person has come into a room and stood there without speaking. She lay still with her eyes open and looked at the pale patch of the cave mouth and listened.

Rain on stone. Rain on leaves just outside. The small thread of the channel down below, still running. Toma breathing, easy, on the other side.

And then, on the drip line just at the mouth of the cave, something scraped.

It was not a hoof. It was not a shoe. It was the sound of a weight resting on a surface and then shifting along it, and under the scrape there was a low, wet, patient kind of breathing.

Naledi turned her head.

In the mouth of the cave, blacker than the dark behind it, there was a shape. She could not see its edges. She could see the paler grey of the rain-filled night beyond it, and into that paler grey there reached down a line that resolved, slowly, into a long arm, or a long neck, or a long leg, resting its lower end on the wet stone and shifting it back and forth, an inch, half an inch, an inch, with a soft grit sound like a boot in gravel.

The shape was not in the cave. It was standing at the entrance, and it was turned a little so that she could not see a face, and it was breathing, and it was waiting for something to happen inside.

She reached out, very slowly, for the little lamp.

Toma's voice came so quietly from the dark that she could not have said whether it had arrived through the air or through the ground.

"Do not touch the lamp."

She stopped her hand where it was.

"Put it out."

She found the little wheel on the lamp, and turned it, and the light died, and the cave went to the colour of the inside of a closed eye.

"He hunts what moves and shines," Toma said, in the same near-silent voice. "He has been here before. He knows a cave. He is waiting for the lamp to move, or for one of us to get up. If the lamp moves, it is a small sun on the wall and it says, Here, here, two of them, one of them old. If we do not move, he has nothing. He does not know what the dark is. He knows what the dark is not."

"What is he?"

"An old one. He comes down when the water is up and the small game has moved. He was not here this far up last season. This season the water pushed everything down and up at once."

"How long until light?"

"Two hours. Maybe more. Sit still."

Sit still, she thought, is a method. Voice low, lamp out, body down, no glint. She lay on her side and did not move except to breathe, and she made her breathing slow, because the one thing she could not stop was the body, and the body heard things and translated them into pulse before she had time to translate them into words.

The shape at the entrance shifted its weight.

She heard the pad of the foot, or whatever took the place of a foot, come down a little further in, past the drip line, onto the dry silt. She heard a breath drawn in through a long nose and let out slow, and then another, deeper, the breath of a thing tasting the cave. There was a smell. It was not a bad smell. It was a warm, animal, wet-fur smell, and it filled the small space like a person coming into a kitchen with bread in his coat.

Beside her in the dark, Toma did not move.

The animal came in further. She could tell by the sound of the scrape, which was now on a different stone. Then it stopped, and turned, on the spot, and something long and heavy passed over the floor of the cave and out again, and came back, and out again, and back, and she understood that it was going around the inside of the cave's mouth in a slow half-circle, the way you circle a thing you have not yet decided about, and that its body must be wider than a person's shoulders, because each pass of it took longer than a person would.

She lay with her face toward the wall and her hands under her cheek and her eyes closed, because eyes in the dark serve nothing, and she listened to the breathing. It came round the mouth four times. On the fourth pass the breath was close enough to her head that she could feel the warmth of it arrive, faintly, and go away.

She did not move. She counted, because counting was something for the mind to do. One, two, three. The sound of the animal breathing. Four, five, six. The sound of Toma breathing. Seven, eight, nine. The sound of her own heart, which was not useful and would not stop. She got to four hundred and something and lost the count when the animal scraped on the drip line again.

And then, at some point that she could not have placed in time, the sound became rain on stone and nothing else. No breathing. No scrape. Only the thread of the channel down below, and the low steady hiss at the cave mouth, and Toma's voice, after a long while, saying:

"He is gone down."

"How do you know?"

"Because the small ones are talking again."

She lay still and listened and he was right, though she had not heard it until he said it: low in the leaf litter below the shelf, small things had started up again, crickets and frogs and the thin pip of something, the ordinary weather of a wet night, all of it absent for the last hour and a half, and all of it now returned.

She breathed out, and found her hands, and found they had gone to sleep under her cheek, and she rolled over onto her back.

"You knew this before we slept," she said. "You knew he was in the valley."

"Yes. This season he is down low. I said so on the shelf."

"You said 'the lower slopes.'"

"This is the lower slopes," Toma said. "Everything is below the grove."

It did not rain past midnight, and by the time the palest grey arrived in the cave mouth the water outside had gone to a low, ordinary sound. Naledi crawled out first with the small lamp lit in her hand, because morning made the lamp less interesting, and stood on the wet slab outside the cave with her arms crossed, looking at the ground.

The tracks were plain, which she had not expected. She had expected the mud of the paths to have swallowed them. But the animal had come along the top of the shelf, and the shelf was stone with a thin skin of silt in the hollows, and the silt had held the prints like the inside of a mould.

She crouched. Each print was a broad pad the length of her two hands end to end, with four clear toe marks fanning in front and a fifth behind, and the front of each pad was scalloped, not smooth. The claws had not gone in. The prints were so deep in the shallow silt that the animal must have been heavy, and the claws did not appear, and the fifth toe was set well back, and Naledi stayed crouched over the fourth print for a while and did not say anything.

"What is it?" Toma said, behind her.

"I don't know," she said, which was the truth. She had seen pug marks before, in the country where she had done her training, and those prints had been prints of a different shape and a different scale. She wrote in her notebook: Large pad, four forward toes, fifth set back, claws not registering, pad length approx the span of two hands. Equivalent to a heavy animal. No matching print in the local list she had seen. She would have to check it against what she knew and what the village knew, when they got down. She looked at the trail. It went along the shelf eastward for thirty metres, and then it turned, and it went down through the low gap she had crossed the day before, toward the head of the wet channel and the wood below it, and then it kept going down.

"He has gone toward the lower valley," she said.

"Yes. He goes where the small game is. Late in the season he will come back up."

"Does he take children?"

Toma stood with his stick planted and looked down the line of the tracks for a while before he answered.

"He takes what is small and what has wandered," he said. "He has not taken a child from the village. The children are in bed at night, and their houses have doors, and doors are what he does not understand. He understands a cave and he understands a stream and he understands a hen. A hen in a lane at night he will take. This is why the hens go in."

"Then he is a cost we pay," Naledi said, standing up and wiping her hands on the seat of her trousers. "A hen for a lair and a bad night." She folded the notebook back into the waxed bag. "He was not what I came for."

"No," Toma said. "But you have written him down."

"I have written him down as a method. Lamp out, voice low, body still, wait for the small ones."

"That is a method," Toma said. "And it is cheaper than a rifle."

They walked down in the middle of the morning, the long way, around the far edge of the hornbill stand rather than under it, and past the standing water on the basin floor, which had fallen half a foot overnight and left behind a pale line on the grass and a small, clear, sloping pool the shape of a spoon, sitting in the middle of the footpath the children used. Naledi stopped at the pool and looked at it and wrote down its dimensions. The shape of the answer was assembling in her hand. She did not yet have the sentence.

She had her notebook open at the fold while they walked, on and off, checking her two pages from the night before, and reading what she had written under them, and adding, at a stone where they stopped to let Toma's leg rest, this:

Stalker works the lower slopes this wet season, moving down after the rain pushed the small game. It circles at night; it hunts by movement and by light; it does not enter a dark cave. Do not sleep in the open on the lower slopes this season. Lamp out; sit still; wait for the return of small voices.

She closed the notebook and put the pencil behind her ear and looked at Toma, who was sitting on the stone with both hands on his stick and his eyes on the valley below them and the roofs of Sorrow's Gate coming up out of the mist.

He had something else to say. She had been able to tell that for two days, because his silences had a shape, and this one had the shape of a thing being carried.

"You have been wanting to say something since the cave," she said.

"Not since the cave."

"Since the canyon gate."

He thought about it. He was not a man who hurried into agreement.

"Since the canyon gate," he said. "But not what you think. Now that you have seen this valley do three things to you and you have read all three, I will tell you the story, because you have paid for it, and because the child in the village is my own."

He set the stick across his knees.

"Before the road to the canyon, the valley was tended," he said. "Not by one village. By four families whose houses stood above the flood line, where the plot is now. They took the leaf and not the root. They cut one tree in the grove in seven years, and only trees that had stopped putting out leaf. They took the small wood from the lower slopes and never from the shelf, because the shelf is the cradle of the grove. They did not take a hen in late season because the hen eats the seed of the swallowed fig, and the swallowed fig is what feeds the stalker when the small game has gone, and the stalker keeps the boar down. That was the shape of it. Every ribbon in the weave held the next ribbon in. They kept the rules for as long as anyone could remember."

"And then?"

"My grandfather's generation," Toma said. "Two generations back. There was money in the lowlands, and the young men went down to the market to buy it, and one of them did arithmetic. He said, We do not take the root because of the rules, but we do not know what happens if we take the root. He said, The rules are an old story told by old people who are afraid of a bad season. So they tried. They took four roots from beneath the grove, and they sold them, and the season after that the grove was quieter, and the season after that there was yellowing above the western edge, and by the third season half the stand had not put out new root, and the moth — the moth was seen less. The fever came down that autumn worse than any autumn anyone remembered. The people said it was a judgement. I do not think it was a judgement. I think it was arithmetic they had not included."

"And the grove now?"

"The grove recovered, in the years of my youth, because there was a bad wet season and the water kept everyone out and the stand was left alone. The old stand you saw is younger than I am. There is one host tree in it older than the rest, the big one on the west side, and that one was already there. It remembers the four roots."

They sat for a while. Below them, in the mist, the roofs, the lane, the clinic with one light in one window. Somewhere under those roofs a girl who was twelve, with a fever she did not yet have a name for, was being given water in small spoonfuls by a nurse with no microscope.

"Why have you never said any of this to the council?" Naledi said. "Baro is head of the council. The council sent for the company man. If they knew the grove had been pulled before, and what went with it, the paper would not have been signed."

"Perhaps." Toma turned the stick a quarter turn in his hands. "Perhaps it would. Perhaps Baro would have said, And so one more pull, at a fair price, and this time we will manage it better than the young men did."

"That is not an argument for silence."

"It is not," he said. "I do not offer it as an argument. I will give you what I have got, which is not an argument. Some knowledge costs more than it heals. I have watched what happens when a village learns that the thing under the grove is worth money. It learns the price first and the shape second, and by the time it has learned the shape, the thing is gone. I chose to carry it and not spend it. That is the whole of my reason, and I do not expect you to think well of it."

Naledi was quiet.

She had a moment — she would be able to describe it later, if she chose — of standing outside her own body and seeing the two of them on the stone: an old man with a stick and a wet coat, and a woman with a notebook and a pencil behind her ear and a suspension letter folded in a pocket she had not opened. And the sentence he had just said came up through her, and she heard it in her own voice, and she knew she had used it before.

"Some knowledge costs more than it heals," she said, slowly, mostly to the valley.

"Yes."

"I have said that."

"I know," Toma said. "It is what you have been saying with your hands since the second day. You write a thing down and you keep it in a bag, and you decide who sees the bag. You have not showed the council your notebook. You have shown Baro two hundred words on one page and kept the rest. I have watched you do it."

"That is not the same thing," she said. "I keep the notebook quiet because half of what is in it is wrong. It is not a secret, it is a draft."

"That is a good reason," Toma said. "It is the reason I have given myself, in fifty years of carrying this, at least half the time. The other half of the time I have had no reason at all. I have simply carried it. I do not offer it to you as an example. I offer it to you as a price that I have paid, which is that this morning I do not know whether telling Baro last month would have kept the paper off the table or brought Renn up the canyon two weeks earlier. I will never know. I have decided to live with the not knowing, and I have decided badly, and that is what a rule is for, and I broke it a long time ago and I am still under the sentence."

The mist below them lifted enough to show the river, running grey and high between its stones, and the footbridge, and a person walking across it with a basket.

Toma stood up. He put his stick in the ground, took it up again, and started down along the path. He had gone four or five steps before he said, over his shoulder, in his ordinary short way, as if he had only just thought of it:

"You have one day of Baro's leave left, so far as I count it. That day is today. We can use the afternoon on the list of days, the water, the dust on the branch. I do not think we will reach the grove today. But if you want to know what heals the child, ask the leaf before the root, and ask the small ones. The small ones knew about the stalker before we did."

Naledi stood where she was with the waxed bag in her hand and looked down at the village and at the man going down the path in front of her, and she thought: two days ago he had told her she was holding the wrong end of the fever question. Now he had told her the whole story of the valley, and the whole story of himself, and she had not been able to tell which was the cure and which was the cost, and she was not sure the two had ever been separate things in this valley, or in her, or in anyone who had ever been made to choose.

She put the pencil behind her ear and followed him down.
