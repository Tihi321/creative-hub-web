The Scout's Arrival

They came down into the village at mid-morning, and the village was already full of strangers.

Naledi saw the first of it from the last ridge above the houses: four figures on the footbridge, standing in a line, and behind them on the far bank a stack of long green cases lashed together on a hand-cart, and behind that, up the canyon track, the moving line of a party that had walked in the same way she had walked in, on foot, because no truck could make the canyon. She stood on the ridge and counted them without meaning to. Nine porters. Two men in clean shirts. One man in a canvas hat, at the front, standing still on the bridge and looking down at the water the way a person looks at a thing they intend to cross.

Toma stopped beside her and looked down and said nothing at all. His hand moved on the stick, found a better grip, and stayed there, and Naledi had been in the valley long enough now to read that as a sentence.

‘He is early,’ she said.

‘He is two weeks early,’ Toma said. ‘Which means he did not wait for the letter to arrive. Which means he left before it was sent. That is worth knowing about a man.’

They went down.

The strangers had been given the schoolroom, because the schoolroom was the only building in Sorrow's Gate with a floor that did not flood and a door that locked, and Naledi came to the door at noon and found it open and the room full and Basel Renn standing at the front of it with his hat off and a folded packet of paper in his hand, doing what he did.

He was in his fifties, broad through the shoulders, burnt along the arms. He had the face of a man who slept well. When he saw her in the doorway he stopped mid-sentence and crossed the room and put out his hand, and his handshake was exactly the right length, and his smile was exactly the right size, and he said her name before she had said his.

‘Dr Okonjo. It is genuinely good to meet you. I read the Kalu debacle papers. Some of them twice.’ He stepped back a pace and looked at her with frank, pleasant interest. ‘You look like you have been up the valley. Did you find it?’

‘Find what?’

‘The plant,’ Renn said, still smiling. ‘Everyone in the room has been telling me for an hour that there is a plant. Nobody has yet been able to tell me where it is. I had assumed that was reticence. Now I think it is simply that none of them know, and you do.’

The room was full. Baro was on the front bench with his hands on his knees. Nuru Banda was at the back with the fever list in her lap, and next to her Essa sat wrapped to the chin in a blanket, upright, watching the doorway with the flat careful attention of a child who has been ill long enough to be bored of it, and Naledi looked at her for three seconds and read what she could from across a room, which was not much: a child sitting up, which was better than yesterday, and a child with colour in the wrong places, which was not.

Renn saw where she was looking, and used it, and he did it so smoothly that later she could not find the seam.

‘Let us all be honest with each other,’ he said, turning back to the room, spreading the packet of papers on the teacher's table. ‘That is worth more than a morning of dancing. Baro and eleven households signed a letter two months ago asking my company to come and take a share of the medicine in this valley, and I have come, and I have brought what the letter asked for: a permit, a laboratory, a contract that pays the village for what leaves here, and men who know how to work in a wet place without getting killed in it. Nobody in this room has to decide anything today. I am not asking for a decision. I am asking to be shown the grove, so that my own people can see what is there, so that whatever anybody decides later is decided on facts and not on rumour.’ He let that sit for a moment. ‘That is all. That is the whole of my position, and I would be glad to hear yours.’

Baro's two grandchildren were on the list. Naledi had known that before she came into the room and she knew it now, and she read the set of Baro's jaw and the way he did not look at her, and understood that whatever she said in the next minute would have to be said past him.

‘You will not be shown the grove,’ she said.

Renn turned to her with an air of mild interest, as if a colleague had raised a technical point he was happy to take up.

‘I may not be. That is possible. But you have been up there, Dr Okonjo, and I am told you have been up there four days, and I am told you went with the healer, and I would like to hear what you found, because I have come a long way on a rumour, and you are the only person here who can turn it into something else either way.’ He unfolded the top sheet of the packet and laid it flat, and his voice stayed even and pleasant and did not rise a note. ‘The committee suspended you, I read. Three years of it. I am not here to rake that up and I do not judge it. But I would be lying if I said it did not matter what a village does with advice from a botanist who cannot collect, and I think you would rather I said it out loud in front of everyone than said it behind your back. So I am saying it out loud.’ He looked around the room, unhurried. ‘What I am asking the village is this. This is your valley and your medicine and your children. If you put this one question to yourself — whose word do I weigh with my daughter's life, the healer's or the letter's — you will find you already know the answer, and you will not need me to tell you what it is.’

‘That is not the question,’ Naledi said.

‘No?’

‘The question is whether the medicine can be taken at all. It cannot. Not the way you intend to take it. I have seen it and I have tested it and I have watched what happens when it is disturbed, and I will tell you what I told the council I would tell them, and I will tell it to you at the same time, because you have asked to hear it and because the village should hear me say it in front of you.’ She took the waxed papers out of her bag and set them on the table beside his contract, three leaves, one from each of three host trees, and the little red-brown stains on the paper where the acid had met the dried juice. ‘The plant is not a root you can dig up. The plant is a floor. It runs as a mat of root across the rock above the flood line, under a stand of host trees, and the mat is the plant, the whole of it, one living thing from one edge to the other. There are moths that live in the mat and come out at dusk, and the moths are the part that keeps the mat alive, and when the root is cut or lifted — even lifted, even for four seconds, even put back flat — the mat dies back at the cut within hours and the moths abandon it, and I know this because I did it, on the eastern edge, and I have seen the patch I made. Take one root and you do not lose one root. You lose a patch, and the patch widens, and the moths go, and when the moths go the mat has nothing left to keep it, and thirty years from now the grove is stone.’ She stopped, and then said the last of it plainly, because it was the part that mattered. ‘There is a compound in the leaves. Weaker than in the root, but usable, and it is present in the leaf of every tree in that stand. The leaf is the part the tree regrows every season, and the old families here took the leaf and never the root, and they took it in the season the tree could spare it, and the reason is not superstition, it is arithmetic. A medicine made from the leaf can be made again next year and the year after. A medicine made from the root can be made once, and after that the valley is a bare slope, and the next fever that comes through this canyon will have nothing at all to stop it, and there will be no one left who remembers that there was ever anything here but rock. I have come up the valley to brew the leaf tea for the sick children this afternoon. I can brew it this afternoon. I would rather brew it this afternoon than argue for another day, and I am telling you all of this now so that the arguing does not have to happen under a roof with a sick child in it.’

The room was quiet. Nuru Banda had her pencil out and was writing something in the margin of the fever list. Baro had not moved.

Renn looked at the three leaves on the table for a moment.

‘May I?’ he said, and picked one up without waiting, and turned it over with two fingers, and held it to the light from the doorway.

‘Weak, you say.’

‘Weaker than the root. Strong enough to be worth brewing, and I have set myself two days to prove it on the girl.’

‘Two days.’ Renn set the leaf down gently, exactly where it had been, and smoothed the waxed paper with the flat of his hand. ‘Two days is a long time in a fever, and I hear you, I do, and I want to say clearly that I am not dismissing you. But let me put the arithmetic the other way, because I am a practical man and practical arithmetic is most of my job. You tell me there is a compound in the leaves. You tell me the leaves will do it. You have tested three leaves with an acid drop in a paper bag, and I do not doubt your hands, but a company laboratory can test a thousand leaves with instruments, and a company laboratory can turn a compound in a leaf into a medicine that a nurse in a town clinic can keep in a cupboard for a year and dose by the grain. That is not theft, Dr Okonjo. That is not a saw. That is the difference between a tea brewed at one fire in one wet valley and a medicine that can reach a child in the next district and the one after that. My company will pay this village for the material. It will pay a share of everything made from that material, in perpetuity, on paper, at a rate you can read for yourself on this table. If the leaves are the thing, then I will take the leaves. If the leaves are weaker, then the village gets less, and the children get less, and that is simply how it is. But I will not promise you a laboratory on a two-day trial by a woman I met an hour ago, and I will not accept that there is only one way to hold a living thing and it is the way that keeps it in the ground. There is a third option in this room, and the third option is a middle one, and a middle one is what I came here to offer.’

‘What would the middle one look like?’ said Baro, from the bench, and Naledi heard the whole of the weight he was carrying in four words.

‘It would look like this, elder,’ Renn said, and laid the packet flat and turned it to face the room, and it was a map, a rough one, with the canyon at the bottom and the upper slope marked in a hand that was not his. ‘A site visit. Not a harvest. Three of my men and myself, up to the grove and back, to see the stand with our own eyes and take a hundred leaves from a hundred trees — one leaf each, the way the women who lived here took them, and I have read the old reports, Dr Okonjo, and I know they took one leaf and not the tree, and I am not proposing to cut anything on the first visit. A hundred leaves, one per tree, a week's work for a laboratory in the town, and if the leaf compound holds up, then we sit down and talk about what the village wants to do next, and if it does not hold up, then I have wasted a walk, and the village has lost nothing but the walk. That is my offer. I will put it in writing before your council this afternoon and sign it in front of everyone, and my men will not carry a saw up this valley on a permit that says leaf.’

He said it all easily, and it was not a lie. That was the trouble with it, and Naledi stood at the end of the table with her three leaves in waxed paper in front of her and felt the room turn first one way and then the other, and could not find the place in his offer where a hand had been placed on the scale.

‘One leaf from a hundred trees is a hundred trees touched,’ she said. ‘You will not take them clean. You will bring porters up a wet slope with sacks, and porters put their hands on a mat to steady themselves, and the mat is the plant, and I have been on it and I know what a boot does to it. A hundred leaves is a small thing. The road to reach them is not. And you have already asked me to show you the way to the grove, which is the one single thing I will not do, because the grove is not hard to reach once you have been shown, and there is no version of this in which a stranger is shown the grove in March and the grove is standing in June.’

‘Then walk up with us,’ Renn said. ‘Come and watch. Put your hands on every sack. Mark every tree we take a leaf from, and count them, and sign them off, and if we take a second leaf from any tree, stop us. I would rather have you with us than against us, and I will say so in front of the whole village. As a specialist, Dr Okonjo. Not as a scout. You would be there as what you are: the only botanist in this valley who knows what she is looking at.’

And there it was, and it was not a trap she could point at, and she heard how smooth it was and knew that everyone in the room had heard it too, and that half of the room had heard a reasonable man making a reasonable offer to a difficult woman, and that was the whole of what he had done.

Nuru Banda looked up from the list and said, ‘We have three children at forty degrees tonight. That is what I have to bring to this room. I do not care who signs what. I care which child I am sitting with at midnight, and I need to know now which one of you I am going to help.’

‘You will help me brew the tea this afternoon,’ Naledi said. ‘Both of us, in your clinic, with your scales, and we will measure the doses and you will write them down and watch them with me, and if it does not work in two days I will say so in this room and I will say it in front of this man.’

‘And the visit?’ Renn said politely.

‘The visit is not mine to refuse or permit. The valley is not mine. Ask the council. Ask them in the open, on the record, one question, and let it be a question with a number in it: how many children are sick tonight, and how many will be sick if the leaf holds and how many if it does not, and whether a leaf that works is worth a road that does not. I have given you my answer and I will give the council the same answer, and then the council decides, and whatever it decides I will help whoever is sick, because that is the only part of this that was ever my job in the first place.’

She gathered her papers. Renn did not stop her; he stepped aside from the table and let her come past, and he said, quietly, as she reached the doorway, out of the room's hearing but not out of hers:

‘I would have read your paper even if the panel had not. It was a good piece of work on a bad situation, and you were right about Kalu, and it cost you everything, and I know it, and I am sorry for it. I would have preferred to meet you in a laboratory, Dr Okonjo. I would have preferred you to have a permit in your pocket and a bench to argue from. But you do not, and I do, and so we will do it here on these terms and not on the terms I would have chosen, and I want you to hear me say that plainly so that neither of us has to pretend it is otherwise. I am not your enemy. I am the man with the permit. That is all I am.’

‘You are the man with the permit,’ she said, and went out into the wet street, and stood in the rain for a moment and breathed, because the room had been small and the morning long.

They brewed the leaf tea at the clinic that afternoon. There were two rings, the big one on the wall and the small one on the table, and Naledi weighed the dried leaf on Nuru's little brass scale and worked out a dose a quarter of the root dose she had planned in the notebook, and she brewed it in a clean pot with water that had been boiled and let stand, and she strained it, and she gave it to Essa at four o'clock in the afternoon, one cup, and then another three hours later, and she sat with her through the night and did not sleep, and the fever came down half a degree by midnight and half again by four, which was not much and was not nothing, and she wrote both numbers down in the clinic book with the time against them, in her own hand.

Nuru Banda wrote them too, in her own book, in the nurse's column, and when Naledi looked over and saw the two columns side by side, the same figures in two hands, she felt something loosen in her chest that had been tight since the canyon mouth.

‘You are keeping the record,’ she said. ‘Both of us at once.’

‘That is what a record is for,’ Nuru said. ‘So that on the fourth day, when somebody in this valley says it was your idea or mine, we can look at the page and neither of us has to be the one who remembers.’

By morning the village had chosen sides, and it had chosen them the way villages do, without a vote and without a fuss, by where people stood and where they did not.

The men who had put their names on Renn's letter stood about the schoolroom door, and their wives stood with them, and they were not hostile to Naledi and did not meet her eye. Baro's house was full that evening of eleven household heads and Baro himself, and the light in Baro's window stayed on late, and no one came and no one went. At the other end of the lane, the four families whose children were oldest on the sick list had moved their mats into Toma's yard without being asked, and every one of them had a sick child in the house, and every one of them had watched Essa drink the tea and had watched her sleep afterwards.

Nobody said the word. It sat over the village through the whole wet day, and over the schoolroom where Renn's two men wrote out his terms in a fair hand on the school's writing paper, and over the clinic where the second child was given tea at noon and the third at three, and by the end of the second day there were four children on the tea and two of them were cooling, and one of them, a boy of seven who had been the worst of them, sat up on the third morning and asked for water in an ordinary voice, and his mother went out of the clinic and stood in the lane and cried quietly against the wall, and then went back in and held the cup herself.

That afternoon Baro came down the lane with his stick and stood at the door of the clinic and would not come in, and Naledi went out to him and stood in the rain with him, because he would not come in.

‘The company man asked me a question last night,’ Baro said. ‘He asked me what happens in the dry season, when the fever is gone and the tea is dry in the pot and the children have gone back to school. And I could not answer him. There will be a dry season, doctor. There always is. And in the dry season there will be no child sick and no tea worth brewing and the leaves will be on the trees and nobody will remember that they mattered, except the man with the paper, who will remember, and who will still be standing at the gate with his permit and his porters and his road.’ He looked at the rain on the water for a while. ‘The healer says the valley keeps those who keep it. He has been saying it for fifty years. I have listened to him for fifty years and I am still not sure I know what he means, and I am not sure he knows either, and the trouble with that, doctor, is that I have grandchildren, and a sentence that a man cannot explain is not the same thing as a sentence that a man can read aloud to his council, and I have a council, and the council wants a number.’ He turned his stick in the wet ground. ‘The visit is tomorrow, if you say nothing to stop it. That is all I came to tell you. Do not answer me tonight. I would not know what to do if you did.’

He went back up the lane, and Naledi stood in the rain for a while, and then went back in and took the cup and gave the girl her tea.

She found Toma in the dark in his own doorway, sitting on the step with the lamp off, and she sat down on the step beside him because he did not move to make room and did not move away.

Down the lane the schoolroom had a light in it. Renn's men were still working, and after a while the door opened and two of the household heads came out and stood in the lane a moment, talking low, and then went up toward the council house, and the light in the schoolroom stayed on.

‘You said nothing this morning,’ Naledi said.

‘No.’

‘They would have listened to you. Half of them came here for you. You are the only person in this valley with the right to stand up in that room and tell them what you saw under that grove when you were fourteen. You have carried it fifty years. Baro asked you straight out whether the paper could be signed and you stood in a corner and said nothing, and the room heard you say nothing, and that silence was worth more to that man with the permit than a speech in his favour would have been. Do you know that?’

‘I know it.’ Toma was quiet for a long moment. In the lane, the last light went out in the schoolroom and the two shapes came out and went away up the lane in the dark. ‘I stood there this morning with the one sentence in me that would have finished it. And I did not say it, and I have been asking myself why for six hours.’ His stick tapped once on the step. ‘And I have found the answer, and it is not a good one. I did not say it because if I said it, and Baro signed nothing, and the fever went on the way it was going on before you came, then it would be my word that turned the paper away, and it would be my name that the mothers would say in the mornings over the graves, and I have spent fifty years carrying one thing and I have learned that I cannot carry two, and I am not ready to be the reason a valley had the cure at its door and did not take it. That is the whole of me, doctor, and it is not the river talking, it is the man. I gave you a leaf and a sentence and the moths, and I have nothing left to give that I have not already spent, and I am telling you now so that you do not go into that room tomorrow counting on me, because if I stand up and fail to speak again you must not read it as a change of heart. It will only be the same old man being the same old man, and it will cost the valley whatever it costs, and I will pay it with the rest of my life if it turns out that way.’

‘Then I will not go into the room counting on you,’ Naledi said. ‘But you will come to the room. And tomorrow, when Baro asks the council, you will not have to answer, and neither will I. Baro has asked for a number, and there is a number, and I have had it on the waterline for two days and have been too busy arguing to write it up.’ She took the notebook out of her coat and turned to the pages she had done the night before, the little plan of the valley floor with the flood line marked, the level she had taken at the footbridge, the list of days and the rain. ‘The fever started when the pool filled, in the week after the first rain. The standing water below the village is new; it is not the year before, and it is not the year before that, and I copied the water levels off Nuru's own list and off the marks on the bank, and it has been rising half a metre a season for three. Renn's clearing is on the lowest ground in the whole of the lower valley, and the water is coming back in the wet season, and when it comes back it will run through his clearing and out to the village footbridge, and not, if we clear the old stone channel above the grove, through the feet of the host trees. That is what I have. It is not the grove and it is not the paper. It is water, and it is where water goes, and it is something I can say in a room without one hand on the scale. I would rather bring the council a ditch than a leaf, Toma. A leaf is still a promise. A ditch is a place where the water will be next week whether anybody believes me or not, and a man can stand on the bank and look at it with his own eyes and then go home and decide what he thinks.’

Toma sat with that for a while in the dark.

‘You will need the council to lend you forty hands and a week,’ he said.

‘Yes.’

‘The council will not lend a week to a woman on a two-day trial and a piece of paper with a red drop on it. Baro will make you bring the water first, and the water will not be back until the next heavy rain, and the next heavy rain may be the flood itself, and then it may be too late for the daylight.’

‘Then I will have to bring the water early,’ Naledi said, ‘and I have to do it without asking for forty hands before I have it, and I have one place I can get it on a loan: the dry days between now and the rains, whatever they are, and the level taken with your own eyes on the bank, and the old channel cleared wide enough by two people that one heavy rain can find it. Mine and yours. If the water comes early, it will come down the channel we have opened. If the water comes late, we have lost a week of sleep and nothing else. The risk is that we clear it and the water does not come until after the flood, and I will have wasted the last week I might have spent on the tea. That is the trade. I am taking it. I want you to know that it is a trade and I am not pretending it is a sure thing.’ She put the notebook back. ‘Will you come up the channel with me in the morning? Before the visit. Before the room. Two people, one crowbar, the old stone channel above the grove, and the earliest I can take a level on the bank before anyone stands up in that room and says the word harvest out loud.’

Toma got up. He stood in his doorway with his stick in his two hands and looked down the lane at the dark schoolroom, and at the light that had gone out, and up at the ridge, at the low cloud lying on the grove and the rain that was not falling on the roof and the smell of the wet ground, and last of all at the clinic, where one soft lamp was still burning and a nurse was still sitting up with a girl of twelve who had had her tea two hours before.

‘I will come the first hour before light,’ he said, and went in, and shut the door.
