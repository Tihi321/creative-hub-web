The Wet Season Lake

The rain came in on the fourth night after the fire and did not leave.

It was not a storm. Naledi had waited for a storm, for a black line on the horizon and a wind to warn her, and instead the wet season settled in the way a guest settles into a house he means to stay in. It rained in the afternoon and stopped at dusk and began again before dawn, and the drops were small and close together and they got into everything. Her socks were wet on the second day and stayed wet. The notebook softened at the corners and she started carrying it inside her shirt, against her ribs, where the paper stayed dry and the pencil stayed warm enough to write.

By the second morning the riverbed below the village had gone.

She walked down to see it happen. There was still a channel, but it was not the channel she had measured on the way in — two walls of stone, a floor of rolled gravel, a stream you could step across. On the eighth day of rain the channel was full to the lower flood mark and moving the colour of weak tea, and by the ninth day it was hard against both walls and moving the colour of the soil it was carrying, and on the tenth morning the water came over the lip at the footbridge and lay a hand deep across the path.

She wrote two point five and stood in it, and felt the cold come through her boots to the bone.

The valley floor filled from the bottom up, which was the opposite of how she had expected it. She had thought of a flood as a thing that arrived — a wall coming down a channel, a wave with a front to it. This was not that. This was water standing, then spreading, then standing higher, like tea rising in a cup that someone was filling very slowly from below. The porous floor drank and drank and could not drink fast enough, and the swallow hole at the foot of the seep took what it could and then took no more, and the shafts in the limestone were full, all of them, and there was nowhere left for the water to go but out across the flat ground.

By the twelfth day the flat ground between the riverbed and the foot of the upper slope was a lake.

It was shallow. That was the thing she had to keep saying to people, because shallow did not mean harmless. In the middle the lake was a metre deep on the line she took with the pole, and at the edges it was knee deep, and at the very edge it was a skin of water over grass with the grass still standing up through it, which was the part the children liked until their mothers called them in.

The footpath to the clinic went under on the eleventh day.

Nuru Banda had moved the sick before the path went under, because Nuru had lived in the valley all her life and knew the order of things. She took the four worst children up the slope to the schoolroom, which sat on the only piece of ground in the lower village that stayed above the waterline, and she carried Essa up herself on her back with a blanket over both of them and the rain coming down, and Toma walked behind her with the tea and the spoons and did not offer to carry the child, because he was eighty-one and knew what he could and could not do.

The schoolroom became the ward. It had a stove, a concrete floor, four windows and a roof that leaked in one corner, and by the fourteenth day there were eleven children in it, and by the eighteenth day there were seventeen.

The fever had found the new water.

Naledi stood at the window with the list in her hand and worked it out the way she had worked out the first one. The first cases had come with the first standing water in the week after the first rain. The new cases were coming now, three weeks into the wet season, in the households nearest the lake, the ones whose doors opened onto the shallow water and whose children waded in it to get from one lane to the next. The houses on the rising ground, twenty paces higher, had no new cases at all. It was the same sentence as before with a new set of numbers under it, and she could see it now from the schoolroom window without a notebook: the water was the road and the water was the house.

‘It is the same,’ she said to Toma. ‘It came from the lake the first time and it has come from the lake again, and both times it came to the houses standing in it.’

‘Then we have been living in it,’ Toma said, ‘for three weeks.’

‘You have been living in it for three weeks and you did not tell me.’

‘The healer does not tell the rain to go away,’ he said. ‘The healer tells the people to move the bed up the hill. That is what my mother did and her mother did.’ He looked out at the water, which had now reached the third step of the shop. ‘It has not been this high in my own lifetime. My grandfather’s lifetime, yes.’

Naledi put the list down.

‘How high did it go in his lifetime?’

‘To the shop door,’ he said. ‘In the bad year, the year after the roots. And then down again.’

The shop door was a hand higher than the third step. She wrote the number in the margin of the list and underlined it once.

She spent the next two days walking the waterline, and they were two of the longest days she had ever worked, because walking a waterline is nothing but counting.

She started at the footbridge at first light with a stick cut to one metre and notched at every ten centimetres, and she walked the shore of the lake anticlockwise, taking a reading at every place where the shape of the ground changed. A stone, a wall, a tree root across the path: each of those things made the water stand up or run faster, and each of them was a fact she could put in the notebook with a time against it.

The water was not rising at the same speed everywhere. That was the first thing she found, and it was the thing that mattered. On the eastern side of the lake, the side under the lower slope, the water was rising about a hand a day. On the western side, where the old channel ran down from the shelf, it was rising a hand a day as well and holding there — steady, patient, predictable.

But in the north lane, the ground between the shop and the first houses, the water had risen a hand and a half between her morning round and her evening round. She went back and did it again the next morning and got the same answer, and then she stood in the lane for a while with the stick in her hand and worked out why.

The north lane had been the old road down to the riverbed. It was lower than the ground on either side of it, a shallow groove worn into the valley floor by two hundred years of feet and wheels, and the water was filling the groove the way water fills a gutter: not by rising, but by running. The lake was a body. The lane was a channel. The channel was carrying the water into the middle of the village faster than the village could drain it, and the village could not drain it at all, because the village drains were the porous floor, and the porous floor was full.

She measured the lane from end to end: a hundred and forty paces, from the waterline at its southern end to the pond that had formed where the groove met the lowest house garden. The pond was the third step of the shop, and the shop was where Nuru kept the medicines that were not in the schoolroom.

At the top of the lane, where the groove met the rising ground, she found the inlet. She stood and looked at it for a long time.

It was a gap in the old wall, half a metre wide, and through it a thin brown stream was moving from the lake into the lane at a pace you could see with your eyes if you watched the floating leaf-scraps. It was not a torrent. It did not need to be. It was a tap left open, and it had been open for three weeks, and behind it was a lake.

‘Nuru,’ she called. ‘Come and look at this.’

Nuru came down through the water in her boots and stood beside her and looked at the gap.

‘It was always there,’ Nuru said. ‘That is where the road went down to the river. It is the old crossing.’

‘It is the old crossing and it is now an open door into your village, and there is a lake behind the door.’

Nuru looked at it for a moment longer and then looked at Naledi.

‘Can you shut a door on a lake?’

‘No,’ said Naledi. ‘But you can take the door off its hinges.’

She did not explain, because she did not yet have the words. What she had was a direction, and a day and a half, and the knowledge that the water in the north lane was rising a hand and a half a day while the water on the western side was standing still, and that a difference like that is never an accident. Water does not rise faster in one place than another unless something is leading it there.

She walked west along the shore.

The western shore was the quiet side, and the quiet side was where the old works were.

She had noticed them on the way in, on the first day, and had not understood them: a line of cut stone, waist high, running down the western side of the valley from the foot of the shelf to the flat ground, half buried in the grass and the low scrub. She had taken it for a boundary wall. It was not a boundary wall. On the second afternoon of her survey she followed it with the stick and found that it was a channel, and that it had a floor.

It was built of dressed stone, laid dry, two courses deep, about eighty centimetres across at the top and narrowing to a flat bed a spade wide. Somebody had cut it into the slope with a great deal of work and no machinery, and had laid it so that it ran from the shelf down to the flat, and it had a fall on it you could feel with your hand on the floor stones, a steady patient fall the whole way. She walked its whole length with the stick and counted the stones and did not stop to write until she reached the bottom, and then she sat down on the bank with the water at her feet and opened the notebook and wrote this:

Built channel, west side, from the shelf contact to the flat. Dressed stone, dry laid, floor clean and level. Fall over the whole run: approx 4 m over approx 400 m. Much older than anything else in the valley. Half choked with brush and gravel in three places. Where it runs out at the bottom, it discharges into the old western pool bed, which discharges into the flat.

This is not a wall. This is a waterway. Somebody made the water go this way on purpose and kept it going this way for a long time.

She sat there and read her own writing and did the rest of the sum in her head, and the rest of the sum was this: if the west channel were cleared, the water coming down the western side of the valley would run down it in an orderly way, at four metres of fall over four hundred, instead of spreading out and seeping and making the west lake edge, and it would arrive at the bottom on the flat ground — and the flat ground at the bottom of the western side was Renn’s clearing.

She closed the notebook. She did not write that down yet. She was not ready to write that down.

She stood up and walked back up the channel and did the thing she had known she would have to do before she would believe it, which was to go and look at the water coming into the top of the channel in the first place.

The top of the channel was at the foot of the western slope, just below the shelf, a hundred paces east of where the burn had stopped at her trench.

The water came into it from the west fork. She had heard the west fork a week ago from the trench, when it was a trickle, and she had heard it from the shelf in the wet night and thought it was rain. Now she stood above it and looked at it properly for the first time, and it was not a trickle.

The west fork was a gully, three metres wide, cut into the western slope below the burnt ground, and it was running full. The burnt slope above it had lost its litter in the fire, and the rain on bare burnt ground does not soak in and does not slow down; it sheets, and it runs, and it collects in whatever low line it finds. The line it found was the west fork, and the west fork was running brown and steady at a pace Naledi could hear from ten paces away, and it was running into the top of the old stone channel.

Part of it. Not all of it. The channel was half choked with brush and gravel and the water was going over the sides and down the west lake edge and into the flat the long way, the way it had been going since the channel was abandoned.

But it was going into the channel too. Two-thirds of the west fork was already in it. Somewhere under the brush there was still a floor, and the water had found it.

Naledi knelt down at the top of the channel with her stick and took a reading and did not believe it, and took another one, and then sat back on her heels in the wet and looked down the long line of cut stone going away from her toward the flat ground, and understood what she was looking at.

Somebody had built this. Two hundred years ago, or four hundred, in the time of the four families or before, someone had sat where she was sitting and had understood the same thing she understood now: that the western side of the valley sheds more water than any other side, and that if you give that water a road it will take it, and if you do not give it a road it will make its own way onto the flat ground and into the grove.

The channel was not a boundary or a drain or a ruin. It was a decision, made a long time ago by people who were no longer here, and it had been kept clear for a long time, and then it had not been kept clear, and the grove had gone into decline, and the fever had come, and nobody in the village had known why.

She stood up in the rain and put the notebook inside her shirt and walked down the channel to the flat ground, and at the bottom she turned and looked back up the four hundred metres of cut stone, and then she looked across the flat ground to Renn’s clearing.

The clearing sat directly in the bottom of the western side of the valley, on the lowest ground between the foot of the channel and the lake. His road came into it from the south. His tent and his two tarpaulins and his stacked timber and his porters’ cook fire and all nine porters and two men and Basel Renn himself were sitting on the exact piece of ground where the old channel had been built to put the western water.

She stood in the rain and looked at that for a long moment, and then she went up to the schoolroom, because the light was going and there was a child in there who had stopped calling her grandfather by name.

Essa had been strange for two days before she got worse.

Naledi noticed it first at the water. She came into the schoolroom in the wet evening and stood dripping by the stove, and Essa looked up from the mattress and said, quite clearly, ‘The water is in the shop now.’

‘Yes,’ Naledi said. ‘It is at the third step.’

‘No,’ said Essa. ‘It is in the shop.’ And she looked at Naledi with the flat patient look of a person correcting a slow child, and then went back to the ceiling.

On the first night she stopped answering when the nurse called her, and turned her face to the wall when anyone stood over her in the lamp light, as though the standing person were a draught she could shut out. On the second day she began to talk to the ceiling in a low steady voice about the moths, as though they were on it, and she asked for the door to be opened and then asked for it to be shut, and could not say which she wanted.

On the second evening Naledi took her temperature and held the thermometer up to the lamp and read 40.4.

Toma came in at dusk with the tea and sat down on the floor beside the mattress and put his hand on the child’s head, the way he had measured Emeka’s fever that first week with nothing but his palm, and he said her name.

Essa turned her head toward the sound. Her eyes found him and went past him and settled on the wall behind his shoulder, and she said something in a small polite voice that was not addressed to any of them, and turned her face back to the ceiling.

Toma took his hand off her head and sat still.

‘She does not know me,’ he said.

‘Her fever is very high,’ Naledi said. ‘At that temperature people do not know anybody. It is not—’

‘She does not know me,’ Toma said again, in the same voice he used for numbers and for the names of plants, and he got up and went to the other side of the room and sat down against the wall with his back straight and his hands on his knees and did not speak to anyone for the rest of the evening.

Naledi stayed with Essa through the night. She sponged her face and the back of her neck with water from the filter jug, and she dosed her with the tea every three hours, and she watched the rash across her chest, which had been faint on the first day and was now a fine dark red under the collarbone, and she wrote the times and the doses and the temperatures in the notebook and did not sleep.

At four in the morning Essa’s temperature was 40.6. At five it was 40.4. At half past five she said, clearly, ‘Papa, the pot is boiling,’ and reached out and took hold of Naledi’s sleeve and held it, and went on holding it until the light came up.

Naledi did not move her arm.

When the light was full, Toma came back in from where he had been sitting on the step, and Essa opened her eyes and looked at him and closed them again, and her hand on Naledi’s sleeve went loose.

‘She held my sleeve,’ Naledi said.

‘Yes,’ said Toma.

‘That is not nothing.’

‘No,’ he said. ‘It is not nothing.’ He looked at the child and then at the window, where the rain was coming down out of a pale grey sky onto a lake that had reached the fourth step of the shop. ‘But it is not a way to keep her, either. She needs the water out of the village, Doctor. The tea keeps her where she is. It does not move her back.’

‘I know.’

‘Then tell me,’ Toma said, ‘what you have been walking around for two days, with your notebook inside your shirt, and not saying.’

She told him that evening, in the schoolroom, with Nuru and Baro and six other household heads standing round the stove, and she drew it in pencil on the back of the list because there was no other paper dry in the village.

She drew the valley floor. She drew the lake and the north lane and the shop. She drew the western slope, and the burnt ground above the west fork, and the west fork running down. She drew Renn’s clearing at the bottom, and the tent, and the timber. Then she drew the old stone channel from the top of the west fork to the foot of the slope, and she put her pencil on it and left it there.

‘This is a waterway,’ she said. ‘It was built to take the western water down the western side and out onto the low ground, and it was built with a fall on it, so the water goes fast and does not stand. It has been choked for two generations and it is half working already, on its own, without anybody clearing it. Today two-thirds of the west fork is going down this. The rest is going over the sides and down the west lake edge and into the flat in front of the grove.’

‘And if we clear it?’ Baro said.

‘Then all of the west fork goes down it. All of it, fast, in a channel, and it comes out at the bottom here, on the low ground, where the flat already lies open. And the west lake edge stops rising, because the water that was spreading over it has somewhere better to go, and the north lane stops filling, because the lake behind it stops growing, and the path back to the clinic comes out of the water two days earlier than it would on its own.’

‘And it floods Renn’s clearing,’ Baro said.

‘Renn’s clearing is on the lowest ground between the two. I measured it this afternoon from the bank, twice. The water will go there because it is already going there. It is going there now, slowly, over the sides, the long way. I am not offering to send it. I am offering to stop pretending it is not coming. It will come whether we clear the channel or not. The only difference the channel makes is how soon, and how fast, and whether the west lake edge and the north lane and the clinic path pay for it first.’ She put the pencil down on the drawing. ‘When I opened the last channel I brought you a measurement and I asked you to stand on the bank and look at the water with your own eyes. I am asking the same thing now. The western water is already in that old stone cut, halfway. Come and look at it tomorrow and see for yourselves. If I am wrong about the fall, you will see it in the first twenty minutes of the walk and you can send me home.’

Nobody said anything for a moment. The stove ticked. One of the children coughed in the corner, and Nuru went to her without being asked.

Baro looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he looked up, and he said, in the loud plain voice he used when he was about to try to buy something cheaply:

‘And what is on Renn’s clearing?’

‘His tent. His two tarpaulins. His stacked timber. His road. His cook fire. His nine porters, his two men, and himself.’

‘And the village signed his paper.’

‘Fifteen households signed his paper,’ Naledi said. ‘The paper is in the schoolroom and I have read it. It grants his company the right to take plant material from the basin. It does not grant his company the right to stand on the lowest ground in the valley in a wet season. Nobody in this room sold him a dry bed. He chose the ground himself, because a clearing is easy to reach from the road, and the road is easy to build from the south. That is his arithmetic and it was his to make. Mine is the water, and the water has no paper in it at all, and the water is going to go somewhere, and the only question in front of me is which ground it goes over first, and I have walked both of them, and I am telling you what I measured.’

Toma was sitting against the wall where he had sat all evening, and he had not yet spoken. Baro turned to him, because Baro always turned to him last and Baro always knew what the answer would be and asked anyway.

‘Toma. You have been quiet.’

‘The water is going to go somewhere,’ Toma said. ‘That is true. And the one who says where it goes is the one who cleared the ditch.’ He looked at Naledi and then at Baro. ‘She cleared the last one herself, before the council. She did not ask us to take her word for it. She asked us to stand on the bank and look. I stood on the bank and looked and the water went where she said it would go, and I have not forgotten it.’ He stood up, slowly, with one hand on the wall. ‘My grandfather pulled the roots out of the ground and sold them to a man with a paper, because he believed he was trading and not wrong. I have carried that for fifty years and I have decided badly with it. This time the ditch is already cut in the rock. Somebody with better manners than my grandfather cut it a long time ago and left it for whoever came next, and it is still there, and it still goes the same way it went.’ He picked up his cans. ‘The valley keeps the ones who keep it. That is the rule. It is not a proverb, it is a fact about water.’

Baro looked at the drawing for another moment. Then he blew out the lamp nearest the stove and said, ‘I will call them for first light,’ and went out into the rain, and Naledi heard him in the lane calling the households by their full names, one after another, all the way down to the water.

She stayed by the stove for a while, with the drawing in front of her and Essa’s hand not on her sleeve any more. She knew what she had just done and she knew what it would cost, and she was not going to pretend, in a warm room with a stove in it, that she did not know.

There was a man asleep in a tent on the low ground with nine porters and two men and a broken-footed foreman, and he had a signed paper in his pocket that said the valley was his to take from, and in the morning the water was going to come up to his feet and he would not know why.

She had said none of that out loud, and she turned it over now and found it was not the same as hiding it. She had told the council the part they could check: the branch that the old cut was already taking, the fall on the stones, the level of the lake at the western edge, the height of the flat at the bottom. She had not stood in the schoolroom and said the word clearing, because the word clearing was not a measurement. It was a verdict. And a verdict spoken before the water arrives is a thing people argue with, and the water does not argue, and she had learned in three years of forms and locked doors that the argument is the enemy of the fact. What she had not said was this: that she had walked the shore of the lake for two days looking for a way to save the grove, and had found one, in a two-hundred-year-old ditch, and the ditch emptied onto the one piece of low ground in the whole lower valley where a man was sleeping. She would say it. She would say it in the morning, standing on the bank with Baro and the households and their eyes on the stones, when the branch could be seen doing it on its own. That was the only order of telling she could live with. It was also the order that happened to keep the ditch from turning into a quarrel about Renn before anyone had seen the water take it. She was not sure those were two different things, and she was not going to sort it out at five in the morning with a child asleep at her elbow. She put the pencil in the notebook and the notebook inside her shirt and went and sat with Essa until the light came.

Outside, the lake had reached the fifth step of the shop, and the rain was small and close and steady, and the whole valley was breathing water.

They went up at first light, and the rain held off while they walked, and that was the only piece of luck in the whole morning.

Eleven household heads came, and Nuru came, and Baro, and Toma, and two of the boys who worked the shop, and they stood on the western bank at the foot of the old cut and looked at the west fork coming down the burnt ground and going into the stones.

Naledi did not talk for the first ten minutes. She let them look. She had learned that in this valley a person who talks at a thing is selling it, and a person who stands beside it and says nothing is showing it. So they stood in the wet and watched the brown water lean into the half-choked cut and run down the stones, and each of them found the floor of it with their own eyes, and each of them saw the branch go, and then they walked down the cut the whole four hundred metres with the fall of it under their boots.

At the bottom Baro stood on the bank and looked across at Renn’s clearing, with the tent and the tarpaulins and the stacked timber, and stood there a long time, and said nothing in front of everybody.

Then Ruth, who had worked the fire line, said, ‘The north lane is up the third step this morning,’ and one of the men said his door had been dry at the sill, and Naledi took out the notebook and read them back their own houses against the waterline she had walked the day before, and the arithmetic arrived in the middle of them like a person walking into a room.

‘The cut is half working without us,’ she said. ‘One morning with thirty hoes and it works the whole way. I am not asking this village to send water into a man’s camp. I am asking this village to choose the side the water goes down, and to choose it now, while there is still a side to choose, and to do it the way we did the last one, by looking at the ground and not at a paper.’ She put the notebook away. ‘If you will not clear it, say so, and I will go back to the schoolroom and dose the children and we will do what we can. If you will clear it, we should start this hour, because the west fork is rising while we stand here and the lane is rising with it, and the path to the clinic went under three days ago and I would like it back before the next child needs to come down it.’

Baro looked at the clearing a while longer. Then he turned round to the eleven.

‘Hands up,’ he said, in the flat voice he used for figures. ‘Who says clear it.’

Thirteen hands went up, counting Nuru’s, counting Toma’s, counting Baro’s own, which he raised last.

‘Who says no.’

Nobody put a hand up at all.

Baro held his hand in the air a moment, looking at it as though it belonged to someone else.

‘Then it is not a vote,’ he said. ‘It is a yes and no argument at all. I will not dress it up as one.’ He turned to Naledi. ‘Doctor, you will stand on the bank and tell them where to cut, and I will carry water in the two cans, and Toma will walk with the ones who go in at the bottom, and when it is open we will all of us stand on the bank and watch where it goes and nobody will say afterwards that they did not know.’
