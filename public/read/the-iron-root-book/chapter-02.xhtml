The Copper Air

They went in at first light, and Toma did not wait for her to be ready. When she came down to the water he was already standing in it, ankle deep, stick in his hand, watching the far wall of the canyon the way a man watches a door.

The canyon floor was not a path. It was a stream bed that had been pressed into service as one: cobbles, gravel, brown water clear enough to see the pale stone underneath. The walls went up on either side, cut clean, thirty metres at the opening and narrowing as they walked. Twice her pack strap caught on an overhang and Toma, ahead of her, did not turn round. He knew by the sound of it.

He stopped. She stopped. He went on.

She checked the time at the first stop, and at the second, and at the third. Eleven minutes, then nine, then eleven. After the fourth she understood and put the watch away: he was not resting. He was listening, and every place he stopped was a place where the sound of the water changed.

The walls carried two lines. The first was a copper stain about her own height above the water, running level all along both sides, dark red-brown against grey. Above it, fainter, was a second line, higher by the length of her arm and a hand.

At the next stop she took out the string, knotted at metre intervals, tied a stone to one end, and measured both.

‘Two point four,’ she said. ‘And three point one above the present level.’

Toma watched the stone swing.

‘Which flood was this one?’ she said, touching the lower line.

‘A wet season.’

‘Which wet season?’

‘Any of them.’

She wrote both figures down and the date and the exact place, in a waterproof notebook with a pencil, because it was wet and because pencil did not run. Then she wrote, below, ask again later.

The canyon narrowed until it was shoulder-width at the base, and there they had to take off their packs and pass them through ahead of themselves, one at a time, Toma first, then her. The rock was cold on her hands, and cold was a measurement too, and she made herself notice it rather than minding it. When they came out the other side the floor widened again and the water was knee deep and moving, and the cold came up through her boots and took the breath out of her for two steps.

Above them, on a ledge, a bird the size of a small dog sat still. Black, with a heavy beak and a casque like a folded wing on top of it. It made a sound she had not heard before: a wooden knock, twice, then a low hinge creak. It watched them the way a man on a wall watches a road.

Toma did not slow. She did not slow either, and she kept her eyes on her feet, and she counted forty paces past the ledge before she looked up again. She wrote the bird down at the next stop: nest ledge, left wall, forty metres above floor, casque, black, one bird. Position noted.

Her GPS found nothing. She held it up, walked ten paces, held it up again, and read the same flat message. The canyon walls were a roof without a sky, and the satellites could not see into it. She wrote no fix in the notebook, put the unit away, and paced instead, counting steps and multiplying by the length of her stride, which she had measured on a football field in the city against a real tape, eleven years ago, and trusted, because trust had to start somewhere.

The smell arrived before the valley did. Copper first, thin and cold, like the air off a struck match, and then underneath it the green, rising crushed and wet, and then a third thing she could not name, mineral and turned over, like soil after a spade.

Light came down the canyon in a single slab and stopped, and beyond it was mist.

They came out.

The walls fell back on both sides into low bluffs, thick with moss and dripping, and the valley opened in front of her, and Naledi stood still, because a survey began with ten minutes of looking and she had learned that lesson twice with her eyes shut and once with a broken ankle.

It was wet. Not raining. Wet. Every surface held a film of water. The mist was not a weather event in that valley; it was the medium the place was kept in, moving slowly from the upper slope down toward the floor, thin enough to see fifty metres into, thick enough to hide the rest. Twenty metres up the opposite bluff a stream fell out of the mist and simply stopped, landing in a mossed shelf and going on without any sound.

Roots ran everywhere. That was the thing her eye kept returning to. They came out of the rock at knee height and crossed over it like cables, grey-brown and smooth, some as thick as her wrist, some as thick as her thigh, and where two of them met they did not cross one another; they joined, thickened, and went on. Above her the mist hid where they came from.

Below and behind her, back along the canyon, she could see two threads of smoke, thin and straight, standing in the air over the outer valley where Sorrow’s Gate sat. Eleven sick, four of them under six, one buried on the ninth day. She took out the pencil.

‘Where do you want to start?’ Toma said.

‘At the bottom, on the water, and work up until the light goes.’

‘Then we start there.’ He pointed his stick at a shelf of flat rock above a seep. ‘Sit when you are tired. You will not know when you are tired in this air. Nobody does.’

She measured thirty metres of knotted string up the slope from the seep, at right angles to the water. She drove a peg at every tenth knot and tied a strip of flagging tape to each. Orange flags in a green valley, so that a person who came back a year later could stand on the same ground and mean the same thing by here.

Then she worked the line.

At each station she did the same six things: air temperature at her own shoulder, soil temperature at five centimetres, the aspect the moss grew on, the water within a step, the leaf litter or its absence, and one sentence in the notebook that was not a number.

The numbers did something she had not expected.

Under the first rock overhang the soil read two degrees warmer than the air. Ten metres away, in the open, it read colder than the air by half a degree. In the hollow on the valley floor, where the ground dipped for three metres and rose again, the air sat four degrees colder than the ridge of the dip, and smelled of nothing, which was its own piece of information: no green, no copper, only cold hanging in a bowl like water.

‘The cold lies down there at night,’ Toma said, when she asked him. ‘The children know. They play on the ridges in the evening and never in the hollows.’

She wrote that down as a fact she had been given, and marked it with a small circle, which in her notebook meant not measured.

At the fifth station she poured a full bottle of water onto a flat patch of ground and started counting. It was gone in under a minute, without a puddle, without a sheen, straight down through pale crumbly soil into broken rock, and the sound of it going was a soft swallowing that went on a moment after the water had stopped.

‘The floor drinks,’ she said.

‘In the dry months it drinks everything,’ Toma said. ‘In the wet months it is full and it stands. You will see it. It has done it every year I have lived, and it will do it this year, and the water will stand on the ground and the paths will be gone, and that is all the valley ever promises.’

She wrote: porous floor, high infiltration, drains to depth. Sink capacity unknown. Question: what happens when the sink capacity is exceeded? Below it, because she had promised herself honesty in this particular notebook: Answer, per T., known locally: it floods.

Then she sat back on her heels and looked at her hands, which were shaking slightly from cold, and at the six stations behind her, and at the slope above, where the mist had thinned enough to show a stand of trees with pale trunks and no visible ground beneath them. She raised the lens.

‘Not today,’ Toma said.

‘I only want the leaf.’

‘The light is going. You will want it more tomorrow anyway. It will still be there tomorrow.’ He said it as though reassuring her. It took her a moment to hear what he had not said.

In the afternoon she went up the seep line, because a seep is a place where two things meet and wherever two things meet there is a boundary, and boundaries are what a survey is for.

The seep came out along the contact of the hard grey limestone and a band of sticky orange clay, and the water was not seeping so much as arriving, all along ten metres of slope, cold and clear, in a hundred small beads of movement. She put her hand in. It held a steady warmth, staying roughly constant year-round as the water flowed through the limestone.

She stood up, and looked at a low green mat of growth on the wet rock beside the seep, and said, because it was where her eye had gone, ‘Selaginella.’

Toma came over. He did not correct her with words. He knelt down, which took him a while, and turned one of the tiny stems over with his thumb and held it up so that she could see the underside and the little pale dust gathered in the angle of the leaves.

‘It has no seed,’ he said. ‘Look how it makes its dust. If you name a thing a fern and let a village pay for a fern, you will be wrong in a way that costs money. What does your book call that?’

She looked, and looked again, and crossed the name out with a single line and wrote beneath it lycophyte — spores, not seed, and then, because he was waiting, she wrote his two-syllable word in the margin and the meaning he gave her for it, which was the one that keeps the rain.

‘Because it holds it,’ he said. ‘Not because it likes it. Hold your hand on that mat and it will give you back a mouthful. When the clay above is bare, that mat is why the seep reaches the village spring in the dry month. Now you have a name from your book and a name from here. Which one will you write in your letter?’

‘Both,’ Naledi said. ‘The book name goes in the report. Yours goes under it, so that whoever reads the report can find the plant again in this valley and not in another one.’

He looked at her for slightly longer than the answer required, and then he got up, using the stick, and moved off along the contact.

Twenty metres further on she stopped hearing the water properly. The seep was still there, still arriving along the clay, but the sound had changed, and it took her a moment to understand the change: part of the sound had gone down. She walked to the place, and there it was — a hole in the floor of the valley the size of a bucket mouth, edged with moss, water going into it without hurry and without noise except a low note, like a finger drawn round the rim of a glass.

She knelt and dropped a leaf in. It turned once and was gone. She dropped a stick in and counted; she had counted to three when the stick went end-up and vanished.

She wrote: swallow hole, floor, position flagged. Carries the seep. Capacity unknown.

‘How many of these are there?’ she asked him.

‘More than anybody has counted.’

‘Have you counted them?’

‘I am counting them now,’ Toma said. ‘Because you are asking.’

She wrote that down too, in the way she wrote down the temperature of the air: because it had happened.

They camped in the lee of a low bluff where a shelf of dry rock gave them somewhere to sit, and she cooked rice on the small stove while he ate cold bread and watched her write. The light had gone off the valley like water out of a bath. Six pages. She counted them twice, because counting was free.

‘And what is it, then?’ he said.

‘I don’t know.’

He waited.

‘I know a great deal about this valley and nothing about the fever,’ she said. ‘The water in the seep is cold and clear. The water in the canyon is clear. There is no smell of sewage at the seep, no rubbish, no dead animal. The moss is healthy. The ground is cold and the mist is constant and everything I have measured today is the same as the day before it, in the same season, in a place nobody has disturbed. The fever started when the rains came. That is the only certain thing I have, and it is not mine, it is the nurse’s, and it is a sentence in a letter.’

She opened the notebook at the back, where she kept the short list.

‘Three roads,’ she said. ‘Something in the water. Something that bites or flies. Something carried between people. If it were carried between people, the ones caring for the sick would be worst, and eleven sick in a village of that size should show me a pattern. I need the names, the ages and the days they fell ill, in order. I need to know which houses, and where those houses draw water, and where the children were playing ten days before the first one went down. I need to see the sick. That is tomorrow. It should have been today.’

Toma put the last of the bread in his mouth and chewed.

‘You measured the ground all day and said the valley is that way,’ he said. ‘The people are not the ground. You can measure a fever with a string if you know which end to hold. You have been holding the wrong end, and you know it, and you came up here anyway, because up here is where the answer that interests you lives.’

‘The answer that interests me is the one that works,’ she said.

‘Then hold the other end.’ He wiped his hands on his coat. ‘You looked at that mat of green and called it a fern and it nearly went into the letter that way. Today you learned one plant. Tomorrow, when you ask the mothers, you will learn eleven. Ask them the way you asked me. Not what is wrong. What happened.’

She wrote, on the sixth page, under everything else: Not enough. Tomorrow: the people. And under that, because she was tired and because it was true: Attention is also a method.

Then she closed the pencil into the notebook and lay down on her side of the shelf with her boots on and her pack behind her head, and lay listening to the valley, which was all small water and no wind, and did not sound like anywhere she had been before.

She was still awake when Toma sat up.

He did it without a sound, which was more frightening than a shout. One moment he was a heap of coat on the far end of the shelf; the next he was upright with his stick across his knees, facing back down the valley, toward the canyon and the gate.

Away below, small and yellow, a light moved. It came out of the canyon slowly, swinging, bobbing over stones, the way a lantern moves when the person carrying it is walking and crying at the same time.

Toma stood up.

‘That is a boy from the village,’ he said, ‘and he is running, and he knows this ground in the dark better than you know your own house. Sit still and let him come.’

Naledi sat still. She reached for the pencil, and then took her hand back, because a thing that was not yet a fact did not go in the notebook, and whatever the boy had come to say was still falling through the dark toward her, unmeasured, with the water in the black ground still going down where she could not follow it.
