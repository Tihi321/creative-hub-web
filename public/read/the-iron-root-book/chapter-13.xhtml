The Hardening Off

The water went down slowly, the way it had come up, and it left behind a valley that smelled of wet ash and turned earth and the copper smell that never left. Naledi walked the shoreline every morning with the notebook open and wrote the level on the wall of the shop in chalk, a hand's width lower each day, until the flat ground was mud again and the old stone channel ran clear and quiet and the western branch was back inside its banks. The schoolroom emptied. The pallets went back to the houses they had come from. Nuru Banda took the last two children home herself and put the clinic book on its shelf and said, in the voice she used for facts, that she had never seen a fever run out of children so fast and that she did not intend to see it again.

Then there was the matter of what Naledi had come for, which was not the fever any more, because the fever was answered, but the plant, because the plant was the answer to the next fever and the one after that, and there was one grove of it on one slope and no reason at all to believe it could live anywhere else.

She had told the council the sentence in the spring and had meant it as a sentence about not taking. She had not yet found out whether it was also a sentence about giving.

She started the plot on the second dry week, on the terrace below the grove where the old houses had stood, because it was above the flood line and below the mist and had four courses of the old wall along its lower edge to hold the soil.

Essa came with her, because Essa had asked to, in the flat way she asked for things now, and because Naledi had learned that a girl of nearly thirteen with a record book was worth two adults with hoes. They paced the terrace out with string and marked six rows with pegs. They cut poles from the scrub on the burnt slope and lashed a frame over the first two rows, palm thatch on top and open at the sides, because the seedlings were coming out of a room and the sun on the terrace was twice what the sun under the host trees had been.

"You are building them a roof," Essa said.

"I am building them the shade they were born in."

"The roof is the shade."

"The roof is the shade and the sides are the air and the timing is the rest of it."

Essa wrote that down in her own book, which had a cover of oiled cloth and a pencil on a string, and put the pencil back in her hair, and Naledi did not correct her spelling, because that was a thing for another year.

The lifting began on the fourth morning, early, before the mist had gone off the slope, because Toma had said the plant would be cooler then and Naledi had agreed with him and had written it as a note before he said it. They took sixteen seedlings — not the biggest, not the oldest, but the young ones no higher than a knee, the ones with a single clean stem and a good fan of root into the mat. Naledi lifted each one with a spade pushed straight down in a square a forearm wide and a forearm deep, and lifted it with the ball of mat and rock and root intact, and carried it on a board with Essa's hands steady on the far corner, and set it in the row and firmed the ground around it and watered it with the watering can Toma had lent them, and wrote the number of the plant and the time of the lifting in the book, every time, so that the record would be worth something whatever happened.

Eight of them died.

They died in the way the notes had promised they would, the way Naledi had told the council they would in the schoolroom the night Baro signed the paper that was now in the box with the other papers. The shade was too much for two of them and the leaves yellowed under it. The shade was lifted too fast on three, an hour a week taken as an hour a day by a boy who meant well, and they scorched and dropped. One was watered too much and rotted at the collar. One was lifted in the heat of the afternoon by Baro's nephew, who had not been told the rule about dawn, and went into shock within the same day. And one simply thinned and went pale and grey over two weeks and died for no reason Naledi could name, except that a wild plant in a place it did not choose does die sometimes for no reason that is written down.

She wrote all of it down. Every death, with the number, with the day, with the weather, with the water, with what was done wrong if she could find it and with the word unknown if she could not. She did not tear out the pages. That was the whole of the work, when it came to it: an honest record of eight failures and eight successes and the difference between them, so that the ninth and tenth and fortieth would live.

The eight that lived put out new leaves in the third week. Naledi went up on her own one morning to look at them and stood in the row with the watering can in her hand and did not water them, because the book said twice a day in the first month and it was the second month and once a day was the rule, and she stood there and made herself hold to the rule that was written in the book and not the feeling in her hand. That was a thing she had never been good at. It was a thing the valley was teaching her.

Baro came up to the plot in the fifth week, on a dry morning, with his coat on and nothing in his hands.

Naledi was at the frame lifting the thatch an hour, the way the schedule said, and she saw him come along the wall and stand at the edge of the terrace and not say anything for a while. She went on with the thatch. He watched her lift it and peg it and check the eight and mark nothing because there was nothing to mark, and then he said the thing he had come to say, which was not the thing she had expected and was, in the end, worse.

"I signed the paper," he said. "Eleven years I have sat in that schoolroom and read the union papers and the clinic papers and the letters from the town, and I read that one twice, and I signed it, because two of the children in that list were my own blood and I could not look at them and say no to a man with medicine in his truck." He stopped. He started again, which was what he did when he was ashamed. "I signed the paper, and Toma would not look up, and you stood there and gave me the numbers straight, and I signed it anyway. I am not sorry the man is gone. I am sorry I was the one that let him in."

Naledi pegged the thatch and stood up and looked at him. There was a way to answer that which was true and easy — that the contract had bought them nothing and cost them the wet season, that any of them might have done it, that the village had voted. She found that she did not want the easy answer this morning. She wanted the water carried.

"You can leave the coat on the wall," she said. "There are two cans by the wall and the row of six is thirsty and I have not got to it. You carry water and we will call it square."

Baro looked at her the way he looked at a figure he did not believe.

"That is all?"

"That is all. The arithmetic is the arithmetic. You cannot sign it back. You can carry water."

He took the coat off and put it on the wall and picked up a can and went down to the channel and came back with it full, and then went again, and again, until the six were watered and the four were watered and Naledi had finished the thatch and was sitting on the wall writing the morning's notes. He set the can down and wiped his face with his sleeve and stood looking at the eight living plants in their rows, and said the last thing in the flat voice he used for figures.

"Eight out of sixteen."

"Eight out of sixteen," Naledi said. "That is the number. The other eight taught us the method."

Baro nodded slowly, as if agreeing with arithmetic, and put his coat back on and went down the slope, and after that he came up twice a week to carry water, and never said anything about the paper again, and Naledi never asked him to.

Toma came up on the dry evenings and sat on the wall and taught the children.

That was the part Naledi had not planned and could not have built, because the rules were not in any book she had ever read, and the children did not learn them from her. They came up the slope in the blue hour with Essa leading them, seven or eight of them, and they sat on the old wall in a row with their feet hanging, and Toma told them the old rules of the tending families, the ones the four families had kept before the roots were pulled two generations back. He did not tell them as history. He told them as instructions.

"The rule of the leaf," he said. "You take the leaf and never the root, because the root is the plant and the leaf is the plant making more. You take from the outside of the stand and never the middle. You take one in four and you leave three, and you leave the oldest tree alone, because it remembers."

"How does a tree remember, Papa?" a small girl asked.

"It remembers by being older than the rest," Toma said. "That is all remembering is, at the end. You stay where the thing happened and you do not move. The old tree on the west side remembers the four roots, and that is why we do not touch it."

"You cannot remember something you were not there for," Essa said, from the middle of the row, in the flat way she argued now.

"You can remember what was taken from your ground," Toma said. "You can remember by how the ground is now. A tree remembers a hole by growing a little crooked at the edge of it."

Essa wrote that down. Naledi, sitting at the end of the wall with the book on her knee, did not.

"The rule of the water," Toma went on. "You do not clear the channel in the wet and you do not leave it choked in the dry, and you do not cut the seep, because the seep is the spring and the spring is the village, and if you cut it the water goes under the rock and the dry month finds you with the dust. The old wall was laid for the crossing, not for the garden, and it does not carry water and it never did. The water runs in the cut, west side, the one she opened, and the wall is for standing on and for stopping what burns."

"And the rule of the small ones," Essa said, because she knew it and wanted it said.

"The rule of the small ones," Toma said. "You leave the moths, and you leave the small things under the mat, and you do not go up on the mat with your boots on. Because the plant does not live alone, and the leaf is not the medicine without the small ones. And if you cannot say why a rule is there, you keep it anyway and find out later, because some rules are the ones that kept our grandmothers alive and nobody wrote them down."

One of the boys asked whether that was why the company man had come, because he did not know the rules, and Toma said no; he said the company man knew the rules and had decided they did not matter, which was a worse thing, and the children were quiet for a while, and then the small girl said her feet were cold and the lesson was over.

When the children had gone down, Toma stayed on the wall, and Naledi stayed with him, and the mist came up off the wet ground the way it did at dusk and put the copper smell back into the air.

"I was fourteen," Toma said, out of nothing. "The year the four roots were pulled. My grandfather's generation, and my grandfather among them, and they counted me old enough to carry the tools. I held the light for the others. They cut four roots on the west side in one afternoon and carried them down and sold them to a man in the town and came back pleased with themselves, because they thought they had made a trade."

He looked at the row of eight living plants.

"I was there for the four of them," he said. "Not the first. The first was my grandfather's. But the fourth, that one was mine, because my hands were smaller and I could get in under the mat where the men could not with the shovel, and I put my arm in and closed my hand on it and pulled it out, root and all, and there was a moth on it and I shook the moth off and I gave the root to my grandfather and he said good. And the mat yellowed where the cut was, the way it yellowed for you when you lifted the short piece last spring. And the moths left the patch. And by the end of that year the yellow patch was three times the size it should have been, because when the mat is cut it does not stop at the cut, and the next autumn there was a fever and there was no leaf, because the grove was sick and there were no moths on it to make it well."

Naledi sat still. She had heard the story before, in the cave, and had written the dates and the arithmetic in her book. She had not heard that he had pulled the fourth root himself. She understood why that was the part he had kept back, and she did not say anything about it, because there was nothing to say, and because he was not asking to be forgiven. He was doing what she had done in the clinic book. He was writing the truth down and letting it stand.

"Fifty years I carried that," he said. "The grove came back in the bad wet season, when the water kept everyone out and nobody could go up and take anything, and that is the whole of the arithmetic, and I have spent the years since counting moths each evening, one evening at a time, wondering whether I caused its leaving and whether I am allowed to watch it come back."

He got down off the wall and took up his stick and stood looking at the eight plants.

"You were not the cause of the leaving," Naledi said. "The taking was. You were the hands."

"I was the hands," Toma said. "The hands are the reason. That is the rule I did not know at fourteen and know now, and it is too late to teach it to myself, so I am teaching it to them." He nodded down the slope where the children had gone. "Your plot will hold it better than my memory did. A plot does not forget."

The village met at the schoolroom on the first clear evening of the dry season and voted the terrace common land.

It was not a difficult vote. Baro read the proposal out in the flat voice he used for figures — that the ground below the grove belonged to no household but to all of them, that no person should sell or lease it, that the plants on it were the village's and the leaf from it was the village's, and that whoever tended it did so for the village and not for themselves. Thirteen household heads said yes. The four who had voted no on the contract said yes. Nobody said no.

Then Baro put the second question, which Naledi had not known was coming until he said it, and which he asked slowly, because it was the whole of the evening and he knew it.

"Who runs it?"

There was a silence in which Naledi heard the water going down the channel outside and the fire settling in the stove, and then Ruth Achieng said what all of them had come to say, in four words.

"The botanist runs it."

Naledi sat with the book closed on her knee and did not answer at first, because the Board of Field Credentials' letter was folded in her coat pocket and had been for a fortnight, and there was a version of her life in which the answer to that vote was no. The letter offered her a restoration of credentials and a supervised position in a herbarium three days' travel south, on terms, answering to a board that had suspended her for exposing a fraud that had been true. She had read it twice on the morning she opened it and once since, and she knew exactly what it said, and she had not yet written the answer.

She thought of the eight living plants on the terrace and the eight failures in the book, and of the method, which was hers now and not the profession's, and of the eight months at the shortest from lifting to a plant that could stand on its own.

"I will run it," she said, "on one condition, and it is the same condition I would put to any board anywhere. The records are kept here, in this valley, in this schoolroom, and anyone who wants to use what we learn comes up the canyon on foot and asks us first."

Baro said that was already the rule for everything in Sorrow's Gate, and put it to the room, and the room said yes again, and the meeting turned to the yam harvest.

Naledi went out into the dark with the book under her arm and stood at the door of the schoolroom a while, listening to the channel. She took the letter out of her coat pocket and read it one more time by the light coming through the door, and then she folded it and put it back, and went down the lane toward the wall where the terrace lay under the mist, and did not answer it that night, because there was a row of six that wanted water before morning.
