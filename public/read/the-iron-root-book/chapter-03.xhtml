The First Illness

The boy was called Emeka, and he was eleven, and he had run the canyon in the dark with a lantern because his sister had gone the way the first child went and his mother had told him to fetch the healer and not to stop for anything, and he had not stopped for anything, including the place where the water went shoulder-width and the stones rolled underfoot.

He stood in front of Toma and said his sister's name and then could not say anything else. His breath came in three parts. He had lost the lantern on the way; the light Naledi had seen was the last of the flame in a tin can.

Toma put a hand flat on the boy's head, which was not a blessing and not a comfort, but a measurement. He held it there.

'How long has she been hot?' he said.

'Since the washing,' Emeka said. 'Since yesterday's washing, at the pool.'

'Where the women wash.'

'Where they took the clothes down and the water was up past the flat stones.'

Toma took his hand back and looked at Naledi in the dark, and she understood that this was the answer to a question she had not yet known how to ask.

'We go down now,' he said. 'Can you walk?'

'I can walk.'

'Then walk behind me and keep your lamp off. The way down is worse than the way up.'

They went down in the dark, and it took two hours. The mist filled the valley like milk in a jug, and the way was all stone and wet root and the small sounds of Emeka ahead of her, breathing through his mouth. Twice she put her foot where the ground was not and caught herself on a root cable with her hand. Once Toma stopped and waited for her without saying anything, and when she came level with him he started again.

At the canyon mouth the mist thinned and the village came up out of it in pieces: a row of roofs, a wall, the square shape of the clinic with a light in one window, and beyond the roofs the thin grey slice of the river, which was up.

Sorrow's Gate was thirty houses, more or less, on two rises of ground above the water line, with a lane between them and a footbridge over the low ground and a shop with a hand-painted sign and the clinic at the end, which was two rooms and a cupboard and a door with a bolt. In the wet months the ground between the houses went soft, and in the wet months the pool below the washing stones came up and joined the river, and both of them came up to the footbridge.

There were two lamps burning in the village and neither of them was moving. That was how she knew, before anyone told her, that a child had died that evening.

The clinic room smelled of carbolic and sweat and boiled cloth. Nurse Nuru Banda was thirty and had done her training in a town three hours' walk away and had come back, and she had two thermometers and a cupboard and a lamp and a list. She gave Naledi the list before she gave her anything else, which Naledi would remember.

'Two of them have gone,' Nuru said. 'The one in the ninth day, that was before your letter. This evening it was Chidi, Emeka's sister, four years old. Fever for five days. The rash came on the third day. She was vomiting and could not keep water. This morning she did not know her mother.'

'Show me the list.'

Eleven names on it before, twelve now, and Nuru had written beside each one the day the fever started, the day the rash began, the age, and the house. She had also written, at the bottom, two columns that Naledi did not expect and would have given a great deal to see in a hospital: which water a house drew from, and where the children had been playing.

'How did you know to write that?'

'I did not,' Nuru said. 'Toma's granddaughter asked the mothers for me. Essa. She is twelve and she can make people answer. She has been going house to house since the first one went down, because I could not leave the sick.' She looked at the door. 'She is the fastest runner in the village and now she is on the list. She went down at noon.'

The village had already gathered. They gathered in the lane below the shop, which was as far from the water as the houses went, and they came with lamps because there was no moon, and they stood in the rain in their coats and did not sit down.

Old Baro stood at the front with his arms folded, a wide man with a wide voice, and he spoke to the whole lane at once.

'We have buried two,' he said. 'Two this month and both of them small. Now Toma's granddaughter. Now I say what I have been saying since the card came. The company man has medicine. He came in a truck up the dry riverbed and left a card at the shop and the card has a telephone number written on it, and I have looked at it, and there is a place where you put money in and a thing that talks. We have three families with money in this village and eleven without. We have voted on things before and we will vote now.'

'What has he promised you?' Naledi said.

Baro turned. He looked at her coat and her boots and her notebook, which she was holding, and he did not look pleased.

'Who is this?'

'Doctor Okonjo,' Nuru said. 'She is the one I wrote to.'

'You wrote to a doctor,' Baro said. 'The card said medicine. Medicine can be carried. A doctor has to walk and then decide.'

'Medicine that has not been made is only a card,' Naledi said.

There was a silence in the lane that had rain in it.

'And what have you brought, Doctor Okonjo?' Baro said. 'You have brought a notebook. I have seen you in the valley with a string and a little flag and a pencil. My grandchild has been hot since Sunday and you have been measuring the ground. Tell me one thing you know that will stop the next one going down. One thing.'

And Naledi stood in the rain in front of thirty people with lamps and said nothing, because she had one thing, and it was not proof, and she had spent three years learning what it cost to hand a village a sentence she could not stand behind.

'I have a list,' she said at last. 'And I have questions. Tomorrow at first light I want to see every sick child and every house, and I want to see where each house draws water and where the children wash. If the list tells me what I think it tells me, I will come back and tell you, and it will be a sentence I can stand behind.'

'Tomorrow,' Baro said. He said it the way a man says a price. 'Tomorrow is the company man's tomorrow too. His card says he is nearby. I will send to him in the morning unless you have something better by then, and I say this in front of everybody so that you can hear me saying it. My grandchildren are among the eleven. He calls everyone by their full name too.'

Toma took her to his house, which was the last house before the rise, and Essa was in the small room at the back with her grandmother, and Essa was very sick.

She was twelve and she was lying on her side with her knees drawn up, and her skin was hot and dry to the back of Naledi's hand and her eyes were open and did not track. Naledi sat down on the floor beside the bed and took out the thermometer and the light.

Temperature thirty-nine point six. Pulse fast and thin. The headache was there — she flinched when Naledi raised the lamp too close. The vomiting had stopped because there was nothing left in her. And across her chest, faint as a watermark, there was a rash: flat, red, the sort of thing that would wash out of the skin under a lamp unless you looked twice, and Naledi looked twice and then a third time and wrote rash, blanching, chest, day 3.

She drew two millilitres of nothing in particular out of her kit and used it on a strip of paper along the inside of Essa's lip, because she was testing for the slow fever that climbs over days and comes with headache and a cough and sometimes a nosebleed, and the paper said nothing, and she wrote that down too, including the fact that she was testing for a possibility she could not treat. She sat with the child while the grandmother slept in the chair on the other side of the bed, and Naledi did what she could, which was water by spoon and cool cloths and counting.

She counted backward.

At the clinic she had copied Nuru's list into her own notebook, in a fresh column, by the days Nuru had written and the days the mothers had given, and she had put the first fever at the top, and now in the small room she worked down it with a pencil and the lamp turned low.

Nine days. Seven days. Ten days. Eight days. Six days. Eleven days. Nine days. Twelve days. Six days. Ten days.

And beside each one, the ages. Four, four, five, six, six, seven, eight, nine, eleven, twelve, twelve. And the one who had died, four.

She drew a line under the ages across the double page, and then she sat back and looked at the shape of it, and the shape of it was not the shape of a thing carried between people. It was the shape of a thing that came from one place and arrived at many houses at once. Her eyes were not bad. It was there in the column.

She woke Toma.

'Look at this,' she said. 'Every one of them fell ill between the sixth and twelfth day after the rains, and the youngest are worst and the oldest are least, and Chidi and the first child were four years old and they died, and there are fifteen children in this village under six and ten of them are on this list. In a sickness carried between people it would come in one house and then the next, over weeks. This came to all the houses in one hand of days, and it did not come to the men and the women who are home all day and it did not come to the grandmothers who sit with the sick and hold them, and it came to the ones who are small and play outside and go down to the water.'

Toma stood at the foot of the bed with his stick across his knees and did not answer.

'Water,' she said. 'Standing water, or what comes out of standing water. That is where the fever started and that is where I will look, and I will look with the light tomorrow morning, when the pool is down and I can see what has been lying in it.'

'It was not the washing,' Toma said. 'The washing is every week of every year and the fever is not every week of every year.'

'No. It is not,' Naledi said, and stopped, because he had handed her something and she had not yet taken hold of it. 'So it is not the water they always use. It is the water that is new.'

She looked at Essa, who was breathing fast and shallow and had begun, in her sleep, to say a word over and over that Naledi could not make out.

She sat with the child until the small hours and then went out for air, because the room was hot and she was beginning to make mistakes with numbers.

The lane was empty and the rain had stopped and the mist had come down off the valley and into the village, so that the houses stood in it to their shoulders and their eaves. Under the footbridge, the pool lay like a slate, and above it the washing stones, and above them the seep and the two shallow wells and the river.

She walked the length of the water herself, in the dark, with the lamp low, and looked at what the flood had brought down. A dead rat against the bridge pier. A bicycle without wheels. A green branch wedged between the stones, and on the branch, caught in the angle where a leaf grows, a small pale dust that held the lamp light and did not fall off.

She had seen that dust three days before, on the underside of a plant, on the seep, on the contact of the clay and the limestone, above the village.

She stood in the mist with the branch in her hand and understood two things at once, and neither of them was a cure.

The first was that the pool had not made the fever out of nothing. The pool had taken something that was already in the valley, high up, and brought it down to where the children played.

The second was that Essa's fever had begun at noon, and the rains were forecast to hold for another week, and there was no drinking water in Sorrow's Gate that came from anywhere except the ground that the flood had crossed.

She walked back up the lane to the house, and at the door she heard Baro's voice inside, loud and too careful, repeating a thing for the second time in the way a man repeats a thing when he is ashamed of it.

'We have voted on things before. We will vote. The company man has medicine and a truck and a paper, and Doctor Okonjo has a notebook and a string, and I say this to Toma's face, and I say it kindly: I will send to him in the morning.'

Naledi stood outside the door with her hand on the frame and listened to the old man, who she had disliked for one evening in the rain, and who she understood, standing there, to be doing exactly what he said he was doing — counting what he could and buying what he could not count, because two of his own were on the list.

She did not go in.

She turned around and looked up the valley, into the mist, where everything she had was: six pages, a string of knots and flags, a list of days, a branch, a theory she could not yet feed anyone with. The answer was up there on the upper slope with the pale-trunked trees and the plant Toma would not take her to see, and it would still be there in the morning, and she had never in her working life been able to see a plant and not name it.

She went back in and sat down by Essa and took the child's hand, and stayed where her hands could do something, and counted the small fast breaths until the window went grey.

At first light she went down to the lane and stood where the village could see her, with the branch in one hand and the notebook in the other, and when Baro came out she did not argue with him.

'I am not asking you to trust me over your grandchildren,' she said. 'I am asking you for one day. Give me the morning in the valley. If I come down with nothing, then send for the company man and I will not stand in your way, and I will write down what he does so that the village will know what it bought.'

Baro looked at her for a long time with the rain not quite falling and the mist standing in the roofs behind her.

'One day,' he said. 'And Toma goes with you, because I want two of you to come back with the same story.'

Naledi looked up the valley one last time. The mist was thick on the upper slope and the grove was not visible at all.

'Then we had better start climbing,' she said.
