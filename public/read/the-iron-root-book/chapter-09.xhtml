The Burning Clearing

The rain had stopped at midnight and the wind had turned, which was the worst thing that could have happened. Naledi stood on the clinic step at first light and smelled it before she understood it: the valley was drying. Not drying the way it dried in the hot months, slowly, from the bottom. Drying from the top down, wind running over the slopes and pulling the wet out of the leaf litter, while the ground underneath stayed soaked. She had measured the litter two days ago above the grove, pressing the moisture meter into it and finding it at forty per cent, the reading of a wet sponge. She had not measured it since the wind came.

Nuru Banda came out with a bucket and set it down. "You are standing in my way."

"Sorry." Naledi moved, and did not move far. "How many are on the tea now?"

"Nine. Two better, one the same, six worse." Nuru poured water from the bucket into the clinic's clay filter and did not look up. "Essa asked for you. She said to say the word for yesterday is a bad word and she will not write it."

"Tell her I said to write it badly anyway."

"Tell her yourself."

The road crew's noise reached them from the lower slope while Naledi was still on the step, and she heard it wrong before she heard it right. It was not the sound of saws. It was a flat slapping, steady, and under it a low tearing that came in bursts, and between the bursts a man's voice calling numbers. She listened for four seconds and then went for her boots.

Renn had started two days ahead of what he had told the council.

She understood the arithmetic on the walk down, and the arithmetic was this: his letter said the cutting would begin after the council's second reading, and the second reading had not happened, and Renn was not a man who was imprecise with words. He had said the road begins at first light. He had never said which morning.

By the time she reached the lower path, Toma was already there, standing still in a way she had learned to read, and four villagers were behind him, and they were all looking up. Naledi looked up.

The smoke was not one column. It was a set of them, four or five, leaning east on the new wind, and they were not rising off the clearing that Renn had begun cutting below the shelf. They were rising off the brush slope above it. The slope between the road and the western host trees, the slope nobody had cut, the slope she had walked three times and could draw from memory: low scrub, dead bracken under it, and between the bracken the litter of years.

"He set the slope," she said.

"He set the slope," Toma said. "He set it to open the way through and the wind took it up."

"How long?"

"Since before dawn. Andile heard the axes at three and thought it was the road." He gestured without lifting his arm, a small roll of the wrist toward the four villagers: two men, a woman Naledi knew as Ruth who worked the shop and had two sick children, and a boy of maybe sixteen who was already holding a hoe.

"How far up?"

"To the western edge." Toma said it plainly, and then he said the next thing plainly too, because that was how he said everything that cost him. "It is in the litter now. It will not stop for the road."

Naledi took out her notebook and did not open it. She put it back.

"Then we do not talk about it," she said. "Wet sacks. Hoes. Pans if there are pans. We cut in front of it along the line of the old stone wall, the one that runs west above the grove. Everybody who can carry water, carries water. Tell Nuru. Tell the ones who are not sick to leave their doors."

The boy said, "What about the grove?"

"We are going to the grove," Naledi said. "Walk."

The fire was smaller than she had feared and worse than she had imagined, which was the ordinary truth about fires.

From the foot of the western slope she could see it clearly. It was not a wall. It was a ragged front three hundred paces wide, moving up and across, and where it met the dead bracken it stood up and roared and where it met damp moss it crawled and slept, and both things were true at once. The wind was going east-northeast, which meant it was crossing the slope rather than climbing it, and that was the only mercy in the whole morning — it meant the grove lay to the fire's left, below the wind, in the lee. But the slope above the grove ran up to the dry shelf, and the shelf was where the burn would go if it turned.

Naledi walked the front once, fast, counting. Then she sent the villagers in.

They cut in front of it along the old stone wall, the way she had asked, and the wall was the reason they could. It was a low thing, four courses of dry stone laid by people who had lived above the flood line, and it ran west across the slope in a long shallow curve, and the brush on its upstream side was thin because the stones held water in their shadow. The fire came up to the wall and the villagers were already there, and they beat it with wet sacks, and where the sacks did not hold they cut bracken with the hoes and dragged it back and piled it in the wet ditch behind the wall.

It worked and did not work. Naledi learned the difference in the first quarter hour.

Where they reached the fire in time, it died against the wall. Where they did not, it crossed, and it took them the length of the front to learn that they could not be everywhere and that the fire could. Ruth's sack caught and she dropped it and stamped it out with both feet, and did not stop. The boy with the hoe cut a strip of bracken the width of three men lying down and then had to run because the fire came round the end of his strip and behind him. Toma carried water in the two tin cans from the shop and threw it where it was needed and nowhere else, and he did not speak once in the whole morning, and Naledi found she was reading him the way she read the channel: where he stood was where the ground would hold.

Then the fire reached the edge of the grove.

She heard it before she saw it. The host trees were not burning; the bark of them was pale and smooth and the old ones were wet at the base, and the flame came up to them and stood back. What burned was the litter between the trees, the deep mat of leaves and moth scale and shed root that lay over the western edge of the root mat, and it burned hot and clean and it burned in a line, and the fire was eating one thing only.

"It is in the mat," she called. "Not the trees. It is in the mat."

Toma came alongside her and looked, and did not answer, and she understood that he had known and had let her be the one to say it.

They went in. The grove was half smoke and half cold, and the smoke lay along the ground like water in a pan and would not lift, and Naledi went into it with a wet sack and beat at the litter at the western edge while the fire crawled under the root. Not on the root. Under it. The mat was woven and the wind ran through it like a draught through a basket, and the fire moved inside the weave and showed itself only at the holes.

She put her hand down and felt the heat in the mat and pulled her hand back and felt it again, and she stopped beating at the surface and started cutting. Four strokes with the hoe through the mat ahead of the fire, down to the pale rock, a trench a hand wide. The fire reached the trench and stopped. It stood there and reached across with little yellow tongues and could not cross bare stone.

"Here," she shouted. "Cut here. Through the mat, not the litter. Down to rock. Everybody, here."

They came. Ruth and the boy and one of the men cut along the line she had started, and Toma cut opposite, and Naledi walked the trench and measured it with her hands and made them widen it where it narrowed, and the fire came up to the trench and sat against it and went out. Not all of it. It went out in the trench and it went on burning in the litter toward the low side, away from the trees, in the flank that would do less damage, and by the time that flank burned itself out and lay down in grey ash, they had all stopped talking.

Naledi straightened up with the smoke in her throat and counted what was left.

Two host trees dead. Young ones, fourteen, maybe fifteen years old, at the western edge, east of where the mat had burned. They had been dry at the base and the mat under them had gone, and the bark had lifted and gone grey, and she knew the look and did not need to touch them. The litter along the western edge was gone, a strip thirty paces wide and a hundred long, and the mat under it was black on the surface and pale underneath where the trench had cut through.

The old westward tree stood. The nursery chambers on the north side stood. The main root mat stood, and the trench they had cut lay across it like a scar, and beyond the trench the mat was whole and dark and wet.

Twenty-seven host trees where there had been twenty-nine. She wrote the number down and did not write anything else.

Then Toma said, "Sit down," and she turned and saw Renn coming down the slope through the smoke with his porters behind him, and one of the porters was carrying a man.

The man was the foreman, the one who had run the axes. He was being carried between two porters with his arms over their shoulders by the wrists, and his right foot was hanging at an angle that feet do not hang at, and he was conscious, and his face was the colour of the ash they were walking through. Basel Renn was walking beside him and not in front of him, which Naledi noted as she noted everything, without meaning to.

Renn stopped three paces from her and looked at the grove behind her, taking it in properly, and she watched him do the sum. She could see the moment he found the answer, and she saw that the answer did not please him.

"Miss Okonjo."

"Dr Okonjo."

"Of course." He glanced at the foreman. "We need to get him off the slope before the light goes. We have a canvas we can make a litter from." And then, plainly, because he was always plain when it cost him: "The road is open to the trees. I have a man down. I have nine porters who have watched one of my promises go wrong. I am not cutting today."

"Today," Naledi said.

"Today," Renn said. "The rain will settle into the shelf by tomorrow evening and we will not be able to work the slope at all. That is an operational fact, not a threat. What I take off this slope, I take before it. If your council's order arrives first, I will read it with the same care I read my own."

The foreman made a sound.

Renn turned and went back to him and put his shoulder under the man's arm and walked him up the slope. That was all. The porters followed him and the smoke followed them, and Naledi stood where she was and watched the whole party go up, and it took them a long time because the foreman had to stop twice.

"He cut the slope to save a week," Toma said behind her.

"He cut the slope because it was cheaper than the long way round, and the wind turned, and now he has one man down and two host trees dead and no material." Naledi picked up the hoe she had dropped and did not remember dropping. "Two host trees dead, Toma."

"Two," he said. "And the mat."

"The mat held at the trench."

"It held at the trench you cut." He looked at the black strip along the western edge and then at the trench and then at the ash beyond it. "Rain is coming. Enough rain and the channel will take the west fork and the west fork is above this ground. If the western edge has no litter, the water will run faster there."

She looked where he was looking. She had cut a trench through the mat to stop a fire. The next heavy rain would come off the burnt western slope with no litter to slow it and no mat to hold it, and it would run west, and the old channel above the grove took the west fork and the west fork was the one they had cleared.

The fire had cleared ground above the channel. She had cleared the channel. Between the two of them, water now had a road.

"When is the shelf due?" she asked.

"Two days. Maybe three." Toma picked up his cans. "Nuru will have the sick ones to move. The clinic is at the low end of the path."

They walked down. On the eastern edge, below the cut, moths were lifting off the grove in a pale cloud, more than Naledi had seen on any evening, rising through the smoke in a long soft line and drifting west because the wind was west, and she stood at the foot of the slope and watched them for a while because there was nothing else she could do that was worth doing.

The moths were not leaving. They were moving. All of them, off the east edge where the smoke lay and off the burnt western litter, settling in the middle of the mat and along the north chambers and on the old tree, in rows, in their numbers, in a place where there was still water in the weave and still shade over it. Two host trees dead and the moths still there, on the part of the mat that had not been cut and would not burn.

The coming rain would test the trench. The trench was on her account and the road was on it too, and she could count the hours the way she counted leaves.

She took out her notebook and opened it and wrote the date and the two numbers and the line of the trench from the old wall to the rock at the west end. Then she closed it.

Renn would be back at first light with his nine porters and a litter and a soft voice, and she had a trench to widen and a channel to watch and nine children on tea at the clinic and two host trees to record as dead. The flood season was bigger than his money. She had known that for a week and had not been able to prove it, and now the fire had written it on the slope in front of witnesses: two host trees at the edge, one cut trench, and a whole valley of water about to come and decide for them all.

She went down to the schoolroom and stood at the door and did not go in, because Nuru had moved the four worst children there that morning and the room was full of the smell of wet wool and steam and boiled leaf, and Essa was at the far end, sitting up against a rolled coat, watching the door the way she had watched it for three days.

"Doctor," Essa said. "Did we save the trees?"

Naledi came in and sat down on the floor beside the pallet and got the notebook out, because a girl with a record book wanted an answer in a form she could use.

"Two trees died," she said. "Twenty-seven are standing. The mat is cut through in one place, in a line, and the line held, and the moths have moved to the middle of the mat and are there now, all of them, in rows." She turned to a clean page and wrote the date at the top and the number twenty-seven and the number two, and then held the pencil out. "Write the weather. You were on the step this morning. Was the wind on your left or your right?"

Essa took the pencil and thought about it, because Essa always thought about it, and wrote the word and put the pencil down.

"The fire came off the west slope," Naledi said, "which means the litter up there is gone, which means when the rain comes the water will run faster down the west side than it ever has, and the channel above the grove is the thing between the grove and the water. That is what the next two days are. Not the trees. The water."

"And the men with the axes?"

"They took their man down the canyon. He has a broken foot. They will be back at first light, and if the rain comes first they will not be able to work the slope at all, and if it does not, we will have to be there."

Essa looked at the two numbers on the page for a while.

"Twenty-seven," she said, in the flat voice she had learned from her grandfather. "Write it with the time. Papa says a number without a time is just a story about a number."

Naledi wrote the time.

Behind them Nuru came down the room with the pot and the ladle and began at the first pallet, and the rain that had been holding off all day came down on the roof in a long grey sheet, all at once, as if it had been waiting for the count to be finished.
