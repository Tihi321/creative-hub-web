The Locked Door

The room had no windows, and the only door locked behind her. Naledi noted both the way she noted a site: position, aspect, what the features were for. Windows let a person look away. A locked door kept a decision inside it.

Three people sat at the table, and a fourth sat slightly apart with a recorder. Professor Lena Marsh chaired. She had spent thirty years on the drinking behaviour of arid-zone ungulates and, as far as Naledi could tell, had never once been rained on.

“Dr. Okonjo. Thank you for coming.”

“I was instructed to come.”

“You were invited,” Marsh said.

“The email said attendance was required.”

The recorder’s red light held steady. Marsh folded her hands.

“This panel has reviewed your file. We are satisfied that your research is not in question. Nobody here disputes your identification work, your survey methods, or the accuracy of your published corrections. That should be said clearly.”

Naledi waited. In her experience, a compliment arrived at the front of a sentence the way a lure arrives at the front of a hook.

“However,” Marsh said, “the file also records a persistent difficulty in working within institutional processes. Three formal complaints from two departments. One disputed authorship claim. One incident in which you communicated directly with a journal editor without notifying your head of department.”

“I notified my head of department. He asked me not to write to the editor. I wrote to the editor.”

“And that is the difficulty.”

Naledi took out her pencil and her notebook, because ink ran when it rained and because a pencil made a sound people could hear. She wrote the date in the top corner. She wrote the room number.

“The panel’s finding,” Marsh said, “is that your field credentials are suspended indefinitely.”

The pencil stopped.

“That means no collecting permits will be issued to you. No removal of plant material from any site, protected or otherwise. Herbarium access by appointment, supervised. Your teaching and your supervision are unaffected. You will keep your salary.”

“For how long?”

“Indefinitely.”

“Indefinitely is a word,” Naledi said. “It isn’t a duration.”

“It is the duration the committee chose.”

The man to Marsh’s left slid a form across the table. Naledi read it twice. The finding was there in the second paragraph, in eleven words, with a clause number beside it: persistent difficulty in working within institutional processes. Nothing about data. Nothing about the mountain range. Nothing about the eighteen months she had spent proving that a published survey had been drawn from a student’s thesis and a set of field notebooks containing site codes, dates, and not one recorded measurement.

She copied the eleven words into her notebook, in front of them, and put the pencil down.

“May I ask what outcome would have followed if I had not reported it?”

“That isn’t the panel’s business,” Marsh said.

“No,” Naledi agreed. “It isn’t.”

She signed the form. She was careful to sign it legibly, because a signature was a fact and facts were what she had left.

Three years earlier, in a corridor not unlike this one, she had stood outside the office of Professor Idris Kalu with a folder under her arm and her hands damp, and she had been quite sure that being right would be enough.

It had not been a hard thing to find. Kalu had published a range map for a genus of upland shrubs he had never seen growing. She had gone looking for one species and found eleven of his notebooks in a filing cabinet, each with the same careful header, each blank underneath. The map had come from a student’s thesis, squared and redrawn. Two species had been recorded from a valley in which no botanist had set foot for decades, which is precisely how she had noticed: the coordinates were too neat.

She had done everything in the correct order. Department first. Then the research office. Then the journal. The retraction had taken fourteen months. Kalu resigned with a pension and a retirement dinner and a speech in which he was described, twice, as a founder of the field.

She had been right, and the profession had answered her with a locked door and a form, and then with a form, and then with a locked door, in that order, for three years. Two former colleagues still crossed the corridor to avoid her, and one, a decent man named Pretorius, had said, kindly, You should have let it go, and had not understood why she had to leave the room after that.

She left the building at four o’clock. At the outer door she pressed her thumb on the reader, out of habit, and the little light stayed amber, and she took her thumb away.

The courier came at half past eight, in the dark, on a motorbike with one working mirror. He had a padded envelope that had been through a great many hands. There was tape on the corner, a stamp from a district office, an address in a small square hand and then the address corrected twice, once in a different ink.

She signed for it on his phone with her finger.

Inside were three pages, written in two hands.

The first hand was small and square and had made a list, because lists were how a careful person held on to a bad week. Eleven sick. Four under six years old. One buried on the ninth day. Fever, headache, vomiting, a flat red rash across the chest. Started when the rains came. Beside the last item someone had written, smaller still, clinic has no microscope, two thermometers, no vehicle.

The second hand was rounder and asked for things.

Doctor, my name is Nuru Banda, I am the nurse at Sorrow’s Gate, at the mouth of the Iron Basin. We have a clinic with one room and a cupboard. We have buried one child and there are eleven burning now and I do not know what I am looking at.

We cannot pay you. We have no money for a doctor and no road for a car. I am writing because a company man came in a truck last month and left a card at the shop, and the card says they have medicine, and I do not trust the card.

I read your name in a journal, in a notice where they were pulling a doctor’s work apart. It was in a list of everybody who had signed. You were first on the list. I thought, the one who says the true thing might come.

We have nothing to offer but a bed, food, and ground to stand on.

Below that, in the first hand again: Toma, healer. Come on foot. The canyon is no road.

Naledi sat with the pages on her knees for a long time. Then she stood up, and took her field kit off the top shelf of the cupboard, and laid everything out on the floor in rows, and began.

There was a rule against this. The rule had been in the letter she had not yet answered, and in the eleventh word of a finding with a clause number beside it, and it said: no collecting, no sites.

She packed the plant press, the straps, the blotting paper, the cardboard. She packed a screw of silica gel for drying leaf material, the ten-times lens and the spare, the clinometer, the GPS with two spare batteries. Pencils, eleven of them, sharpened. Waterproof notebook. Two lengths of string, because string measured things that rulers could not reach. Boots resoled in the spring. A first-aid kit containing more gauze than a botanist needs. A thermometer with a cracked case.

She wrote a note to the department: Personal leave, three weeks. Lectures covered. She wrote it plainly, without apologising, because an apology would have been a lie and she was down to very few currencies.

Then she wrote one line in the notebook, on the inside cover, where she kept the things she had to be honest with herself about.

Not fieldwork. A visit.

She looked at it for a while. It was the weakest sentence she had written in years.

The first bus went through the night and took eleven hours and smelled of diesel and orange peel. The second bus took nine and stopped for a goat, twice for a puncture, and once so that the driver could argue with a man about a fence. The towns thinned. The tar went from black to grey to a stripe down the middle of a dirt road.

At the depot at the end of it she hired Dumi, who owned a truck with a cracked windscreen and a load of cement bags and a refrigerator strapped on top.

“How far?” he said.

“As far as the truck goes.”

He looked at her boots, and at the press under her arm, and laughed. He drove for three hours on a track that was mostly a suggestion. Twice he stopped to move stones. At a drift where brown water ran over the road at knee height he stopped, looked at it, and shook his head.

“With the fridge on top, no.”

“Then I’ll walk.”

“It’s four hours to the gorge, and the gorge is another two to the gate. Nobody walks that at night.”

“It isn’t night yet.”

He watched her get out and put on her pack and adjust the strap across her chest. He scratched his jaw, and something in his face changed from bargaining to something more like pity.

“There is a man waiting at the gate,” he said. “He has been sitting there four days. He is very old and he does not like people who come in trucks.”

“What’s his name?”

“Toma.”

She smelled the copper before she saw the canyon. It came out of the gorge on the cold air like a struck match, copper and wet stone and something green and crushed underneath it, and she stopped on the track and stood still for a moment, the way you do at a boundary.

The mouth of the canyon was a slice in a limestone wall, twenty metres wide at the base and narrowing upward into a channel of grey rock with rain streaks down it. A stream ran out of it, low and clear, over a bed of pale stones with a red-brown stain along the waterline. Above the stain, the stone was black with old water.

A man sat on a boulder beside a dead fire, with a stick across his knees.

He did not stand up. He was seventy or eighty, thin, in a coat gone shapeless with wear, and his hands, resting on the stick, had knuckles like river stones. He looked at the pack, the press, the boots, and last of all at her face.

“You walked up the last hill,” he said.

“The truck couldn’t cross the drift.”

“No.” He said it without satisfaction. “What will you cut?”

“Nothing, if I can help it.”

“If you cannot help it?”

“Brush on the path. Nothing living that I don’t have to, and if I have to, I’ll write down what and where.” She shifted the pack. “The letter asked me to come on foot. That’s why the truck stopped where it stopped, and not because of the fridge.”

He considered that for a while. A bird called somewhere inside the canyon, twice, a hollow knock of a sound.

“Others came with saws,” Toma said. “They cut a way in. They went ahead of the rain and they did not come back out.”

“When was that?”

“Nine wet seasons.”

“And his body?”

“Not that either.” He got up. He was taller than he had looked sitting down. “What did he want?”

“I don’t know.”

“He took the wrong thing,” Toma said. “The valley has rules. Not laws. There is nobody to arrest you. The rules are just the way the water and the roots have arranged to keep living here, and if you don’t learn them you don’t break them, you break yourself.” He pointed his stick at the stream. “Water is already up. We have three hours of light. We sleep here and go in at first light.”

“I’d rather start now.”

“I know,” he said, and sat back down.

She made camp on flat ground above the copper stain, and ate bread and a boiled egg, and wrote by the last of the light. He watched her write with the interest of a man watching a tool he had seen used badly.

“What do you measure?”

“Everything I can,” Naledi said. “Water level. Slope. Seep lines. Which way the moss faces. Leaf counts. What’s flowering and what’s setting seed. The temperature at the soil and at the surface.” She wrote down his answer as well, then realised she had written nothing but the question, and stopped. “I came two days on buses to ask a village what it’s already asked me in a letter. What I don’t measure, I can’t compare. And what I can’t compare, I can’t promise.”

Toma was quiet for a long moment.

“Then at least you know the difference between a promise and a guess,” he said.

The mist came out of the canyon at dusk, not rising but pouring slowly, the way milk goes into tea, and it brought the copper with it, and under that the smell she could not yet name, green and bitter and turned over, like a field after a harrow. She sat with the notebook open and her pencil hovering over the first line of a page that was going to need a great many more.

In the last of the light she wrote the date, the place, and the water line on the stone measured by hand against her boot.

Then, at the bottom, because there was no sense pretending otherwise, she wrote: Rule one: unknown.

Behind her, the old man settled himself against the boulder with the stick across his knees, facing the gorge, and did not sleep, or did not seem to, all night.

In the morning they would walk in.
