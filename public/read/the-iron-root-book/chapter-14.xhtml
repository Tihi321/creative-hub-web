The Iron Root Keeps

The dry season came on quiet. The lake had drained back into the limestone shafts by the third week after the diversion, leaving the valley floor cracked and pale, the old stone channel dry again, the western slope blackened but holding.

The community plot on the terrace below the grove held forty plants now. Sixteen seedlings had gone in that first planting. Eight died. Eight lived. Naledi had transplanted eight more from the grove's eastern edge, lifting each one with a wide ball of mat and soil, and six of those had taken. She had transplanted eight more from the western edge, where the fire had scorched but not killed, and four of those held. Essa kept the arithmetic in her oiled-cloth book: sixteen in, eight dead, eight living; eight in, two dead, six living; eight in, four dead, four living. Forty plants in all. Thirty alive. The dates stood beside every number, and the weather, and the water given, measured in tin cups, and the mornings the shade frame had been moved, and the moth count on the host trees above.

Essa sat on the old wall with the book on her knees and the pencil on its string around her neck. She wrote the way she did everything, each letter separate, each number sitting directly above the one before it. She did not look up when Naledi came along the wall. She finished the line she was writing, capped the pencil, and turned.

'Doctor. The moths came back last night. First warm week. You said they would.'

'I said they might.'

'You said they would. I wrote it down.' She held the page up, and there it was, in Essa's small square hand, dated two weeks back. 'Hundreds on the old tree. Hundreds on the western edge, where the new root shows. Papa says the grove remembers.'

Naledi looked up the slope. The western host tree stood above the scorched ground with its bark pale against the blackened litter, and from its base a fresh root cable had pushed out through the ash, thick as a thumb and already dusted with the pale scales of moth wings. The mat was knitting itself back together. The moths had come back to the nursery chambers in the unburned middle, and now they were moving outward, settling on the new growth, laying their eggs in the fibres of it.

'Your grandfather knows the arithmetic,' Naledi said.

Essa smiled, a quick flash of teeth. 'Papa says arithmetic is what keeps a person honest. He says you taught him that.'

'I didn't teach him that. He taught me.'

Essa looked down at her book and smoothed the page with her thumb, and when she spoke again her voice had gone flat, in the way it went when she was telling a thing exactly as it had happened. 'He is sleeping more now. He says the valley is tended again. He says he can rest.'

Naledi felt the words settle in her chest. She had known it was coming. Toma had told her as much on the night the fever broke, sitting in the schoolroom with the lamp between them: that he had carried the fourth root for fifty years, and that seeing the plot voted to the village, and the grove standing, and Essa sitting up and asking questions, was the accounting he had been waiting for. He had not said he was dying. He had said he could rest, and she had heard the difference and had not wanted to write it down.

'Go and count the moths properly,' she said. 'The warm week matters. Write the date and the time at the top.'

Essa uncapped the pencil and bent to the page. Naledi watched her a moment — the straight back, the book held level on the slope of the wall — and then turned and went down toward the village.

The letter from the Board of Field Credentials had sat in her coat pocket for four months. She had carried it up the canyon, onto the shelf, into the grove, through the fire and the flood, into the schoolroom and out to the plot. Its weight had changed with the season. At first it had been a judgement. Then a distraction. Then a ghost. Now it was paper.

She took it out at the clinic table, under the window where the light fell clean, and broke the seal with her thumbnail. The letter was short. Professor Lena Marsh's signature at the foot of it, in the same level hand that had read out the eleven words with a clause number beside them three years before.

The panel had reviewed her case in the light of the emergency survey request she had submitted under the 1972 Survey Act, and in the light of the field reports forwarded by the truck man who had carried her letter down to the town-hall post and paid for the stamp himself. The panel noted that her unauthorised fieldwork in the Iron Basin had resulted in the identification of a novel therapeutic compound, the preservation of a unique symbiotic system, and the mitigation of a public health crisis in a recognised community. The panel voted six-to-one to restore her field credentials with full collecting privileges, effective immediately, and to append a commendation to her record. The letter invited her to return for a formal hearing at the beginning of the next academic term.

She read it twice. The words were smooth and official and absolving. They gave her back what had been taken. They did not mention Idris Kalu, or the fourteen months the retraction had taken, or the corridor where two former colleagues still crossed the floor to avoid her, or the door that had locked behind her with three people on the other side of the table and a recorder's red light holding steady. They mentioned no door at all.

She set the letter down and took a clean sheet from the back of her notebook, the part she kept for methods, and wrote.

To the Board of Field Credentials.

I accept the restoration of my credentials. I decline the hearing. I ask instead for the establishment of a research station in the Iron Basin, on the following terms. The station to be sited on land chosen by the council of Sorrow's Gate. All protocols to be approved by the council before they are begun. All material collected to remain the property of the village. All publications to carry the healer of Sorrow's Gate or his named successor as co-author. The station to train two residents a year in survey and propagation. No compound derived from the Iron Root or its host system to be patented by the station or by any body acting for it. No living material to go to any commercial party without the written consent of the council.

Dr Naledi Okonjo.

She folded the sheet, put it in a fresh envelope, wrote the address, and walked it down to the truck man, who came every Tuesday with salt and paraffin and who took letters out without asking what was in them. He read the envelope, put it in the tin box under the seat, and went off up the riverbed with the wheel complaining in the ruts.

She went back to the clinic and brewed leaf tea.

The next fever would come. It always came. The water carried things, and the dust on the flood-brought branch had been a spore, a mould that bloomed in new standing water after the first heavy rain, and the children who played in the pools had breathed it in. The leaf did not kill the mould. It held the body steady while the body did its own work. It bought days. It was not a cure and she never once called it one, not even on the evening when seventeen children came down from the edge and no one else died. She brewed fifteen leaves for eight minutes by the sand timer Toma had made, poured it into the tin cup, and set the cup on the shelf for Nuru Banda, who would know the dose and who would know which child.

She kept teaching, because a method that lives in one pair of hands is not a method.

Ruth Achieng learned the watering. One tin cup to a plant at first light, no more and no less, unless the soil at three centimetres was dry to the second knuckle, which she tested with the back of her finger, the way Naledi showed her. The boy with the hoe, whose name was Chidi and who had been named for Emeka's sister and who had cut his own channel thirty centimetres wide on the day of the diversion and had to be pulled out of it when it filled, learned the shade frame. He learned to move the thatch when the sun passed the third post and to lower it when the mist lifted before noon, and he learned it so well that by midsummer he was moving it before Naledi would have, and she stopped telling him and started writing down what he did.

Both of them learned the record book. Date, weather, water, shade, moth count on the old tree, leaf count on the plot, yellowing, new growth. Both of them learned to lift a seedling with a ball of mat and root the size of a football, to carry it level on a board, to set it at the same depth it had grown at, to firm the ground with the flat of the hand and not the heel of the boot.

'If I am away,' Naledi told them, on the morning she taped a copy of the rules to the clinic wall, 'the plot does not stop. The counting does not stop. The moths do not stop for us.'

Ruth nodded. Chidi nodded. They had both sat up through the fever nights. They had cut the fire line at the wall. They had stood in the rain and watched the western branch go down the old cut and out onto the low ground. They knew what stopping cost, and they did not intend to pay it again.

The dry season deepened. The copper smell went out of the air and the air turned to dust and dry leaf, and the stream in the canyon ran clear and thin over the pale stones. The hornbills nested again on the ledge in the canyon wall, and their call came down the gorge in the early mornings, a wooden knock and a low crease of sound, and the children learned to answer it and were told not to.

The night stalker came up once in the dry season and was seen on the upper slope at dusk, moving through the litter at the western edge of the grove, where the new root showed. The animal with the broad pad and the four forward toes and the fifth set back, with no claw marks in the print, went through the grove and did not come near the village, and left a track line Naledi copied into the notebook the next morning, and the chain held the way it had held before there was anyone in the valley to write it down: the figs fruited, the hens ate the figs, the stalker took a hen, the boar turned the ground where the stalker had been, and the valley went on breathing.

Toma died in his own bed in the autumn, three days after the first rain of the new wet season brought the copper back into the air.

He had been up on the wall the day before, with Essa, counting moths, and he had come down and eaten a little and slept. In the night his breathing changed, and his granddaughter Amara, who was a small girl of nine and who had gone up the slope on the fourth morning of the fever and had broken on the sixth night and had lived, sat with him and held the cup and did not wake the house. In the morning he was gone. He had not been in pain. He had folded his hands on the blanket and gone the way an old tree goes, standing, and when Naledi came up the lane Nuru was already at the door and did not need to say anything.

The clinic book showed his last entry, in the hand he had learned from his mother and had never once written in a straight line: Moth count, west edge, 347. New root, 12 cm. Valley kept.

They buried him on the terrace, above the flood line and below the mist, with the old wall running along the lower edge of the plot and the forty plants in their rows above him and the grove standing further up the slope where he had walked for eighty years.

The village came. Baro came and carried water in the shop's two tin cans and would not put them down, and stood through the whole of it with one can in each hand. Renn's foreman came from the town at the head of the valley, walking with a stick, his foot healed crooked from the break he had taken on the burnt slope, and stood at the back and said nothing. The truck man came. Nuru Banda came, and Ruth, and Chidi, and the household heads who had signed the contract and the household heads who had voted against it, and the four who had shut their doors and had opened them again over a pot of tea and a question about the yam harvest. The children came in a row, tallest at the back, and Essa stood at the front of them with her record book held flat against her chest, because it was the only thing she had that was worth bringing.

Naledi spoke three sentences. She said that Toma had carried the four roots for fifty years and had put down the last of them in the year the valley was tended again. She said that the plot was his arithmetic and not hers, and that she had only written it down. She said that the moth count on the old tree stood at three hundred and forty-seven, and that he had put the number in the book himself the day before, and that the valley was kept.

Then Essa read from her book, in the flat voice her grandfather had taught her, because a number without a time is a story about a number and she was not going to tell a story.

'First planting, sixteen in. Eight dead. Eight living. Second planting, eight in. Two dead. Six living. Third planting, eight in. Four dead. Four living. Forty plants. Thirty alive. Moths returned on the first warm week. Grove standing.'

She did not cry. She had decided not to, and she did not. She read the numbers out and closed the book and stepped back, and the numbers stood behind her in the air without needing anyone to say anything about them.

After the burial, Naledi and Essa walked up the slope to the grove.

Essa carried a host sapling in a wrapped ball of mat and soil. She had grown it from seed she had collected herself from the old tree's first fruiting after the fire, off the low western branch, in the week the moths came back. She had raised it in a pot on the clinic windowsill, watering it by the rule and shading it by the frame and writing its growth in her book in millimetres, and now it was the height of her forearm and had nine leaves on it, and she had counted them four times over.

Naledi carried the spade.

They chose the place together, on the western edge above the burnt litter, where the new root cable came out of the ground and ran over the rock. The moths were on the cable in a pale line, a hundred of them, settled with their wings flat.

Essa put the pot down and knelt and set the sapling beside the hole to check it for straightness, the way Naledi had taught her. Then she stood back and Naledi cut the hole, wide and shallow, the way the mat wanted it, and Essa lifted the sapling out of the pot with both hands under the soil and set it in, and filled the hole with the loose mat and firmed it with the flat of her hand. She took the two tin cups off the strap and walked down to the channel — the old channel, running clear and low the way the old people had built it to run — and came back with the water and gave the sapling both cups.

Then she took the pencil from its string and turned the tag over on her knee and wrote, in her separate letters, with the date beneath: Planted for Papa. West edge. Moths present.

She tied the tag to the stem with a strip of oiled cloth and stood up.

'Will it remember him?' she said.

Naledi looked at the old tree, and the new cable, and the moths moving on the new growth in the last of the light. She thought about the four roots that Toma had been present for, and the one he had pulled with his own hand, and the fifty years after it, and the moths he had counted for every one of those evenings. She thought about the leaf tea, and the plot, and the letter she had sent down the riverbed with terms in it. She thought about the difference between a thing that is taken and a thing that is kept, which is mostly a difference in the arithmetic, and about how long it takes a person to learn the arithmetic, and how the learning never happens all at once.

'The ground remembers,' she said. 'It remembers what it was asked to carry. That is all remembering is, in the end. You stay where the thing happened and you do not move.' She put the spade over her shoulder. 'He is in the ground here now. The moths will find the root and go on laying on it. The root will carry the leaf. That is the whole of what keeps anything.'

Essa looked at the sapling for a moment longer, and then bent down and took a handful of the loose black litter at its foot and let it run through her fingers, the way her grandfather had let soil run, and made a note of something in her head without writing it, and turned to go.

They walked down the terrace together, past the forty plants and the shade frames and the old wall, and the children were sitting on the wall in a row, waiting, because word had gone round that there was a planting and a tag, and they wanted to see it. Essa stopped and told them what was on the tag, and its date, and then she sat down in the middle of the row with her book on her knees, and the smallest girl asked whether the moths ate the leaves, and Essa began to explain in the flat and patient way of a girl who has been asked the same question fourteen times and intends to answer it properly the fifteenth.

Naledi went down to the clinic and set the spade in the corner.

Nuru Banda was at the table with the clinic book open, and on the shelf above her the tin cup stood where Naledi had left it that morning, and beside the cup lay the letter from the Board of Field Credentials, folded once, in the pocket of the coat that hung on the nail by the door.

'The truck man came back,' Nuru said, without looking up. 'He says he carried your letter to the town hall post and it went out on the Friday bus. He says he left the outside of it in the box, in case you want the date.'

'I don't need the date.'

'He says you do not owe him anything for it and he would not take it if you offered.' Nuru turned a page and wrote a figure in the nurse's column. 'He says you paid him in the flood, and he keeps accounts the same way we do.'

Naledi hung her coat on the nail and sat down at the table and drew the second chair up with her foot, and pulled the clinic book across, and looked at the two columns side by side, the same figures in two hands, hers and Nuru's. Then she turned to a clean page and drew the head of a new column, and wrote at the top of it, in her own hand, in the letters she used for anything she meant to keep: Plot record. Method. To be kept in this valley.

Below the line she wrote the rules, one to a line, the way Toma had said them from the wall on the dry evenings, with the children's feet hanging over the stone.

Take the leaf and never the root. Take from the outside of the stand and never the middle. One in four, and leave the oldest alone, because it remembers. Clear the cut in the dry, and never in the wet, and never cut the seep, because the seep is the spring and the spring is the village. The old wall is for the crossing and for stopping what burns, and it does not carry water and it never did. Leave the moths, and leave the small ones under the mat, and do not walk on the mat with your boots on. And if you cannot say why a rule is there, keep it anyway and find out later.

She put the pencil down and looked at the page for a while.

'This goes on the wall of the schoolroom,' she said, 'when the school opens again in the dry.'

'It goes on the wall of the clinic too,' Nuru said. 'I have sick children in here and mothers who have never been told why the leaf is dosed the way it is dosed, and if they are going to carry the cup up the lane in the dark they should be able to read the rule that made the cup.'

Naledi copied it out again for the clinic wall, in larger letters, and while she wrote the rain came back onto the roof, the first steady rain of the new wet season, and the two of them sat with the lamp between them and the pages and the tin cup and the column of figures, and listened to the water going down the old stone cut on the western side, doing exactly what a hundred years of older hands had arranged for it to do.

Outside, the valley was keeping. The forty plants stood on the terrace, thirty of them alive and ten of them teaching the method, and the new sapling on the western edge had its tag and its two cups of water and the moths already moving on the litter around it. The old tree stood above the burnt ground with the root cable running out of it, and the small ones were in their chambers under the mat, and the hornbills were on their ledge in the canyon, and the water was in the channel and out of the village, and the arithmetic was written down in two books in two hands, with the dates against it.

The root was still in the ground. That had been the point of all of it.

Naledi had been given back a door, and had decided, in the end, not to walk through it on the terms offered, and to build a different one in the place where she happened to be standing. It was not what she had come for when she stepped off the first bus. It was better than what she had come for, and she knew that, and she was still going to have to write the letter about it in the morning, and there was a row of six that wanted water at first light, and Essa was on the wall with a book and a question, and a fever was going to come again in some wet season, the way it always did.

She turned the lamp down and went out into the rain to see whether the new sapling had taken.
