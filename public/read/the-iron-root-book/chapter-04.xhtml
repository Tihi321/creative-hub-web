Reading the Forest

They left at first light with two days' food, a lamp each, and a coil of cord Toma had cut from a fallen fence. The mist stood in the lane as they passed the last house, and the wet came off the roofs in slow drops, and above them the valley closed its mouth and did not show its teeth.

The climb to the upper basin took the first three hours. The lower path was a braid of channels and old landslip; Naledi set a zigzag course along the contact of the limestone and the clay, reading the seep lines the way a navigator reads the colour of the water. Where the clay thinned, the water ran clear and fast. Where it thickened and domed, the water came out in beads and the moss was browner. A path was nothing here. A seep told you what was under you, and she trusted the seep.

Toma walked behind her and said very little. At the third switchback he stopped to rest, both hands on the stick, breathing through his nose, and when she turned to wait he spoke as if continuing a conversation they had not been having.

"You know the plant I would not take you to see."

"You did not say a plant. You said the wrong end of the question."

"That is the same thing said longer."

He started walking again, and she followed, and they did not say any more for a while. The canopy came down over the path in patches of pale leaves and thin branches, and the mist thinned, and the ground grew a little drier underfoot the way ground does when it has been raised up above the flood.

They came at last to a stand of tall trees, and in the crotch of the largest one, three metres up, there was a nest.

Naledi heard the birds before she saw them: a deep hollow lowing, two or three notes at once, the sound of a thing speaking from a cave. She put her hand on Toma's arm and they both stopped.

Two birds came out of the trees. They were as big as a large dog would be if a dog could stand on the air, black in their wings and body, dark in the head and neck with a wide pale bill and a casque like a blade on top, and they came at the two of them not in a rush but with a terrible slow purpose, swinging side to side and low to the branch, and every swing took them closer. Their wings made a drumming sound that went into Naledi's chest before it went into her ears. Then one of them dropped off the branch and tilted toward her, and she saw the throat pouch fill, and it started a long low call that was not a warning but a statement, made in the valley's own voice, and the statement was the whole stand of trees.

"Stay still," Toma said.

"I am staying still."

"Do not look at the nest. Look at the one that is coming."

The bird that was coming came down to the ground, and the other stayed on the branch and displayed, opening the wings to show the white under them, and the casque caught the light, and it took two steps toward them across wet leaf litter and stopped. It tilted its head. It did not tilt its eye away. Naledi looked at the bird and did not move, and the bird looked at her, and she understood, in a way that was not verbal, that the bird was not deciding to attack her. The bird was deciding whether she was going to keep coming.

She was not.

"We go around," she said. "Behind those trees to the west and back to the seep line above the nest."

Toma stood beside her and looked at the bird's feet, not its face, which was what she had seen him do in the village when Baro was shouting, and he did not move.

"Yes," he said. "We go around."

They backed away, one slow step at a time, four steps, six, ten, and the bird on the ground let them get to fifteen and then flapped up to the lower branch and refolded its wings and continued to watch them. The other one went quiet.

It took them twenty minutes to walk the longer way, up over a rise of rock, down through a wet gutter, along the far side of the stand, and back to the contact line above the nest. Twice Naledi put her foot through a hollow under the leaf litter and caught herself on a root cable. She counted her plants all the way. A stand where a pair of hornbills nests will be a stand where everything else is quiet too — she noted that at a pause on the rise, because it was true and because she wanted to know what it meant later.

At the far edge of the stand, above the contact line, Toma spoke again.

"Most outsiders," he said, "would have driven the birds off with a stick and a shout."

"Would they."

"Yes. And the birds would have gone off and come back at their backs and they would have run. And running on this ground means the wet channel lower down. That is the way most outsiders do not come back."

She stopped and looked back down along the way they had come, and there was the wet channel, a black thread of rock and rushing water where the mist had not quite filled it in, running exactly where a person running downhill would try to go.

"That is a very expensive lesson," she said.

"It is free," Toma said. "You paid attention instead of money."

Air moved up the valley after noon in a way she did not like. She could not say it was wind. It was the mist beginning to move in the wrong direction, and the top leaves of the stand turning the same way at the same time, and then the small sounds of the forest going thin.

She stood still and listened. There was a bee in her ear and Toma's stick on the rock and Toma's breathing. And then there was not even the bee.

"Stop," she said.

Toma stopped.

The water in the channel to their left — a side channel, dry enough that morning to cross on stones — was now discoloured, and that was the first thing she read. The water had been clear and green. Now it was brown and grey and running with a slight forward lean that meant it had picked up speed in the last hour somewhere above them. That was not rain in the channel. That was rain higher up, in the upper bowl, where the catchment was, and it was coming down.

"Feel the ground," she said.

He put his hand flat on the rock, which was the thing she had watched him do to Emeka's head, and he looked at her.

"Yes," he said.

The hush was the second thing. The hush was total. Every small mouth in the stand had shut.

"Up and right," Naledi said. "Not the channel. The shelf."

The shelf was six metres up the slope, a ledge of harder rock under an overhang, and it was above the flood line that she had marked on her map in the second page of her notebook, and above either of the canyon marks she had measured on the way in, and it would be dry, and their feet would slip going up it.

They went. Naledi went first, and found handholds that were not handholds, and put her weight on a wet root cable and lifted, and Toma came behind her, slower, one leg worse than the other, and she turned back once and held out her hand and he waved her off without speaking, and then they were on the shelf and he was sitting on it with his back to the rock face and his stick over his knees and his chest going up and down.

She was still standing, watching the channel, when it came.

It did not come with a roar. It came with a steady, growing weight of sound, like a truck in the next valley, and then it was in the channel below them and it was not water as she had been walking beside it all morning. It was a brown wall two metres high and three-quarters as wide, and in its front edge there were stones, whole trees, a fence post, one of the small flags from her own string line on the seep, and all of it went past them in the time it took to count to fifteen, and it took the floor of the channel with it, and then it was gone and there was a new channel where the old one had been, wider and stonier, still moving at speed.

Naledi looked at Toma. Toma looked at the new channel.

"You saw the colour," he said.

"The colour and the quiet."

"The quiet is the one most people cannot hear," he said. "You heard it because you were listening for it."

She sat down on the shelf beside him, because standing was no longer sensible, and she took out her notebook and the pencil and, because the pencil shook, she steadied her wrist with her other hand.

She noted: Presence of hornbills marks a stand worth not disturbing; do not drive birds, read their posture, withdraw to rear, route round. She recorded: Sudden hush precedes flash flood. Discoloured side channel is early warning. Go up and back from the channel, not down and along. And under both of those, because she was honest, and because the honesty cost her nothing on this quiet shelf:

Both escapes were methods, not luck. Next time: leave earlier, cross to the contact shelf before noon, and do not pass below a hornbill stand during the run-off hours.

Toma was quiet, so she looked at him, and he was watching her write. He did not look like a man who had just been carried out of a flood. He looked like a man who had seen something he had been waiting to see.

"You write down the mistakes."

"I write down what to do as well."

"You write down the mistakes as the same thing as what to do."

She looked at the two pages. The mistakes were, in fact, the same thing as the method. She had written the crossing wrong and the crossing right on one page, and the page was better for it.

"Yes," she said.

He nodded, once, and looked back at the new channel.

The rain began again at mid-afternoon, thin and steady, and the sound of it on the leaves above the shelf was the sound of a room with a tap left running at the far end of it. Below them the side channel was still moving, brown and deep, and would keep the floor of the upper basin cut off for the rest of the day. Above them the slope ran up through wet rock and thin trees toward a line of pale trunks she could now see from the shelf, unaccountably straight, standing in a dark band of leaf litter that did not look like leaf litter, at the height above the flood mark that Toma had told her two days ago and that she had drawn in ink on the back page of her survey, because she drew things in ink once she had confirmed them, and the line of pale trunks was one of those things.

The grove she was looking for lay beyond the flooded shelf, upslope. She could see it now, a stand of trees whose ground did not appear to have a ground, which is what you see when a root mat lies over rock, and within the stand there was a colour she could not name at this distance — a paleness over the litter, low, like a dusting of flour left out in the rain.

She put the notebook away in the waxed bag.

"Tonight we cannot cross," Toma said, meaning the channel.

"No."

"Sleep on the shelf, keep the lamp low. The stalker works the lower slopes this season, not here. You will not be dry."

"I have never been dry," Naledi said.

The rain went on. In the fading light the small pale dust down in the grove was still there, patient, waiting to be named, and lower down in the village a fever was in a small room with a girl in it who had stopped recognising her grandfather's voice, and the channel below the shelf went on moving at a terrible speed, and nobody in the village had heard the sound of it because the sound of it had been eaten by the rain.

"Tomorrow," Toma said, "we go up."

Naledi lay on the shelf with the waxed bag for a pillow and the cord around her wrist in a loop, and looked at the palest line above the wet rock and counted what she had, and what she had was two escapes, four pages, a name for a mat that held the rain, a list of days, and a hand of certainty about where to look. It was not a cure. It was a direction. Given the morning, it might become a sentence.

The rain came down on the leaves and did not stop.
