Bread, Lemons

Ivka's shop, late morning

Ambience: wind rattling a shop window, a wall clock ticking

Sound: a small brass bell jangling over a door

The only door on the riva with a light behind it. Inside it is warm. It smells of bread and paraffin, and of the dried figs strung on a cord behind the till.

Rope, batteries, washing powder, three kinds of tinned fish, and one kind of everything else. A wall clock ticks over the door, five minutes fast, as it has been since 1990.

On screen: Mara (raincoat dripping on the mat), Ivka (behind the counter, black cardigan, glasses on their chain, hands folded, doing nothing very attentively)

Ivka sits behind the counter doing nothing, in a way that looks exactly like surveillance.

IVKA Mala.

IVKA (looking her up and down) You're thin.

MARA Same as August.

IVKA You were thin in August.

MARA Good to see you too, Ivka.

IVKA Sit down. No, don't sit down, you'll drip on the stool. Stand on the mat. What do you want?

MARA Bread.

She takes a loaf from the shelf and sets it on the counter.

IVKA Yesterday's. Today's didn't come. The ferry brought chickens and no bread.

MARA Yesterday's is fine.

IVKA It's not fine. It's yesterday's. But you can have it.

Then, before she has decided to, Mara takes two lemons from the crate by the counter and sets them beside the loaf.

Ivka looks at the lemons. Then she looks at Mara over the top of her glasses, and keeps looking.

IVKA (dry) Who's drinking tea?

MARA Nobody's drinking tea.

IVKA Then who are the lemons for?

MARA They're... I don't know. They're lemons.

Mara finds the photo on her phone and holds it out across the counter.

Ivka takes the phone and reads at full arm's length, her glasses forgotten on their chain. Her lips move on the last line.

She hands the phone back screen down.

IVKA (airily) Bread, lemons. Half the island buys bread and lemons.

MARA On a Monday?

IVKA Who said Monday?

MARA It printed on Monday night.

Ivka rings up the loaf on a cash till older than Mara. The drawer opens with a bang that makes the figs swing.

IVKA Your grandfather bought bread and two lemons every Monday for thirty-five years.

IVKA Then Katica died. And he bought bread and two lemons every Monday for fifteen more.

MARA (quiet) Fifteen years.

IVKA Every Monday. Ferry or no ferry. If the bread didn't come, he bought the lemons and came back for the bread on Tuesday.

Ivka reaches past the crate, down to the bottom of the box underneath, and picks out two heavier lemons. She swaps them for Mara's without comment.

She slips them into a paper bag and folds the top over twice, sharp, like a letter.

IVKA I asked him once. I said, Ante, who are the lemons for?

IVKA He said, she liked lemon in her tea.

IVKA (with a shrug) I said, Ante, she's not drinking tea.

IVKA He said, you don't know that.

Mara looks at the paper bag. She does not pick it up.

MARA What did you say to that?

IVKA What do you say to that? I gave him his change.

MARA And the apology?

IVKA (innocent) What apology?

MARA One apology to Ivka. It's on the paper. You read it. Your lips moved.

IVKA My lips move when I read. I'm eighty. Everything moves.

IVKA Many people owe me apologies, mala. I don't keep a list.

IVKA Eleven forty.

She counts change out of a biscuit tin, coin by coin, as if each one were being returned with regret.

MARA Ivka. Do you know who's doing this?

IVKA Doing what?

She is already looking past Mara, at the window, where the wind throws spray over the bollards.

IVKA (brisk) You'll want batteries. The power goes when it blows like this.

MARA I don't need batteries.

IVKA Double A or the big ones?

MARA (giving up) ...The big ones.

Ivka puts four batteries in a second bag without being told how many.

IVKA And candles. And matches, his are damp. He kept them by the sink, I told him, Ante, not by the sink.

MARA How do you know where he kept his matches?

IVKA Everybody knows where everybody keeps their matches. Fourteen sixty.

Mara pays. Ivka counts the change again, slower.

IVKA And eat something. Not lemons.

MARA I'm going out to the lighthouse.

IVKA (not looking up) In this?

MARA In this.

IVKA Take the bread inside your coat. The wind takes bread.

The causeway, early afternoon

Ambience: a hard gusting wind over open sea, waves breaking on stone

Four hundred metres of fitted pale stone run out into a dark sea. An iron rail stands on the sea side, cold enough to hurt. Spray comes over in sheets.

At the far end, on its rock, the white tower waits. In this wind the walk is four hundred metres of being shoved.

Picture: Mara bent double on the stone causeway in the bora, bread under her raincoat, the white lighthouse ahead

On screen: Mara (bent into the wind, raincoat snapping, the paper bag clutched to her chest)

She walks bent double with the bread inside the raincoat, like something smuggled. The lemons knock against her ribs.

MARA (shouting at nobody) Thing not worth shouting about, number one!

A gust shoves her a full step sideways. She grabs the rail.

MARA Point taken.

Salt dries on her lips. Every wave that hits the stones sends up a white wall, and the wind carries it over her in a fine cold rain.

MARA Two hundred metres.

MARA Two hundred metres of fitted stone. He walked this twice a day for thirty years. In this.

MARA (between gusts) And came home and said, "A bit breezy."

At the end of the causeway: the rock, the tower, a steel door with a padlock, and three old flowerpots lined up against the wall, out of the wind.

MARA (counting) One. Two. Three.

She tips the third pot. Nothing has grown in it since the eighties. The earth inside is dry and grey as ash.

The key is there, where it has been all her life, in a little nest of dry earth.

MARA (quiet) Of course it is.

MARA Thirty years of "don't tell anyone about the flowerpot", and it's still the third flowerpot.

Sound: a heavy padlock snapping open and a steel door grinding inward

Service room, Tuesday afternoon

Ambience: wind moaning round a stone tower, a faint electrical hum

A round whitewashed room, cold as a cellar. It smells of hot dust, which means something in here is on. One bare bulb hangs from a flex.

On a steel desk squats the grey teleprinter, the size of a sewing machine. A roll of yellowed paper feeds up through it.

Beside it stands the receiver: a cream metal box with one big dial and two small lamps, one red, one green.

On screen: Mara (hood back, hair wet, crouched at eye level with the receiver)

Close-up: two scraps of old masking tape under two small lamps, lettered MARK and SPACE

Under the red lamp, in her grandfather's capitals: MARK. Under the green: SPACE.

MARA (softly) Hello, you two.

She straightens and looks the room over like a fault she has been called out to.

A telephone socket on the wall, its cable cut years ago and taped over. The teleprinter doesn't need it.

A cable runs from the receiver to a small box between the two machines. Another climbs the wall and goes up through the ceiling, to the aerial on the gallery.

She puts her hand on the receiver. It is warm.

MARA Every switch on.

MARA (dry) Of course. You never turned off anything that might come in useful later.

She talks herself through it, because talking helps. It always has.

MARA Right. Two tones. Frequency-shift keying.

MARA One tone's a one, the other's a zero. Five bits make a letter.

MARA The box in the middle turns the tones into pulses. The pulses drive the printer.

She taps the paper roll. It is blank above the tear where Dino took his strip.

MARA So somebody, somewhere, has a transmitter on this frequency.

Her voice comes back to her too big from the curved walls, as if the room is repeating it to check.

MARA (quieter) And a sense of humour.

She turns the big dial a hair to the left, and a hair back, to where it was. The pencil mark on the panel says it was right where he left it.

MARA You marked it. Of course you marked it.

She sits down on an upturned crate and looks at the two lamps.

MARA I was eight. You sat me on this crate.

MARA You said the lamps were a married couple. They had to agree before anyone could speak.

MARA (rueful) I asked what happened if they didn't agree.

MARA You said, then nobody says anything. Like at home.

She laughs out loud, once, and stops.

The wind tries the door. The bulb sways a little on its flex.

MARA (standing up) Right. It printed at night. So I come back at night.

MARA Thermos. Stove. Back before dark.

Ante's kitchen, dusk

Ambience: wind pushing at shutters, an old fridge humming

The kitchen is grey with evening. Through the salted window, out at the end of the causeway, the lighthouse switches itself on.

On screen: Mara (kneeling at the cast-iron stove, raincoat on, matches in hand)

She has fetched wood from outside at last, three armfuls, and got soaked by the spray for every one.

She strikes a match from the box by the sink. It does nothing. She strikes another. Nothing.

MARA (through her teeth) Not by the sink, Ante.

She uses Ivka's matches. The first one catches.

The stove smokes and sulks. It will not draw. She opens the window a crack and the smoke goes the wrong way.

MARA Fine. Smoke.

She fills the thermos with coffee, wraps the bread in a tea towel, puts the lemons on the windowsill, both of them, side by side.

She looks at them there.

MARA Don't look at me like that.

She packs the batteries with two candles and the thermos, and goes back out into the dark.

Service room, nine o'clock

Ambience: wind leaning on a steel door, waves far below

Night. The bulb is the only light inside. Every half minute the beam of the lamp overhead goes out across the water and comes back, and the whole tower seems to breathe with it.

The bora leans on the door like somebody waiting to be let in.

On screen: Mara (on the crate, raincoat buttoned to the chin, thermos cup in both hands)

MARA Nine o'clock.

MARA Nothing.

MARA (to the teleprinter) You're not dead, you know. Dino's very upset about it.

The teleprinter, which has said nothing for twenty years, goes on saying it.

She pours more coffee. It is mostly warm.

MARA If it's a prank, it's a good one. You'd need a transmitter. Two metres. Something that does forty-five baud. That's not a kid with a walkie-talkie.

MARA That's a museum.

She looks at the receiver, which is also a museum.

MARA Nine thirty.

MARA If it's kids, they're in bed.

MARA If it's Luka, he's doing a crossword in pen.

She checks her phone. No signal out here, which is fine, because there is no one she would ring.

MARA (quietly) Ring Mara Sunday.

The beam goes round. The wind tries the door. Far below, a wave hits the rock, and the floor hums with it.

MARA Nine forty-five.

MARA Nothing is going to happen and I'm going to walk back along that thing in the dark and Dino is going to ask me in the morning, in his official capacity...

MARA Nine fifty-eight.

She puts the cup down on the desk, very carefully, as if it might be the thing that set the machine off.

Service room, ten o'clock

At ten exactly the red lamp flickers.

On screen: Mara (frozen on the crate, staring at the receiver)

MARA (whisper) Mark.

Then the green lamp flickers.

MARA Space.

Picture: Mara half risen from the crate as the red and green lamps of the receiver glow together, bright and equal

Then both lamps steady together, equally bright, and agree.

Sound: a teleprinter clattering awake with a crash like a filing cabinet falling downstairs

The teleprinter wakes. The carriage slams. Mara stands up so fast the crate goes over behind her.

MARA Okay. Okay, okay, okay.

The keys hammer. Capitals climb out of the platen, line after line of the same two letters.

MARA (breathless) RY. RYRYRY. That's the test pattern.

MARA (fast, to no one) It works every part of the printer, so you know it's all...

She stops herself. The carriage slams back. The paper jumps a line.

The test pattern ends. For one second the room holds only the wind.

Then the machine types a heading, slowly, at the speed of a careful old man.

Close-up: teleprinter paper with a row of RYRYRY and, below it, a heading in capitals

Under the row of RYRYRY, in fresh capitals: BOATS THAT DID NOT COME BACK.

MARA (barely aloud) Nono?

The machine draws breath, and the next line begins.
