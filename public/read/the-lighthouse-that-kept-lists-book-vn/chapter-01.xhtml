The Afternoon Ferry

Ferry deck, late on Monday afternoon

Ambience: ferry engine thrumming under a gusting sea wind

The sea is grey-green and combed into white stripes. The little ferry takes the wind broadside and groans, a long iron groan you feel in your back teeth.

Spray rattles on the rail. Somewhere below, a crate of chickens complains about every wave.

Picture: Mara alone at the ferry rail in the huge yellow raincoat, the white lighthouse ahead

A woman in a yellow raincoat three sizes too big stands at the rail, alone on the open deck.

On screen: Mara (yellow raincoat swamping her, hood down, hair whipped flat)

MARA (to herself) Two days of bora. Of course.

The ferry left Split at two. It will reach Galebnjak at a quarter to five. In summer they call it the afternoon crossing. In November it is just the ferry.

The cabin behind her smells of diesel and somebody's sardines. She has chosen the wind.

She rolls the cuffs of the raincoat back twice. They unroll themselves at once and hang past her fingertips.

MARA (dry) You did that too. Every time you got going on an explanation.

MARA Sleeves down, hands up. "Now, Mara. The photocell."

A wave slaps the hull. The deck tilts and comes back. The chickens below disagree with it loudly.

A man in a flat cap carries the crate up the stairs. He sets it down out of the spray and looks at the sea with satisfaction.

From the look on his face this is nothing, a breeze, and he has seen it worse in August.

MARA (politely) Of course.

The man goes back down. The chickens stay, and go on disagreeing.

MARA (to the chickens) I know. Nobody asked us either.

The wind comes off the mainland in shoves, not in a stream. Each shove leans on the ferry, and the ferry leans back.

Mara holds the rail with both hands and lets it push her about. It is easier than sitting in the cabin being looked at.

Galebnjak rises out of the grey: low and grey itself, the harbour first, then the houses stacked up the hill like crates, then the bare top of the island with its stone walls and nothing else.

And at the end of a long stone causeway, on its own rock, stands the lighthouse, white as a tooth. Not lit yet.

MARA (quiet) It comes on at dusk by itself.

MARA Photocell and relay. Replaced in ninety-seven.

MARA He told me about that relay every summer. Every single summer, Nono.

MARA (in his voice, gruff) "Twenty-four volt coil, Mara. You never put a twelve volt coil in a twenty-four volt circuit."

MARA (her own voice) No, Nono.

She lifts her phone and frames the lighthouse. The tower sits in the middle of the screen, a white chip very far away.

She puts the phone away without taking the picture.

MARA Fine. One week.

MARA (counting on her fingers) Bin bags. Masking tape. An estate agent in Split.

MARA Who says he came out to see the house in the spring, and already has plans. A jacuzzi on the terrace.

MARA (dry) Nono would have loved that.

MARA Sort. Bag. Label. Sell.

MARA Then the ferry home, and Monday morning, a meeting about antenna masts in Velika Gorica, and nobody on this whole boat will know my name.

A gull hangs beside the rail at her eye level, not flapping, just held up by the wind, and looks at her as if it knows her name perfectly well.

MARA Don't.

Sound: the ferry's deep horn sounding twice

The ferry turns into the harbour mouth, and the wind lets go of her all at once, so suddenly that she staggers.

The riva, a quarter to five on Monday

Ambience: wind gusting along a harbour front, rigging ticking on masts

The stone harbour front is wet with spray and empty. The pale paving shines. Fishing boats knock together at their moorings, and their rigging ticks against the masts.

Every café on the riva has stacked its chairs on its tables and chained them together.

Sea smoke drifts low over the water. One window across the harbour is lit.

On screen: Mara (raincoat flapping, laptop bag on her shoulder, holdall in one hand)

MARA (deadpan) Chained. In case the chairs make a run for it.

She walks down the ramp. The man with the chickens passes her and goes off along the riva without looking back.

Nobody meets the ferry. Nobody is on the riva at all, except a cat on a bollard and a plastic bag the wind walks along the stones like a dog.

MARA I didn't tell anyone which day I was coming.

MARA (sighing) So everyone knows.

The harbour office is dark. The café is dark. The church on the hill is dark.

Across the harbour, behind the one lit window, a curtain moves and falls still.

MARA (not looking that way) Hello, Ivka.

The cat jumps down from the bollard and walks off in front of her, tail up, as if it has been sent to show her the way and is embarrassed about it.

MARA I know the way.

The cat does not care. It goes up the lane first.

She shifts the holdall to the other hand and follows it, up the steep lane between the shuttered stone houses, past the green doors, past the walls with the figs growing out of them, up to the house with the tide table nailed beside the door.

Ante's kitchen, Monday night

Ambience: wind pushing at shutters, an old fridge humming

A small kitchen with whitewashed walls, and on every wall a list. The lamp is yellow and weak. Salt has dried on the window in grey ferns.

The house smells of pipe tobacco given up in 2004. Under that, machine oil and oranges.

On screen: Mara (still in the raincoat, standing just inside the door, very still)

MARA (quiet) Hi, Nono.

Nobody answers, which is what she came for.

She does not take off the raincoat. She reads the fridge door instead, because it is there.

MARA (reading) Bulbs, twenty-four volt. Coffee. Ring Mara Sunday.

MARA I rang you. Sundays. You talked about relays.

MARA Every Sunday. I'd ask how you were, and you'd say, "The relay is holding up."

She opens the fridge. A jar of olives, half a lemon gone hard as wood, a bottle of something homemade with no label.

MARA Half a lemon.

She shuts the fridge, quite quickly.

By the door hangs the tide table, every high water ringed in his pencil, little notes in the margins in capitals. NEAP. SPRING. WIND SW, IGNORE TABLE.

Her hand goes out to it and stops short.

In the bedroom doorway, over the bed, a framed list hangs a little crooked.

Close-up: a framed handwritten list headed THINGS NOT WORTH SHOUTING ABOUT

THINGS NOT WORTH SHOUTING ABOUT, in Ante's capitals, fourteen items long. Number one: THE BORA.

MARA (almost smiling) Number one. Obviously.

MARA (reading down) Two. The ferry. Three. Plovput. Four. Prices in Split.

MARA Seven. Football. Nine. The bishop.

MARA Eleven. Mara's driving.

MARA (indignant) I passed first time.

The fourteenth line has been rubbed out and written again, and she cannot make out either version.

She tilts her head. She tilts the frame. The grey smudge stays a grey smudge.

MARA Fourteen things. You could have just said "nothing".

She straightens the frame, which makes it crooked the other way, and leaves it.

She goes back to the kitchen table and sets down what she has brought: a roll of black bin bags, a roll of masking tape, a card with a phone number.

MARA (businesslike) Bin bags. Tape. Estate agent in Split.

MARA Sort, bag, label, sell. Four verbs. One week.

She stares at the three things on the table until the fridge clicks off.

Then at the lists on the wall: dozens of them with curling edges, all in capitals, all in his hand.

MARA I'm supposed to put those in bags.

MARA (to the bin bags) Not tonight.

She looks at the cold stove.

MARA The stove needs wood. The wood is outside.

The wind throws itself against the shutters, and the whole house creaks like a boat.

MARA (flatly) And the outside is the bora.

She turns off the lamp and goes to bed in the raincoat.

In the dark the fridge hums, stops, and starts again.

Ante's kitchen, eight on Tuesday morning

Grey morning light through the salted window. The bin bags are still on the table, still rolled.

Mara is at the gas ring in yesterday's raincoat, waiting for the coffee pot, which takes its time, the way it always did.

On screen: Mara (raincoat over pyjamas, hair flattened on one side, half awake)

MARA (to the pot) Come on. You have one job.

Sound: three firm knocks on a wooden door

MARA (to the pot) That's not for us.

The knock comes again, exactly the same three knocks, as if the first set had been filed and copied.

She turns off the gas with a sigh and opens the door.

The harbour master fills the whole doorway.

Picture: Dino filling Mara's doorway, collar up, clipboard flat to his chest like a breastplate

On screen: Mara (squinting at the daylight), Dino (navy jacket, collar turned up, salt in his moustache, clipboard pressed to his chest)

He is a big man, and the wind has made him smaller. There is salt in his moustache, and his clipboard is pressed flat to his chest like armour.

DINO (formal) Mara Bašić.

He checks the clipboard to be sure.

DINO Granddaughter of Ante Bašić. Arrived Monday, the sixteen forty-five ferry.

MARA Morning, Dino.

DINO For the record, I am here in my official capacity.

MARA (dry) Do you want coffee in your official capacity?

He considers this as if it were a form with two boxes.

DINO After.

He slides a strip of paper from under the clip and holds it out by one corner, the way you hold evidence.

Close-up: a narrow strip of yellowed teleprinter paper, torn at both ends, three lines in uneven capitals

BREAD. LEMONS. ONE APOLOGY TO IVKA. The capitals are punched into the paper more than printed, a little uneven, like a careful man typing with cold hands.

MARA (frowning) Where's this from?

DINO The lighthouse. The machine in the service room.

He turns the clipboard round to show her a form with numbered boxes, each box filled in with a neat square hand.

DINO I went at seven this morning, to do the inventory for Friday. Item fourteen. Teleprinter, one.

DINO (pained) Condition: I wrote "dead". Then I saw the paper hanging out of it. Now I must cross it out.

DINO I hate crossing things out.

MARA So it's not dead.

DINO It is printing shopping.

He studies her face, a little too long.

DINO Twenty years, Mara, that machine says nothing. Not a word. Not a letter.

DINO Then you step off the ferry, and the same night it wants lemons.

MARA (flat) You think it's me.

DINO I think you are a radio engineer.

MARA I was asleep. In a coat.

She hands the strip back.

MARA (brisk) It's a prank. Kids. Somebody found an old transmitter in a garage and they're winding you up.

DINO (slowly) Kids.

He turns and looks up the lane. It is empty except for the same cat and the same plastic bag, which the wind is still walking like a dog.

DINO The youngest person on this island is my nephew Luka.

DINO He is forty-one. He mends nets.

DINO (grim) His idea of a prank is doing my crossword in pen.

MARA All right.

MARA Coffee.

Ante's kitchen, a quarter past eight on Tuesday

Ambience: wind pushing at shutters, an old fridge humming

The coffee pot hisses on the gas ring. Dino stands in the middle of the small kitchen with his cup, and does not take a chair.

He looks at the lists on the walls the way some people look at icons in a church: politely, without getting too close.

On screen: Mara (pouring), Dino (standing very straight, cup held level, clipboard under one arm)

MARA You can sit.

DINO Thank you. I am fine.

MARA (dry) A chair doesn't commit you to anything, Dino.

DINO That is what people think about chairs.

He drinks. It is too hot. He drinks it anyway, standing up, then sets out Friday as though reading it from a timetable.

DINO (formal) Friday. A Plovput crew on the Friday ferry. Two men.

DINO A solar beacon on a galvanised pole. LED. Twelve volts. Serviced once a year from Split.

DINO The old lantern is switched off. The old equipment goes down the causeway on a trolley and away.

MARA The receiver. The teleprinter. All of it.

DINO All of it.

He pauses.

DINO (very quietly) Unless the harbour master signs otherwise.

MARA (watching him) And will you?

DINO Why would I?

DINO The light is old. The machine is old.

DINO (aggrieved) And now the machine is ordering lemons.

MARA Twelve volts and a pole. He'd have hated that.

MARA He replaced that relay in ninety-seven. Photocell, relay, a twenty-four volt coil. He described it to me every summer. I could draw you the circuit.

MARA I could draw you the circuit on a napkin, Dino, with the contact ratings, and the...

DINO I believe you.

MARA (catching herself) Sorry. I do that.

DINO Do what?

MARA Explain. When I'm... when it's early.

DINO It is eight fifteen. It is not early.

MARA It's early for me.

He leafs through the clipboard to file the strip. Forms flip past: berthing, fuel, lost property, a notice about rats.

One form is soft at the corners from being carried. He turns past it fast.

MARA What's that one?

DINO A form. I have a form for everything.

MARA That one. Proposal for registration of a... what?

DINO (stiffly) Of a cultural good.

MARA Ever filled it in?

DINO Not yet.

MARA How long have you had it?

DINO Some time.

He finishes his coffee and rinses the cup at the sink. Then he sets it upside down on the draining board.

MARA (softer) He'd have liked that.

DINO Liked what?

MARA The cup. Upside down. He used to say it was a list with one item.

Dino does not know what to do with this, so he buttons his jacket up to the collar.

DINO Thank you for the coffee.

DINO (at the door, formal again) Please don't print anything tonight.

MARA I'm not printing anything.

DINO For the record.

MARA For the record, I'm not printing anything.

He nods once, as though the statement has been received and stamped, and goes out into the wind.

She watches him from the doorway. He stops halfway down the lane and looks back at the lighthouse for a moment. Then he goes on down to the riva, the clipboard against his chest.

Ante's kitchen, Tuesday morning

The door shuts. The fridge hums. The bin bags sit on the table where she left them, rolled and patient.

On screen: Mara (alone, phone in her hand)

Before he went, she photographed the strip. She looks at the photo now.

MARA For evidence.

MARA That's all. Evidence.

She zooms in on the capitals. Up close they are not even. The B leans. The second L is fainter than the first, as if the key stuck.

MARA Somebody typed this by hand. Slowly.

MARA (quiet) Two fingers.

She zooms in on the last line until the capitals go soft and grey.

MARA (reading) One apology to Ivka.

She looks across the harbour through the salted window, to where the one lit window was last night.

MARA Bread. Lemons.

She puts down the phone. She picks up the roll of bin bags and puts that down too.

MARA Sort, bag, label, sell.

MARA After.

She swaps the pyjamas for jeans and a jumper, then shrugs the raincoat back on, both arms this time, and rolls the cuffs back twice.

MARA (dry) Fine. Shopping.
