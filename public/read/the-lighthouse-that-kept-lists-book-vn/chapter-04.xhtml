Three Tape Measures

Ante's kitchen, Wednesday afternoon

Ambience: wind pushing at shutters, an old fridge humming

The bin bags are still on the kitchen table, but they have been pushed to one end. The rest of the table belongs to a laptop and a radio dongle the size of a cigarette lighter. A length of wire runs from it to the window, held there with masking tape.

On the laptop a waterfall display runs blue noise slowly down the screen. Nothing in it.

The borrowed logbook lies beside the laptop, open at the back cover.

On screen: Mara (raincoat off at last, grey jumper, sleeves pushed up, the frequency in ink on the back of her hand)

MARA Two metres. Set. Bandwidth, fine. Gain, fine.

She checks the number on her hand against the number in the logbook. Then she checks it again.

MARA (to the screen) Nothing. Good. Nothing until ten.

She has seven hours to wait and a house to empty, so she opens a drawer.

MARA Right. Sorting.

She lifts out screwdrivers, one at a time, and lays them in a row on the table. Flat, cross, flat, a tiny one for watches, a huge one for God knows what.

MARA Nine. Ten. Eleven.

MARA Eleven. You had eleven screwdrivers, Nono. You had two hands.

She does not put any of them in a bin bag.

Next drawer. A jam jar, labelled in his capitals.

MARA (reading) "Buttons. Other people's."

She shakes it. It is full of buttons: brass, horn, a blue one from a child's cardigan, a big black one from a coat.

MARA Whose? Whose buttons, Nono?

MARA Did you go round the island picking up buttons? Did people just give you their buttons?

She puts the jar back exactly where it was, label facing out. The bin bag stays rolled.

At the back of the drawer is a steel tape measure, five metres, its case dented, with something scratched into it.

Close-up: a battered steel tape measure with BASIC scratched into the case

BASIC, scratched into the steel with the point of a nail, the way the teleprinter would have written it. No hook on the S. The machine never could do Croatian letters.

MARA (quiet) Bašić. Without the hat.

She pulls the tape out a little and lets it snap back. Then again.

She turns the tape measure over in her hands, twice.

Then she looks at the wire on the window, and at the waterfall, and back at the tape measure.

MARA (slowly) Five metres of spring steel.

MARA A hundred and forty-four megahertz, give or take. A half-wave is about a metre.

MARA Three elements. Reflector, driven element, director. A bit of pipe for the boom.

MARA Hose clamps.

She looks out of the window, towards the shed at the bottom of the yard.

MARA (decided) Sorry, Nono.

She picks up the tape measure and goes out through the back door to the shed, where he kept the pipe.

Ante's kitchen, evening

The window is dark. The lamp is on. The stove, with the window open one crack, is finally drawing, and the kitchen is almost warm.

On the table, among the bin bags and the eleven screwdrivers, lies something new.

Two crossed lengths of white plastic pipe, and clamped across them three pieces of yellow tape measure, cut with Ante's tin snips.

It looks like a fishbone made by somebody who has only ever heard fish described.

On screen: Mara (tightening the last hose clamp with a screwdriver, tongue between her teeth)

MARA He'd approve in principle.

MARA (wincing) And object in practice. He loved that tape measure.

MARA "You can buy a tape measure, Mara. You cannot buy that tape measure."

She turns the screwdriver one more quarter turn, and the clamp bites.

MARA There. Done.

She holds it up by the boom and points it at the fridge. The fridge hums back at her.

Sound: three hard knocks on a wooden door, the same three as before

MARA It's open!

On screen: Mara (at the table, screwdriver in hand), Dino (in the doorway, dripping, clipboard sealed in a plastic bag)

DINO Half past nine. As agreed.

MARA We didn't agree.

DINO I agreed.

He comes in, wipes his boots twice on the mat, then once more, and looks at the thing on the table.

DINO (suspicious) What is that?

MARA A directional antenna. A Yagi. Three elements.

MARA It hears best where it's pointing. If the signal's close, we point it, we listen, and we walk to wherever it's loudest.

DINO It is a tape measure.

MARA It's three tape measures now.

DINO Ante's?

MARA (guilty) Ante's.

Dino leans over the table and looks at the scratched letters on one of the strips of yellow steel: B, A, S.

DINO He measured my office with this. In 2003. For a cabinet.

DINO The cabinet did not fit. He said the cabinet was wrong.

Dino considers the antenna a moment longer, and then he pulls out a chair and sits down.

Mara notices that he has sat down. She does not mention it.

MARA Coffee?

DINO Now, yes.

Ante's kitchen, ten o'clock

Ambience: wind pushing at shutters, the coffee pot ticking as it cools

Two cups. The laptop screen glows blue in the lamp light. Dino has turned his chair to face it, as if it were a television about to show the football.

On screen: Mara (leaning on the table, eyes on the screen), Dino (sitting very upright, cup on his knee)

DINO What am I looking at?

MARA The waterfall. Frequency across, time going down. Anything transmitting makes a line.

DINO There are no lines.

MARA There are no lines yet.

DINO Is it ten?

MARA Nine fifty-nine.

DINO My watch says ten.

MARA Your watch is wrong.

DINO (offended) My watch is set from the radio.

MARA Then the radio is...

The waterfall lights up.

Two hard bright lines appear side by side, a hundred and seventy hertz apart. The level meter jumps so far into the red that Mara lunges for the gain.

Close-up: a laptop waterfall display with two bright parallel lines cutting down through blue noise

Two lines of light fall side by side down the screen. Mark and space. Strong enough to drown everything else.

MARA (staring) That's not Split.

DINO How do you know?

MARA Split, through a wire on a window, would be a whisper. A smudge.

MARA This is shouting. This is close enough to hit with a stone.

DINO On Galebnjak.

MARA On Galebnjak.

The two lines dance and swap brightness: mark, space, mark, space. A box on the screen offers to decode them.

Mara's hand goes to the mouse. And stays there.

DINO Can't you read it? On the computer?

MARA I could. Two clicks.

She takes her hand away and puts it in her lap.

MARA (quiet) I want to read it on the paper. The way he meant it to be read.

Dino looks at her and does not ask anything else.

MARA (already pulling on the raincoat) Last night it ran about five minutes. Most of that's tuning at the start.

MARA We've got maybe four.

She grabs the handheld receiver and punches in the frequency from her hand. When she turns it up, two small stubborn tones chirp out of it.

MARA Bring the fish.

DINO (standing, gathering the antenna in both arms) It is not a fish.

MARA Bring it anyway.

The riva, Wednesday night

Ambience: a hard gusting wind along a harbour at night, sea smoke hissing over stone

They run down the steps to the riva. The bora comes along the harbour in hard flat blasts. Sea smoke blows over the bollards like steam off a pot.

Yellow lamps swing on their poles. Every half minute the lighthouse beam crosses the water and comes back.

Picture: Dino holding the fishbone antenna upright in both hands on the windy riva, solemn as a cross on a saint's day, Mara beside him with a handheld receiver

On screen: Mara (hood up, handheld receiver held out in front of her), Dino (holding the antenna upright in both hands, very solemn)

Dino holds the antenna upright before him in both hands, solemn as the processional cross on the saint's day.

In Mara's hand the receiver sings: two tones, alternating, like a very small bird being very insistent.

MARA (raising her voice over the wind) Turn. Slowly. Left.

Dino turns the antenna, with ceremony.

MARA Stop.

The tones swell. He turns a little further. They fade. He turns back.

MARA There. That way.

DINO That way is the whole harbour.

MARA Then we walk the whole harbour.

They go that way. Past the harbour office, dark. Past the café with its chairs stacked and chained to the tables.

DINO (to the chairs, passing) Good evening.

MARA Are you talking to the furniture?

DINO I have known that furniture forty years.

The tones grow stronger. Mara turns the volume down, and then down again.

MARA Point it a bit lower. It's not up the hill. It's down here. On the water side.

DINO Down here there is only the harbour and the shops.

MARA Then it's the harbour or the shops.

Sound: a loose shutter banging overhead like a gunshot

Dino flinches. He does not drop the antenna.

MARA (glancing at him) You didn't drop it.

DINO It is Ante's tape measure.

The tuning run ends in her hand. The tones turn quick and burbling: the data, the list itself, spooling out across the harbour and along the causeway to the lighthouse, with nobody there to read it.

MARA That's the message. Three minutes, maybe less.

DINO (grim) I know where this goes.

MARA Keep pointing.

DINO Mara. I know where this goes.

MARA Then keep pointing, and we'll both know.

The riva, the far end of the harbour

The last lamp on the riva. Beyond it, an alley runs up behind the only door on the harbour that ever has a light behind it.

Tonight the front of the shop is dark. The shutters are down. The bread crates are stacked inside the door.

On screen: Mara (receiver clutched to her chest), Dino (antenna lowered, looking up the alley)

MARA Up. Point it up the alley.

The fishbone swings round and holds steady.

Sound: a handheld receiver shrieking two loud tones

The receiver screams so loud she presses it against her raincoat to muffle it.

MARA (whispering) Okay. Okay. We're on top of it.

DINO (whispering) We are behind Ivka's.

MARA We're behind Ivka's.

DINO Ivka is eighty.

MARA Ivka is eighty and knows everything on this island.

DINO She does not know anything about radios. She calls the television "the box".

MARA Then somebody's using her roof.

They go up the alley, one careful step at a time, past the bins, past a stack of lemon crates gone grey with salt.

Picture: Mara and Dino in a dark alley looking up at a small lit window over the bins behind Ivka's shop

Round the back, at the small window over the bins, a light is on.

The glass is steamed from inside. Through it the same two tones squabble faintly.

A wire runs out of a hole in the window frame and up the wall to the roof.

MARA (barely breathing) There's your aerial.

DINO (very quietly) For the record...

MARA Dino.

DINO Yes.

MARA Not now.

He closes his mouth. Mara puts her hand on the back door, and it is not locked.
