The Debt Clock

Madame Jobin lent them snowshoes, which turned out to be a kind of tennis racket you strapped to your feet in order to fall over more slowly. Mère Clothilde lent Dec a pair of boots from the museum's lost property box, which had belonged, she said, to a German geologist in nineteen eighty-seven who had gone home in his socks for reasons nobody had ever discovered. They were a size too big. Dec stuffed the toes with newspaper and did not complain, because Inès was watching him to see if he would.

They left the village at one o'clock on Friday afternoon, when Monsieur Ruel of the forester's house had been seen by Madame Jobin's nephew getting on the little red train down to Delémont with a briefcase.

The track to the priory went up the valley between the wooded ridges, a white groove in the snow with the black spruce close on either side. It was two kilometres, Clothilde had said. It felt like twenty. There was no sound but their breathing and the creak of the snowshoes and, every so often, a soft heavy thump somewhere in the forest as a branch let go of its load. The cold was a dry, high cold that got into Dec's nose and made it ache. Inès went ahead of him in her navy coat and her mustard scarf, a small, upright, determined shape against the white, and she did not fall over once, and he fell over four times, and she waited each time without turning round, and he was grateful.

The priory of Combe-Noire was not much of a ruin, as ruins went. When they came up out of the last of the trees onto a shelf of open hillside, there was only a long broken wall of grey stone a little higher than his head, with snow along the top of it like icing, and some humps under the snow that might once have been buildings, and in the corner of the wall, the tower.

The tower was square and tall and black, built of rough dark stone that the snow would not stick to, and it had no windows at all except, very high up, a row of narrow slits like the gills of a fish. Near the top, on the side facing the valley, there was a great round shape set into the stone. A dial. It must have been three metres across. The snow had caught along its lower rim. The dial was very old iron, almost black, with a ring of figures round it that Dec could not make out from below, and a single great iron hand.

"Nine," said Inès quietly beside him. "Count them. There are only nine."

He counted. She was right. Nine marks round the great dial, where twelve should have been. And the single hand pointed straight up.

At the foot of the tower, set into the wall, was a door. Not a new door. Old oak, black and swollen, studded with iron nails, and a lock plate on it the size of a dinner plate with a keyhole you could have posted a letter through.

This was the door, Dec thought: the old one in the listed wall, the one lock the foundation's man from Basel had not been allowed to touch. He took the abbess's key out of his pocket. The iron was so cold it burned his fingers through his glove.

"Right," he said. "Let's see if the commune of Les Bréviaires knows how to protect its heritage."

The key went in. It did not turn. He worked it, gently, rocking it, feeling for the levers through the iron the way you feel for a tooth with your tongue, and something deep inside the door gave a long reluctant groan like an old man getting out of a chair, and turned, and the door opened inwards onto the dark.

Inside, the tower was hollow all the way up.

It was like standing at the bottom of a chimney. The walls went up and up, lit grey by the slits near the top, and the stone stair that had once climbed round the inside of them had been broken off: you could see the stubs of the steps sticking out of the wall, a spiral of broken teeth. But somebody had put in a new way up. A steel ladder, bolted to the wall in sections with platforms between, went up the inside of the tower in a zigzag, brand new, the bolts still bright, the kind of thing you see on the side of a ship.

"The foundation," said Inès. "Cultural heritage."

"They've spent a lot of money," said Dec, "on a heritage nobody's allowed to visit."

He went first. It was a long way up. He kept his eyes on the wall in front of his nose, because he had seen what looking down did to Inès on a roof in Paris, and he had no wish to find out what it did to him. Behind him her boots rang on the rungs, steady and slow and perfectly even, and once, near the top, he heard her say something very quietly in Hungarian that was either a prayer or the opposite.

At the top the ladder came up through a trap in a wooden floor, and they climbed through into the chamber of the clock.

It filled the room.

The clock was a cage of black iron the size of a garden shed, built of bars as thick as his wrist, joined with wedges and rivets, and inside the cage were wheels: huge wheels, iron wheels with hand-cut teeth, one inside another inside another, going up into the gloom. A great stone weight on a rusted chain hung down through a hole in the floor into the dark of the tower below. And at the front of the cage, between the clock and the inside of the great dial, where an iron rod went out through the wall to turn the single hand, there was a box.

Iron, riveted into the frame, the size of a small trunk, with a door in its front. And set into the door, black with age, its brass gone almost brown, was a cylinder with nine thin wheels on it.

Dec stood in front of it and forgot to breathe.

"It's the big one," he said. "It's the one in the drawings. It's the actual one. Roux made a copy of this. This is the..." He took off his glove and put his bare hand on the cold brass, very gently, as you would put your hand on a horse. "Inès. Six hundred and seventy-five years."

"Can you open it?"

"Not with the word. We haven't the word."

"Can you open it without the word?"

He looked at the lock. He breathed on his fingers.

The thing about a letter lock, his father had told him once, sitting on a bucket by a door in Salthill, is that it's only as good as the man who cut the gates. Every wheel has a little gap cut in its inside rim. When all nine gaps line up, the bar drops through. A modern one, made by a machine, the gaps are cut so tight there's no feeling them. You need the word or a drill. But an old one, cut by hand with a file, by candlelight, by a man in a hurry because the world was ending: those gaps are never quite tight. There's play in them. A hair's width. And if you pull on the shackle, just a little, and turn a wheel very slowly, you can feel, in your fingertips, the moment the gate goes past the bar.

"Maybe," said Dec. "Give me a minute. Don't talk to me."

"I wasn't going to."

"I know. I'm talking to me."

He took hold of the iron handle of the box door and pulled on it, very gently, putting the smallest possible pressure on the bar inside, the way you'd put pressure on a tension wrench. Then he took the first wheel between his finger and thumb and began to turn it, one letter at a time, slowly, slowly, with his eyes shut.

Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

There. A tiny give, less than a hair. The wheel slipped forward a fraction and caught.

"One," he said.

The wind came up outside while he worked, and snow began to blow in through the slits and settle on the iron cage of the clock. His fingers went numb and he warmed them in his armpits and started again. Inès stood behind him and did not say a word, and he could feel her not saying it.

Four. Five. Six.

The seventh wheel was stuck with six centuries of grime and took him a long time, and the ninth took him longer, because by then his hands were shaking, and he had to stop and breathe and start again. And then, with a sound so small he felt it more than heard it, a sound like a key turning in a very small door a very long way away, the bar dropped.

The door of the box swung open an inch on its own weight.

"Dec," said Inès, very quietly, and it was the first thing she had said in forty minutes. "What does it say? The wheels. What word did you open it with?"

He opened his eyes and looked down at the nine wheels. The letters were tiny and worn and in an alphabet a little different from his own, and in the bad light he had to bend so close his nose almost touched the brass.

They said nothing at all. A nonsense row: Q, F, Z, A, and so on. He had opened it by the gates, not the letters, and the gates and the letters did not line up any more.

"Somebody's reset it," he said slowly. "Had the wheels off and put them back in a different order, so the word doesn't spell. Recently." He pulled the box door the rest of the way open. "Let's see what they were so keen on."

There were three things in the box.

The first was a book, huge and thick and very old, bound in leather that had once been red, and half of it was gone. Not torn: burnt. The back half of the book was a black crust of charred pages that crumbled at the edges when Inès lifted the cover with the end of her pencil. The front half was brittle and brown and covered, page after page, in columns of tiny Latin script and numbers.

"His book," said Inès. She kept her fingers off the pages and turned them with the pencil, one by one, her face so close her glasses misted. "Vey's ledger. Horae solutae. The hours paid. These are the people of the valley. Names. Hours." She turned to the last page before the burn began. The writing changed there, bigger, shakier, the letters sprawling across the ruled columns as if the hand had stopped caring about columns. She read it under her breath, and stopped, and read it again. "Dec. Look."

He looked. Latin. It meant nothing to him. A few lines, and then the black edge of the burn cutting across the page, and on the very edge of the burn, half eaten by it, one word that went off the page into the char.

REMIT...

And then nothing. Black.

"Remit," said Dec. "That's English."

"It's Latin first. Remittere. To send back. To let go. To forgive a debt." Inès's voice had gone very flat and careful. "It could be a lot of things. Remitto, I forgive. Remittitur, it is forgiven. It's cut off." She took out her phone, pulled off her glove with her teeth, and began to photograph the page. "It's the start of a word. It fits. Nine letters. R at the start."

"Fits what?"

"The gap. In the painting." She kept photographing. "Look at the other things. Quickly. I don't like this place."

The other two things were not old.

One was a notebook, a fat black hardback with an elastic band round it, the kind sold in every stationer's in Europe. Dec opened it. It was full of a small, neat, slanted handwriting in French, in blue biro, and diagrams, and columns of figures, and graphs drawn with a ruler. He could not read most of it. He could read the heading on the first page because it was in capitals and underlined twice.

ALS162. Essais de substitution locale.

"Inès," he said. "What's substitution?"

"Replacement." She had stopped photographing the ledger and was looking at the notebook over his shoulder, and he felt her go still. "Local replacement. Of ALS162. That's..."

"Allouis. Roux said. It's the voice. The time signal. All the clocks listen to it."

The third thing was in a plastic anti-static bag, of the silver kind that computer parts come in. Dec slid it out. It was a circuit board about the size of a paperback, green, crowded with little black chips and coils and a row of switches, and a coaxial socket at one end for an aerial. Someone had written on it in white paint pen, in the same neat slanted hand as the notebook: ALS162 / PROTO 3 / 162 kHz / 9 min.

Inès took the notebook out of his hands and turned the pages, fast, the way she never turned the pages of anything. And then a page of tests, dated, each one with a time and a place and a result.

12/02. Bois de Vincennes. 400 m. 9 min. OK.

05/03. Quai de la Rapée. 1,2 km. 9 min. OK.

26/03. Gare d'Austerlitz + bd de l'Hôpital. 2,5 km. 07h51. 9 min. OK. Aucune réaction.

No reaction.

"That was yesterday," said Dec. His mouth had gone very dry. "That's Pichon's text. That's yesterday morning, in the station. That was them. That was a test."

Inès turned the page.

There was only one line on it, in the middle of the page, written more carefully than the rest, as a man writes the thing he has been working towards all along.

Panthéon, galerie du dôme. 1er avril. 14h51 à 15h00. Neuf minutes. Rayon prévu: 5 km.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. The wind whined in the slits. A flake of snow landed on the page and sat there without melting.

"Five kilometres," Inès said at last. "From the Panthéon. That's the whole centre of Paris. Every radio clock. The stations. Gare de Lyon, Austerlitz, Montparnasse, the Gare du Nord, maybe. Every traffic light that takes the signal. Every hospital clock." Her voice was very quiet and very even, and he had learned by now that the evener it got the more frightened she was. "At three o'clock. In an eclipse. In the dark. With the whole city out in the street looking up."

"And on the telly," said Dec, "a man in a nice suit at the Panthéon, saying look, the clocks have stopped, God's calling in the debt." He felt sick. "It's not a miracle. It's a radio."

"It's a radio," Inès agreed, "and nine minutes of every clock in Paris lying."

Then she held out her phone to him. "Photograph it. All of it. Every page. Mine's on four per cent."

He took her phone, and his own, and photographed the notebook as fast as he had ever done anything, page after page, the graphs and the figures and the list of tests and the page with the Panthéon on it, while she held the pages flat with her pencil and her bare cold fingertips, and he had got to the last page and was turning back to check he had them all when, below them in the hollow of the tower, the oak door opened.

They heard it groan. They heard the wind come in with it, a long cold breath up the inside of the tower, and the door bang back against the wall. Then they heard feet on the steel ladder. Two people. One light, quick and even. One very slow, and very heavy, so that the whole ladder hummed.

There was nowhere to go. There was the cage of the clock and the open box and the trap in the floor, and the trap was the only way out, and the feet were coming up through it.

"Put the phones away," said Inès, very fast and very low. "Inside your clothes. Not your pockets."

He did. Both of them, down the front of his jumper, against his skin, so cold they made him gasp.

Fabien came up through the trap first. He was not out of breath. He stood up in the chamber of the clock and brushed the snow off the shoulders of his grey coat and looked at them, and at the open box, and at the notebook in Inès's hand, and his narrow choirboy face stayed as smooth as a plate.

"Every hour is lent," he said, in French, quite pleasantly, as if he were reading out a platform announcement. "Every hour lent is owed. You're in a private building, Madame Varga. Monsieur Hartley. You don't have permission to be here."

"We had a key," said Dec.

"Of course you did."

Behind him, up through the trap, came the bald head, and the shoulders, and the rest of Brother Kessler, slowly, like something surfacing from deep water. He stood up. He had to stoop under the beams. The little blue cuckoo clock on his neck moved when he swallowed.

He held out his hand to Inès. Palm up. Not threatening. Simply waiting, the way you hold your hand out to a child for the thing it should not have picked up.

Inès looked at the hand for a long moment. Then she put the notebook in it.

"And the board, please," said Brother Fabien. "And the book."

Dec gave him the board. He had no choice. Kessler took the ledger himself, very carefully, holding it flat on both enormous palms as if it were a sleeping animal, and turned and went down through the trap, and the ladder hummed under him all the way to the bottom.

The grey coat closed the door of the iron box. He spun the nine wheels with one quick sweep of his gloved hand, zzzip, like a man shuffling cards. Then he went to the trap, and stepped down onto the ladder, and looked back up at them from the hole in the floor with only his head and shoulders showing.

"It will be cold tonight," he said. "Minus twenty, they say, on the plateau. The Clockless One lends us every hour. Tonight, perhaps, you'll learn the price of one."

He went down. The trap came down after him with a bang that shook the floor. And then, from underneath it, there came a new sound: a scraping, and a clank, and the bright, final, modern snap of a steel padlock closing on a hasp.

Then, far below, the long groan of the oak door. And then the key, Clothilde's abbess's key, which Dec had left in it, turning in the lock from the outside.

Dec stood in the chamber of the Debt Clock and looked at the trap in the floor, and then at the slits in the walls, where the snow was coming in sideways now, and the light was going, and the wind was rising to a howl.

"Well," he said, "that's my key gone."
