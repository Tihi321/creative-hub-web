What the Varnish Kept

At eleven o'clock on Monday night the atelier was dark except for the lamp on Inès's bench, and the skylight overhead was a square of orange Paris cloud, and Dec Hartley was asleep on the old green sofa by the door with his boots on and his mouth open, talking.

He talked in his sleep the way he talked awake: fast, with his hands. Mostly it was in English. Once it was something about a van. Once, quite clearly, he said "Ah, Mrs Kilbride, sure it's no trouble," and smiled, and turned over, and the sofa groaned. Inès had put a blanket over him at ten. He had kicked it off at a quarter past. She had not put it back, because she was working, and because she did not want to be the kind of person who got up twice to put a blanket back on an Irishman.

On the bench in front of her, under the lamp, she had everything she had.

Her laptop, with photograph twenty-three on it, the raking light across the gap in the Latin line, the nine ghost ticks in the ground. Her notebook, open at R/P/B _ E/L. Her phone, with the burnt last page of Vey's ledger, and the four lines in his late, sprawling hand that ran into REMIT and the black. And spread out across the rest of the bench, held flat at the corners with a jar of pigment, a palette knife, a coffee cup and her own left elbow, Anselme Roux's five sheets of drawings for the nine-wheel lock.

She had looked at the drawings in the shop, on Tuesday, over Dec's shoulder. She had not looked at them.

Ne csak nézd, her father said, somewhere at the back of her head, in his fine baritone, at a red light on the boulevard des Belges. Lásd.

She looked.

The fourth sheet was the section of the wheels. Nine rings, drawn side by side, each one cut through to show the inside rim. On each inside rim, a small notch: the gate. And beside each gate, in faded brown, a number. XVII. IIII. XII. VIII. Roman numerals, in Vey's hand, written the old way with four strokes for four. Roux had said it, grumbling, in his shop. The gates I cut where the drawings told me, and the drawings had no letters on the gates, only numbers.

And the third sheet was the outside of the wheels. Each with its ring of tiny letters round the rim. Vey's alphabet: twenty-three letters, the old Latin alphabet with no J and no W, and the U and the V the same letter, and at the end two marks she had taken in the shop for decoration and now saw were the old abbreviations for et and con. Twenty-three letters on every wheel. Each wheel drawn with its first letter at the top, at the twelve o'clock mark, and the rest running round.

Not decoration. A table. A cipher wheel, and the key next to it.

She took a pencil.

Wheel one: the gate at seventeen. She counted round the letters of the first wheel with the point of the pencil, slowly, the way you count stitches. A, B, C, D... Seventeen.

R.

She sat back. Her heart had started to go, hard and slow, in her throat.

Wheel two: the gate at four. A, B, C, D... no. She had miscounted. The numbers ran from the top, and the top was one. Four. D. She frowned. That was wrong. She looked again, closer, with her loupe, and saw that the second wheel's ring did not start at A. Vey had drawn each wheel's alphabet beginning at a different letter, offset, the way a careful man would, so that anyone who saw only one of the two sheets would have nothing. The second wheel began at B. One, B. Two, C. Three, D. Four...

E.

Wheel three. Wheel four. Wheel five. She did not let herself think. She counted, and wrote, and counted again, and checked every letter twice against the offset of its wheel, the pencil going round the little rings under the lamp while Dec talked to Mrs Kilbride on the sofa and the skylight hummed with traffic. M. I. T. T. E. R.

Eight wheels. Eight letters.

R E M I T T E R.

The ninth wheel's gate had no number. There was a little blank square drawn where the number should be, and in it, in Roux's own modern biro, in a furious little hand: enveloppe scellée!!

The sealed envelope. The young man in grey standing over the old man while he cut it.

Inès turned from the drawings to the laptop. Photograph twenty-three. The nine ghost ticks in the ground of the painting, and at the end of the gap, at the foot of the ninth letter, the faint short horizontal she had seen in Les Bréviaires at six in the morning, under a quilt, before any of this. E, or L.

Not L. There was no word that ended remitterl.

E.

She wrote it out at the bottom of the page in capitals, in pencil, and looked at it.

REMITTERE.

To send back. To let go. To forgive a debt.

She sat for a long time without moving.

It was not, she found, the word that held her. She had half known the word since the tower. It was what it meant, now that she had it whole, in the place it belonged: under the clock with nine at the top, at the end of a line that began mutuo datum. She said it aloud, very quietly, the whole line, as the painter must have lettered it, before somebody took a knife to it.

"Mutuo datum remittere hora nona."

What was given as a loan, to forgive at the ninth hour.

Not repaid. Not collected. Forgiven. The opposite of everything the Brotherhood had chanted in the dark under Paris. Reddetur, it shall be repaid, eighteen voices had said. And their founder, at the end, old and frightened and watching what he had made, had written the other word. Had put it in his book. Had painted it, or had it painted, under the clock on a sundial with no hours. Had made it the word that opened his lock, the one thing in the whole priory that was meant to stay shut, and then, Clothilde's sisters said, he had opened it himself before he died, and left it open. A lock that had given up.

And his brothers had scraped the word off the painting, and burnt the page in the ledger, and kept the combination of his lock as nine sacred numbers, for six hundred years, without ever once, she would have bet her father's watch on it, counting round the letters.

Their own lock said forgive. And they had never read it.

She did not cry. She wanted that clearly understood, afterwards, by the only other person in the room, who was asleep and would not have known. She took her glasses off, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a while, and put her glasses back on.

Then she went over and kicked the sofa.

"Wha'," said Dec. "Mrs Kilbride. No. What?"

"Get up," said Inès. "I've got the word."

He took it, she thought afterwards, very well for a man who had been asleep on a sofa ten seconds before. He sat up with his hair in four directions and listened to all of it with his elbows on his knees, and when she showed him the eight letters on the drawings and the ninth in the photograph, he looked at the page for a long time without saying anything. Then he looked up at her, and his face was a thing she would remember.

"That's mad," he said softly. "That's absolutely mad. You did that with a pencil."

"Don't make a thing of it."

"I'll make a thing of it if I want. You did that with a pencil." He looked down at the drawings again, at Roux's angry biro in the blank square. "So Roux's lock. The copy. The brick of brass he built for them. If they had him cut the gates to these numbers, and the last one from the envelope..."

"...then it opens with the same word. Yes."

"And they'll have it in the dome. On the cabinet. On the radio." He was quiet. "And I was going to pick it."

"You can't?"

"I could pick Vey's. In the tower. Because Vey cut his gates with a file, by candlelight, in a hurry, and there's play in them, and you can feel it." He shook his head slowly. "Roux didn't cut his by candlelight. Roux had a lathe and six weeks and a grudge. You saw his shop, Inès. That man's never made a sloppy thing in his life. He's eighty-one and he's furious and he's the best I've ever seen." A short, astonished laugh. "He'll have cut those gates so tight you couldn't get a thought between them. I'd be there till Easter." He looked up. "I couldn't have opened it. I'd have got up that stair on Wednesday with the cutlery and found out I couldn't. In front of Vaulmont."

"Now you won't have to."

"Now I won't have to," Dec agreed. He looked at the word in pencil. "Remittere. Forgive."

"Forgive," said Inès.

La Taupe met them at nine on Tuesday morning in a café on the rue Mouffetard, in the same woollen hat and a bright yellow raincoat and a mood of high professional excitement, and spread a map across the table between the croissants that was not a map Inès had seen in any shop. It was hand-drawn, on several sheets of tracing paper taped together, in four colours of ink, and it showed the streets of the fifth arrondissement in faint grey and underneath them, in black and red and blue, something like the roots of a tree.

"The Panthéon," said La Taupe, tapping a large square in grey, "is built on top of the quarries. Everybody knows that. In seventeen fifty-something the architect, Soufflot, finds out the hill is full of holes, nearly has a heart attack, has to fill half of it with masonry before he can put his church on top. But not all of it." Her finger moved across the red lines. "There's a gallery here, under the rue Clotilde, which is an old inspection gallery from when they checked the foundations. It runs right up under the east end of the crypt. And at the end of it there's a shaft." She grinned. "With a grille."

"Of course there's a grille," said Dec.

"It's lovely. Seventeen nineties, maybe. Nobody's opened it in two hundred years. I've only ever seen it from the bottom with a torch. It comes up," she checked her drawing, "into a little room off the crypt. A store, maybe. Or a chapel that isn't one any more."

"The north-east chapel," said Dec slowly.

They both looked at him.

He took out his phone and showed them the email from Mademoiselle Ferrand. The room will be installed in the north-east chapel of the crypt of the Panthéon.

"My room," he said. "My escape room. They're putting it in a chapel at the east end of the crypt. With a grille in the floor nobody's opened since the Revolution." He looked at La Taupe's map for a long moment. "If it's the same room."

"Only one way to find out," said La Taupe, and folded the map, and finished Inès's croissant without asking.

It was a shorter walk than the one under the Barrière d'Enfer, and drier, and much worse.

It was worse because of the weight. Inès had not expected that. In the chapel tunnels the dark had been big and old and indifferent. Here, every step, she knew what was above her. The rue Clotilde. The Lycée Henri-IV. And then, as La Taupe led them along a narrow inspection gallery with walls of fitted grey blocks and a ceiling so low that even Inès had to stoop, the Panthéon itself: the whole immense stone weight of it, the columns and the dome and the crypt full of the great dead of France, pressing down on the rock over their heads.

"Is it safe?" she asked, and hated the sound of her voice.

"Safe?" said La Taupe, pleased. "It's been standing on Soufflot's masonry for two hundred and fifty years. It's the safest place in Paris. It's not going anywhere." She paused. "Probably."

At the end of the gallery the walls opened into a small square chamber, and in the middle of its ceiling was a shaft, round and lined with brick, going straight up into blackness. Iron rungs were set into the brick, rusty and very old. La Taupe went up them like a squirrel and Inès went up after her, because there was no other way, with her eyes on the rungs in front of her face and the old cold tightness starting in her chest, the one from the roof on the Île Saint-Louis, and she did not look down, and Dec, below her, did not tell her not to.

Four metres up, the shaft ended in a grille.

It was the same kind as the one in the chapel under the Barrière d'Enfer: wrought iron, thick bars, red with rust, set into a stone frame. Above it Inès could see, through the bars, darkness, and then not darkness: a faint grey light, the light of a room with a high small window. And something else. The underside of something. Planks. New planks, pale and clean, with the stamp of a timber merchant on them in blue ink, laid across the top of the grille like the floor of a stage.

Dec came up beside her on the rungs, squeezing, until his shoulder was against hers and his face was a hand's width from the bars. He looked up at the pale new planks for a long time. Then he started to laugh, very quietly, with his forehead against the cold iron.

"That's mine," he said. "That's my floor. That's the floor of the cell. I specified that timber. That's my order, from the yard in Montreuil; I'd know that stamp anywhere." He looked down at Inès, his face lit grey from above, and he was grinning like a boy. "They've built my room right on top of their own back door. And they don't know it's there."

"Can you open the grille?"

He looked at its lock: a big square box lock, eighteenth century, rusted almost solid. He looked at it the way, she imagined, a man looks at an old friend in a crowd.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I'll open it tomorrow. From the top. When I'm inside. If I open it now somebody might notice." He reached up through the bars and touched the underside of his own planks, very lightly, with the tips of his fingers, as if he were checking they were real. "It's a way out. That's all. Nobody's going to need it. But I like to know there's a way out."

They made the plan that afternoon at the atelier, with the map and the drawings and the permit and the pass spread across Inès's bench under the north light.

It was not a good plan. They both knew it. Dec would go in at seven on Wednesday morning with his pass and his crates, as the contractor for the clockmaker's cell, accès toutes zones, and finish installing his room in the north-east chapel, and open the grille under its floor from above, and then, when the guests came, watch the catering. Inès would go in as what she was: the restorer of the Allegory of the Sundial, which Vaulmont was going to show his guests at the vigil, and which no restorer in her right mind would let out of her sight with a crowd and a buffet and a television crew around it. She had written to Mademoiselle Ferrand at the Fondation that morning to say so, in her most professional French, with three references to the conservation code of ethics and one to the insurers. Mademoiselle Ferrand had written back in twelve minutes. But of course. Monsieur de Vaulmont would be delighted.

"He wants you there too," said Dec.

"Yes."

"Where he can see you."

"Yes."

"And then what? We're both inside, we've a word for a lock in the roof and a back door in the cellar, and a hundred guests and a minister and a radio and a fella the size of a wardrobe on the stairs. And then what?"

"And then we stop it," said Inès.

"How?"

She looked at the drawings. At the word, in pencil, at the bottom of her page. REMITTERE.

"I don't know yet," she said honestly. "I'll know when I see it. You'll know when you see it. That's how we do it. You look at the door. I look at the painting. We see what's wrong."

Dec looked at her across the bench for a long moment, under the north light.

"That's a terrible plan," he said.

"Yes."

"It's the worst plan I've ever heard."

"Yes."

"Grand," said Dec. "I'm in."

Through the skylight, high over the Marais, a single grey cloud was moving very slowly towards the south, towards the Latin Quarter and the dome, and the sun, the real one, the one that would go out tomorrow at three o'clock, came through behind it for a moment and laid a square of cold clean light across the bench, across the drawings and the word, like a hand.
