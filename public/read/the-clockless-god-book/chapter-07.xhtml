Night Train to the Snow

The train to Basel left the Gare de Lyon at seven minutes past eight on Thursday morning, the twenty-sixth of March, and by twenty past Dec had told Inès about his father.

He had not meant to. He had meant to say something light about the coffee in the restaurant car, which was the colour of a puddle and cost six euros, and instead, somewhere between the suburbs and the first green fields, it had come out. That his father had been a locksmith in Galway for forty years, with a shop on Dominick Street and a van with Hartley & Son painted on the side in red letters before there was a son to speak of. That the old man used to take him on call-outs on Saturdays when he was eight, nine, ten, and sit him on an upturned bucket by whatever door it was and talk him through it.

"He used to say every lock's a story," Dec said, "about what somebody's afraid of. You look at a lock and you know the person. A fella with three deadbolts on a flat with nothing in it, he's not afraid of burglars, he's afraid of somebody in particular. An old lady with a chain on the door and the key left in the back of it, she's afraid of being found too late. You open the lock, you're reading the story." He turned his paper cup round on the little table. "He died in twenty-one. I tried to keep the shop going. Turns out I'm a very good locksmith and a very bad businessman. I'd do a job for an old dear and she'd give me tea and tell me about her grandson and I'd charge her for the part and forget the hours. Closed it the year after. Sold the van. Somebody's painted it white now and uses it for plumbing." He shrugged. "So. Escape rooms. Same thing, really. You build a story about being afraid, and people pay to get out of it."

Across the table, Inès said nothing at all. She had said almost nothing since the train left. She had her coat folded on the seat beside her and her scarf still on and a small notebook open on the table in front of her, in which she had written nothing either, and she was looking at him over the top of her glasses with an expression he could not read.

"Sorry," he said. "I talk. When I'm, you know. It's a thing."

"I know," said Inès. "Go on."

So, to his own surprise, he went on.

At the change in Basel he noticed the man in the grey coat.

He would like to have said afterwards that he had noticed him in Paris. He had not. In Paris the station had been full of people in coats, and he had been busy carrying Inès's bag, which she had not asked him to carry and he had picked up anyway, and which weighed about as much as a small car and turned out, when she opened it on the train, to be half full of books.

But in Basel they had twelve minutes to cross from the French side of the station to the Swiss side, and Dec was standing on the platform under the big departures board, eating a pretzel the size of his head, when he saw, across the platform, a thin young man in a long grey coat get off the same train three carriages down, look up at the same board, and look away from it quickly, at nothing, the way people look at nothing when they are trying very hard not to look at something.

He was pale. Twenty-eight, maybe, with fair hair cut very short and a narrow, earnest, unlined face, like a choirboy who had grown up without anyone telling him. Across his grey waistcoat, under the open coat, was a silver chain.

Dec stopped eating the pretzel.

"Inès," he said, without moving his lips. "Don't look round."

She looked round. Of course she did. She looked straight across the platform at the young man in grey, who was now examining a vending machine as if it held the secret of life, and then she looked back at Dec.

"Him?"

"The chain. On his waistcoat. I'll bet you the rest of this pretzel there's a watch on the end of it with no hands."

"I don't want the rest of your pretzel."

"It's a figure of speech."

"It's a bad one." She picked up her bag of books before he could. "Platform four. Delémont. Walk normally."

"I always walk normally."

"You don't," said Inès. "You walk like a stepladder somebody's trying to carry."

On the Delémont train the young man in grey sat at the far end of their carriage, facing them, behind a Swiss newspaper.

Dec decided to watch him discreetly.

He had seen people do this in films. You held a newspaper up and peered over it. He did not have a newspaper, so he borrowed Inès's notebook, which was the wrong shape, and held it open in front of his face and peered over it, and after a minute Inès reached across the table without looking up from her book and turned it the right way up.

"He's reading the same page," Dec reported. "He's been reading the same page since Basel. Nobody reads the share prices of Swiss pharmaceutical companies for half an hour."

"Swiss people do."

"Not with that face. That's not a share-prices face. That's a face of a man trying to remember what you look like." He lowered the notebook an inch. "He's looking at you."

"Then stop looking at him."

"I'm not looking at him, I'm watching him discreetly."

"Dec," said Inès, very quietly, turning a page, "you are the least discreet thing in Switzerland. You are less discreet than the mountains."

He put the notebook down.

Outside the window the country had changed while he wasn't looking. Flat green fields had given way to wooded slopes, and the slopes to narrow valleys with fast brown rivers in them, and there was snow now, first in the shadows under the trees and then everywhere, thick on the roofs of the farmhouses and the stacked logs outside them, white on the black spruce. The light had gone flat and silvery. Every so often the train went through a village where every second house seemed to have a sign on it with a watch drawn on it, and men in white coats could be seen through lit workshop windows bent over benches.

"Morel was glad I was going," Inès said suddenly.

He looked at her. She had shut her book.

"I told him yesterday afternoon I needed a week. Family. He didn't ask what. He didn't ask about the painting. He just said of course, of course, voilà, take as long as you need. And he looked..." She frowned out of the window. "He looked relieved. And frightened. As if I'd told him something he'd been waiting to hear, and he didn't like it."

"Our restorer is close to the word," Dec said slowly. "That's what the warm-oil fella said. Down in the chapel."

"Yes."

"I've been thinking about that all night." He had. He had lain awake in his room above the closed bakery in the eleventh with his hair still wet from the shower, listening to the pipes knock, thinking about it. "I thought he meant you. That they knew you'd found the clock under the varnish. And they did, sure, somebody did, because they sent you the painting in the first place. But our. Our restorer. Like you're theirs."

"I'm not theirs."

"I know you're not."

"I'm nobody's," said Inès, and looked out at the snow, and her jaw went tight in a way that made him pick up his paper cup and study the bottom of it for a while.

Her phone buzzed on the table between them. She turned it over, read the screen, and went still, and then turned it round so that he could read it too.

It was a text message, from a Paris number, in French. Dec read it slowly, moving his lips.

Mme Varga. This morning at 07:51 every clock in the Gare d'Austerlitz and the traffic lights on the boulevard de l'Hôpital showed nine o'clock for nine minutes. Then they went back. The SNCF calls it a technical fault. I call it nothing at all yet, because I have no evidence and a very good lunch booked. Please tell me you are in Lyon with your mother. A. Pichon.

"He gave you his number?"

"I took it off his card. On the desk. While he was eating." She pushed her glasses up. "He didn't notice."

"He noticed," said Dec. "He's a commissaire. He let you."

Inès read the message twice, her thumbnail tapping the edge of the screen. Then she typed, with one finger, very precisely: I am with my mother. And sent it, and put the phone face down on the table, and looked out at the snow.

"Your mother's in Lyon," Dec said.

"Yes."

"We're in Switzerland."

"He doesn't need to know that," said Inès. "Yet."

This morning, Dec thought. At seven fifty-one he had been on his way to the Gare de Lyon with Inès's bag of books, half a mile across the river from Austerlitz, and he had not looked up at a clock once. Nine minutes of nine o'clock, in a railway station at rush hour. It sounded like nothing. It sounded like a man on the radio apologising for a delay. He thought about the clocks on Roux's walls, all silent, all pointing the same way, and a station full of people looking up at the boards, and something cold went down his back that had nothing to do with the snow outside.

At the far end of the carriage the choirboy turned a page of his newspaper. It was, Dec noticed, the same page.

At Delémont they changed again, onto a little red narrow-gauge train of two carriages that climbed into the hills in a series of switchbacks, so slowly that a man on a bicycle could have kept up with it, if he had been very fit and slightly mad. The grey coat got on the second carriage. Dec saw him through the connecting door, sitting very straight with his hands on his knees, not reading anything now, looking out of the window.

At the top of the climb, the woods opened and the world went white.

It was a high, wide plateau, rolling and bare, with dark clumps of spruce scattered across it like ink blots, and snow over everything as far as he could see, under a sky the colour of an old spoon. Fences ran across it in long grey stitches. Here and there a farmhouse sat low in the snow with its huge roof coming almost to the ground, and smoke going straight up from it, and nobody anywhere.

"Franches-Montagnes," said Inès, beside him at the window. "The Free Mountains."

"Free of what?"

"Taxes, originally. In the fourteenth century. The bishop gave the land free to anyone who'd come and clear it." She pushed her glasses up. "It's in the guidebook. I read the guidebook."

"Course you did."

Les Bréviaires was the next stop but one: a halt with a single platform and a wooden shelter and a sign, and beyond it a village: a dozen big farmhouses, a church with an onion-shaped spire, a long low building with a row of tall windows that had been a watch factory once and now had a banner on it advertising a cheese festival in August, and a scattering of smaller houses, each with a workshop on the ground floor and a light on in it. Behind the village the valley narrowed between two dark wooded ridges and climbed, and at the far end of it, a mile or two off, high on a shoulder of the hill under the trees, there was a ruin. A square tower, black against the snow, and the broken line of a wall.

Dec stepped off the train into snow that came over his trainers and up to the middle of his shins and filled his socks with cold so complete it felt like a noise.

"Ah, God," he said.

"I told you to bring boots."

"I don't own boots. I'm from Galway. We don't have weather. We have rain."

Behind them the second carriage door opened and the young man in grey stepped down onto the platform. He kept his eyes on the village. He walked straight past, close enough that Dec could have touched his sleeve, and Dec caught a glimpse, as the grey coat swung, of the silver chain across his waistcoat and, on the end of it, tucked into the pocket, the round edge of a watch.

He walked away up the single street of the village, his shoes leaving long, neat prints in the snow, and turned in at a gate, and was gone.

The inn was called Le Balancier, which Inès said meant the balance wheel of a watch, and it stood in the middle of the village opposite the church, a tall old house with green shutters and a painted sign of a little golden wheel. Inside it was hot and dark and smelled of woodsmoke and melted cheese and wet dog, and Dec had a moment of fear about the dog, on Inès's behalf, before he saw that it was a very old Bernese mountain dog asleep on the hearth and showing no interest in anything at all. Inès gave it a wide berth anyway.

The landlady was called Madame Jobin. She was perhaps sixty, built like a well-kept barn, in an apron with a pattern of edelweiss, and she looked them both up and down, and Dec's soaked trainers, and the bag of books, and then she smiled at them with the special warmth of a woman who has decided that two people are in love and is going to help.

"Two rooms," said Inès firmly. "Please."

"Of course, of course." Madame Jobin was already reaching for the keys. "On your honeymoon? Everyone comes to the plateau on their honeymoon. It's the quiet."

"No," said Inès.

"Ah, well," said Dec, at exactly the same time, "it's not..."

There was a short silence. Madame Jobin looked from one to the other, and her smile grew, if anything, even warmer.

"Rooms three and four," she said. "They have a door between. It locks." She winked at Dec. "From both sides."

Inès took the key to room three and went up the stairs without a word, and Dec stood in the hall holding the key to room four with his ears going hot, and the Bernese mountain dog opened one eye and looked at him with enormous pity and closed it again.

The room was small and very clean, with a wooden ceiling and a bed under a vast white feather quilt, and a radiator that he wrapped his feet round for twenty minutes until he could feel them again. The door between his room and Inès's was old pine with a little iron bolt on his side. He looked at the bolt. It was a nice bolt, hand-forged, eighteenth century probably, and he examined it with great professional interest for some time in order not to think about anything else.

At a quarter to ten there was a knock on it. From her side.

He drew the bolt. She was standing in the doorway in a long grey jumper, with her glasses on and her hair sticking up at the back where she had been lying on it, and the word honeymoon stood there in the doorway between them, and they both stepped carefully round it.

"Turn off your light," she said. "Come and look."

He turned off his light and followed her to her window. Her room was dark too. The village lay below them under the snow, blue and silent, with a light on in the church porch and one in a workshop window down the street. Beyond it, the valley climbed away into the dark between its wooded ridges. And at the far end of it, high up, where in the afternoon he had seen the black tower against the snow, a small light was moving.

It moved slowly. It stopped, and went on, and stopped. It went in behind the line of the broken wall and came out the other side. Then it went up, very slowly, as if someone were carrying it up a stair inside the tower, and it came to rest near the top, and stayed there, a single yellow point in the black.

"The priory," Inès said quietly. "Combe-Noire. Madame Jobin says nobody goes there in winter. The road's closed. The snow's two metres deep on the track."

"Somebody's going there," said Dec.

They stood at the window, side by side in the dark, not touching, and watched the little light at the top of the tower. It burned there for a long time, steady and patient, the way the chalk mark had been patient, and then, at what Dec's phone told him was exactly nine o'clock, it went out.
