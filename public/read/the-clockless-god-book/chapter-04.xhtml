The Man with No Hands on His Watch

The Irishman was sitting on the bottom step of the atelier's staircase at ten to eight on Tuesday morning with two paper cups of coffee and a newspaper, and Inès's first thought, which she was not proud of, was that he looked like a large untidy dog that had been told to wait.

"I brought coffee," he said, standing up too fast and slopping some of it. "To say thanks. Properly. For yesterday. I didn't know how you take it, so I got one black and one with everything in it, and you can have whichever, and I'll have the other, or I'll have both, I'm not fussy."

"How did you find me?"

"You told Pichon. Atelier Morel, rue Vieille-du-Temple. I've a very good memory for addresses. It's a locksmith thing. You remember where the doors are." He held out the black one. She had not said which. He had guessed. She took it, mostly to stop him holding it out. "Also I wanted to show you something. Two things. Well. One thing and a bad thing."

"Which first?"

He handed her the newspaper, folded open to the fourth page.

It was a small item, one column, under a photograph of the Seine with a police launch on it. Notary found dead near the Pont Marie. A man of sixty-two had been taken from the water below the quai d'Anjou early on Monday evening by the river brigade. He had been identified as Maître Gérard Lassalle, notary, of the quai d'Orléans. Police believed he had suffered a heart attack while walking by the river and fallen in. There were no signs of violence. His family asked for privacy.

Inès read it twice. The coffee went cold in her other hand.

"He was my client's broker," she said. "For the painting. The one with the clock under the varnish. He telephoned Morel on Saturday to ask if we could be faster."

"Yeah," said Dec. "I thought he might be. That's the other thing." From inside his leather jacket he produced a large brown envelope, creased down the middle, and showed her the front. It was addressed to M. D. Hartley at an address in the eleventh, and in the corner, printed in small dignified capitals, was the return address. Étude Lassalle, Notaires. 18, quai d'Orléans, Paris 4e.

"The parts list," he said. "From the Fondation. It came from his office. I never noticed. Why would I notice? Who reads the back of an envelope?"

"I do."

"Well, you would." He said it without any malice at all, almost admiringly. "So the man who sent me the gear with the mark on it, and the man who sent you the painting with the mark in it, is the same man, and he's fallen in the river. Of a heart attack. The day the clocks stopped."

Upstairs, four floors up, a door banged. Morel. She heard him start on the stairs and stop on the first landing, and she knew he would have the newspaper too, and she knew, with a cold little certainty, what his face would look like.

"I'm going to the quai d'Orléans," she said.

"Grand," said Dec. "I'll come with you."

"I didn't ask you."

"No, I know. But I've a good feeling about this door."

The quai d'Orléans ran along the south side of the Île Saint-Louis, where the old houses of the island turned their pale stone faces to the river and the back of Notre-Dame. Number eighteen had a tall carriage door painted a glossy dark green with a brass plate beside it, Étude Lassalle, and a smaller door cut into the big one for people who were not carriages.

The small door was not locked. It was not even shut. It stood an inch open on a dim courtyard paved with cobbles and a staircase going up on the left.

"Huh," said Dec, and his voice changed.

They went up. Second floor, a door with a second brass plate, and the door of the office was open too, not an inch this time but all the way, and from the landing Inès could see in.

She had been in rooms that had been burgled. Every restorer had; paintings attracted thieves the way jam attracted wasps. Burgled rooms were stupid. Burglars pulled out drawers and tipped them on the floor and took the television and left. This was not that. Someone had been through Maître Lassalle's office the way she went through a painting: square by square. The tall bookcases had been emptied onto the carpet, but the books lay in rows, spines up, each one opened and laid flat. The filing cabinet stood with every drawer pulled out to exactly the same length. The pictures had been taken down from the walls and stood against the skirting with their backs turned out, the brown paper on each one slit neatly down the middle and folded back.

It was patient. It was careful. She thought of the chalk drawing in Pichon's photographs, and her mouth went dry.

"Somebody's been looking for something," Dec said quietly behind her.

"Yes."

"And they didn't find it."

She turned. "How do you know?"

"Because they're still looking. Look at the desk." He nodded past her. "They've done the room in a ring. Bookcase, cabinet, pictures, round and round. They've done every drawer of that desk, see, they're all out. And they've stopped. They've not done the desk itself. Which means either they were interrupted, or..." He went past her into the room, carefully, stepping between the open books, and stood in front of the desk with his head on one side. "...or they don't know what this is."

It was a big desk. Nineteenth century, mahogany, with a leather top worn pale where a man's elbows had rested for forty years, and a high back of pigeonholes and tiny drawers, all of which had been pulled out and stacked on the floor.

"It's a Second Empire bureau," Dec said, and she heard in his voice the same thing she heard in her own when she put a painting under the raking light. "Paris-made. There was a workshop in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine made hundreds of these for lawyers and notaries, and every one of them has a secret drawer, because every lawyer in Paris wanted one, and every one of them's in the same place, because the workshop only knew the one trick. You take out the middle pigeonhole. Yeah? Then you reach in the hole..." He reached in, up to the elbow, frowning at the ceiling. "...and at the back, on the left, there's a little wooden peg. You push the peg, and..."

There was a click.

A slim panel at the base of the pigeonholes, which Inès would have sworn was solid wood, dropped forward half an inch on a hidden hinge. Behind it lay a shallow drawer no deeper than a book.

Dec looked at her, and his face was so pleased that for a moment she almost forgot the dead man in the river.

"Every desk of that make," he said. "Every single one."

In the drawer there were three things.

The first was a pocket watch: silver, plain, heavy, on a short chain. Dec lifted it out and turned it over and it swung, shining, in the grey light from the window. Its face was white enamel, with twelve black numerals in a circle. It had no hands. Not broken hands; no hands at all, and no little post in the centre where hands would go, as if it had been made that way.

The second was a card, stiff and cream-coloured, with deckled edges. Printed in grey: La Fondation Vaulmont vous prie de lui faire l'honneur d'assister à la Veillée de l'Éclipse. Panthéon. Le premier avril. Quinze heures précises. The Fondation Vaulmont requests the honour of your presence at the Eclipse Vigil. The Panthéon. The first of April. Fifteen hundred precisely.

The third was a scrap of paper, torn from a notebook, with three words in a cramped old-fashioned hand: Barrière d'Enfer. La neuvième niche.

"The gate of hell," said Dec, reading it over her shoulder. "The ninth niche. That's cheerful."

"The Barrière d'Enfer is a place," Inès said. "It was one of the old toll gates in the city wall. At Denfert-Rochereau. Where the catacombs are."

"Oh, that's cheerful."

She took the watch out of his hand. It was warm, as the gear had been warm, from his pocket, from the drawer, from whoever had held it last. She ran her thumb round the rim of the case and found the catch for the back, and pressed it.

Then the floor of the landing creaked.

Not loudly. A single plank, under a single weight, the way an old house speaks when something big stands on it carefully. Inès looked up and saw, in the doorway, a man.

He filled it. He was enormous, taller than Dec by a head and twice as wide, in a black wool coat, and he was completely bald, his skull shining in the light from the landing. He had small pale eyes and no expression whatever. On the side of his thick neck, above the collar of the coat, was a tattoo in faded blue ink, a little wooden house with a pointed roof and a tiny bird coming out of a door in it. A cuckoo clock.

He looked at the open drawer. He looked at the watch in Inès's hand.

He did not say anything at all. He simply came into the room.

"Right," said Dec, very quickly, "right, okay, window."

She would remember afterwards that he had grabbed her hand, and that she had let him, and that she had been too frightened to be annoyed about it.

The window was a tall French one, and it opened onto a narrow stone balcony, and the balcony ended at the corner of the building where a stretch of grey zinc roof sloped up to the next house along. Dec went over the balcony rail like a man climbing a garden wall. Inès went after him because the alternative was the bald man, and found herself on the roof of the Île Saint-Louis with the Seine forty feet below on one side and a courtyard full of bins fifty feet below on the other.

Her knees did something they had not done since she was nine years old on a Ferris wheel in Lyon.

"Don't look down," said Dec.

"Basszus," said Inès, who had looked down.

They went along the ridge of the roof on their hands and feet, the zinc cold and wet under her palms, past a row of chimney pots and a satellite dish and a terracotta pot of dead geraniums somebody had put out to die in style. Behind them the window banged. She did not look back. She heard the zinc boom under a great weight and knew without looking that the bald man had come out onto the roof and that he did not run, he walked, and that he was not hurrying because he did not need to.

At the end of the ridge, the roof stopped.

There was a gap. A light well between two buildings, a square shaft going straight down past windows and drainpipes to a tiny courtyard at the bottom. It was not wide. A metre. A little more. On the far side, the next roof began, slightly lower, with a skylight in it propped open on a stick.

Dec jumped it. He did it without thinking, long-legged, landing hard and skidding on the wet zinc and catching himself on the frame of the skylight. Then he turned round and held out both hands.

"Come on."

"No."

"Inès."

"I can't."

"You can. It's a metre. You've walked further than that to get a coffee."

"Not over a hole." Her voice had gone up and thin and she hated it. "I'm afraid of heights. I don't do this. I clean skies. I don't..."

The zinc boomed behind her. Close. She looked round.

The bald man had stopped, three metres back along the ridge. He was not looking at her. He was crouched beside the gutter, where a strip of plastic netting had been fixed along the edge of the roof to keep birds off, and he was looking at a pigeon. A grey city pigeon, caught by one foot in the net, beating its wings against the zinc in a frantic grey blur.

As she watched, the huge man reached out with both hands, slowly, the way you reach for a sleeping child, and folded the pigeon's wings against its body to calm it. With one thick finger he worked the netting off its foot. Then he held it a moment against his coat, and opened his hands.

The pigeon went up past Inès's face in a clatter of wings.

And Inès, without deciding to, jumped.

For a second there was nothing under her but the light well, and her stomach stayed on the other roof. Then Dec had her by both wrists and she hit the zinc on her knees and they both went over sideways against the skylight, and the stick holding it open fell out, and the skylight banged shut on Dec's elbow and he swore in a long fluent rush of Galway English that seemed to have no full stops in it at all.

When she looked back across the gap, the bald man was standing on the other side. He looked at the gap. He looked at them. He did not jump. He took a phone out of his coat, and turned away, and began, very slowly, to walk back the way he had come.

They came down through the skylight into somebody's attic, full of trunks and a dressmaker's dummy, and down a service stair into a different courtyard, and out through a different carriage door onto the quai de Béthune, and then they walked very fast in no particular direction until they reached the Pont de Sully, and then they stopped in the middle of the bridge and held onto the parapet and breathed.

"You jumped," said Dec, after a while.

"Yes."

"You said you couldn't."

"I couldn't. And then I did." She took her glasses off and wiped the rain off them on her scarf with hands that would not quite keep still. "Don't make a thing of it."

"I'm not making a thing of it." He was grinning. "It's a small thing. It's a tiny thing. It was a metre."

"It was a metre and twenty."

"I'll grant you the twenty."

She put her glasses back on. Her other hand had closed, she found, round the silver watch, so tightly that the chain had printed little links into her palm. She had not let go of it the whole way across the roof.

She opened her hand and pressed the catch on the back of the case, as she had been going to do in Lassalle's office before the floor creaked.

The back sprang open.

Inside, where a watchmaker would put his name and the serial number, there was no movement at all. The case was empty: no wheels, no spring, nothing to make it go. And on the inner face of the lid, engraved very finely into the silver, was a circle of nine small rings, side by side like a row of tiny wheels, and round the edge of each ring, in letters smaller than grains of rice, ran the alphabet.

"Dec," she said. It was the first time she had used his name. "What is this?"

He bent over her hand. He was quiet for so long that she looked up, and his face had gone serious in a way she had not seen before.

"That," he said, "is a lock."
