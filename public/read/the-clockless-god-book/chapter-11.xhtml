The Grey Communion

The tea came at four o'clock on Saturday afternoon, on a tray, with a knock.

Dec was alone in room four. Inès had gone up to the museum after lunch with the brown page from the tower in a plastic sleeve, to sit with Mère Clothilde and a magnifying glass and a Latin dictionary the size of a breeze block, and to telephone her mother, and possibly Pichon, and possibly not. She had slept until eleven. So had he. When he had come down to breakfast at half past, she had been sitting by the window in the dining room in the grey jumper with her hands round a bowl of coffee, and she had looked up at him, and he had looked at her, and they had both very carefully talked about the weather for twenty minutes, and not about anything said in a freezing tower about fathers. It had been, Dec thought, a very good conversation about the weather.

So he was alone when the knock came, lying on the bed under the huge white quilt with Roux's drawings spread out on top of it, turning the wheels of the lock in his head.

"Oui?"

Nobody answered. He got up and opened the door. There was nobody in the corridor. There was a tray on the floor: a white china teapot, a cup and saucer, a little jug of milk, two biscuits on a doily, and a folded card. Avec les compliments de la maison, pour vous réchauffer. With the compliments of the house, to warm you up.

"Ah, Madame Jobin," said Dec to the empty corridor, touched, "you're a dote," and carried it in.

He poured a cup. He put milk in it. He sat on the edge of the bed with Roux's drawings and drank half of it in one go, because he was still cold somewhere deep in his bones from the night before, and it was hot, and he was thinking about wheels.

Then he stopped.

He sat very still with the cup halfway down to the saucer and moved his tongue round his mouth.

Cloves.

Faint. Under the tea. Sweet and dusty, like Christmas in a church. And behind the cloves, something flat and mineral and cold, like the taste of a wet stone, like licking a roof.

Everything tastes of cloves and slate, said Roux, in his head, a little deaf and very angry. Like licking a church roof.

Dec put the cup down on the saucer, very carefully, because his hand had started to shake. Then he got up, crossed the room in two strides to the windowsill where Madame Jobin kept a large pot of red geraniums, stuck two fingers down his throat, and was sick into the geraniums with an energy and thoroughness he would later describe to Inès, when she asked, as professional.

He knelt on the floor by the window, gasping. His eyes were streaming. His heart was going like a train.

How much had he drunk? Half a cup. How much was enough? He had no idea. Roux had lost a day. The judge had lost a day. He could feel nothing yet, nothing but sick and frightened, but his head felt, maybe, a little light, a little far away, the way it did after the second pint, and he did not know if that was the tea or the fear.

He thought: they'll come.

He thought: whoever left that tray is going to come back in a while to see if it worked. To do what they did to Roux. The clocks. The chalk.

He looked at the tray, and the teapot, still nearly full.

He thought about it for perhaps five seconds. Then he poured the rest of the tea from the pot down the sink in the corner, all but a finger's depth at the bottom, and rinsed the sink, and put the pot back on the tray. He refilled his cup to the brim with water from the tap, poured half of that away and set the half-empty cup on the bedside table where a man would leave it who had drunk half and fallen asleep. He ate both biscuits, because a man who had drunk the tea would have eaten the biscuits. Then he lay down on the bed on top of the quilt, on his side, facing the door, with one arm hanging off the edge, and half-closed his eyes, and waited.

It was forty minutes. He knew because he could see, through his eyelashes, the little travel clock on the bedside table, and he watched the minute hand go round, and it was the longest forty minutes of his life, including the night in the tower.

His head stayed light. Once or twice the room seemed to tilt, very gently, as if the inn were a boat, and then tilted back. He bit the inside of his cheek and held on.

The key turned in the lock.

The door opened. Brother Fabien came in, in his grey coat, and closed it quietly behind him and stood there, looking at Dec on the bed, for a long moment. He had a ring of keys in his hand, a big iron ring that Dec had seen hanging on a hook behind Madame Jobin's desk downstairs, and a canvas bag over his shoulder. His narrow face was calm and attentive and almost kind, like a nurse checking a ward.

He went to the bedside table and looked into the cup. He looked into the teapot. He nodded once, to himself.

Then he went to work.

Dec watched him through his eyelashes and did not breathe more than a sleeping man breathes. It was quick. It was practised. Fabien took Dec's watch off the bedside table, a cheap digital thing with a rubber strap, and pressed its buttons until it said 09:00, and then, with a small screwdriver from the bag, took the back off and did something inside it, and closed it again. He picked up the travel clock, stopped it, wound its hands to nine, and from a little tin in his bag took a sliver of brass no bigger than a matchstick and pushed it into the back of the movement. On the wall by the door hung a small cuckoo clock that Madame Jobin had evidently thought would be charming for honeymooners; Fabien stood on a chair, opened its little door, set its hands to nine, and wedged its pendulum with another sliver. He did all this without hurry and without a sound, the way a man lays a table who has laid a great many tables.

Then he picked up Dec's phone.

Dec's heart did something it had never done before, which was stop and then start again twice as fast.

Fabien thumbed the screen. It was locked. He did not seem troubled. He took Dec's limp hand from the edge of the bed, gently, as you would take a child's, and pressed Dec's thumb to the button, and the phone opened.

He went to the photographs. Dec watched him scroll through them, the black notebook, page after page, the graphs and the tests and the Panthéon, and saw nothing at all change in his face. Then, one by one, with his thumb, the young man deleted them. Then he went to the deleted folder, and deleted them again. Then he went into the settings, and changed the phone's clock to nine o'clock, and turned off its automatic time, and put it back on the bedside table exactly where it had been.

Last, he opened the wardrobe, and from his bag took a stick of white chalk, and on the bare pine on the inside of the door, carefully, patiently, with his head on one side, he drew a circle, and twelve small marks round it, and at the top, IX.

He stood back and looked at it. He seemed satisfied.

Then he went to the door and opened it and looked back at Dec once more, and Dec saw his lips move. He was saying something, very quietly, in Latin. A prayer, maybe. Or the thing Vey had cast round the bell. It might have been for Dec. It might have been for himself.

The door closed. The key turned.

Dec lay on the bed, staring at the chalk mark on the inside of the wardrobe door, and found that he was so angry he could no longer feel his hands.

He gave it a count of sixty. Then he rolled off the bed, pulled on the German geologist's boots and his leather jacket, opened the window over the geraniums, and climbed out onto the roof of the porch.

It was not a rooftop chase. He would tell Inès this afterwards, firmly. There was no chasing. There was a short, undignified slide down the snow on the porch roof, a drop of two metres into a snowdrift, and a long cold walk, crouching, behind a row of parked cars and a woodpile and the back wall of the church, keeping the grey coat in sight.

Fabien was walking up the single street of the village in the blue dusk, quite openly, with his canvas bag over his shoulder, past the lit windows of the workshops. He walked to the end of the village, where the street became the track up the valley, and turned off it towards a small dark house on the edge of the forest with smoke going up from its chimney. The forester's house. A big black four-by-four was parked outside it.

Kessler, Dec thought. He did not take the train. He drove.

Fabien did not go in. He stopped by the woodpile at the side of the house, where, Dec supposed, the signal was better, and took out a phone, and made a call. Dec crept along the side of a hay barn until he was ten metres away, behind a stack of logs taller than he was, and crouched in the snow and listened.

Fabien spoke French, in a low, respectful voice, the voice of a junior clerk on the phone to a senior partner. Dec's French was not good enough to catch all of it. He caught enough.

"...yes, the Irishman. This afternoon. Half the pot at least. He'll sleep until tomorrow night... No. The photographs are gone. I saw to it." A pause. "The woman, no. She's at the museum with the old nun. She'll find him in the morning. She'll find the chalk." A pause, longer. "Yes. Yes, I understand. And Morel? ...He'll have the word by Sunday? Good. And then? ...Yes. Of course. As the rule says."

Dec, behind the logs, went cold all over again.

"...and the guests," Fabien went on. "Yes. I have the list here. The minister. The prefect, yes, he's confirmed. The two bankers from the Bourse, the journalist from the television, the cardinal's secretary... The catering is ours. The wine is served at half past two, as agreed. Half an hour before None. When they wake they'll remember the eclipse, and the dark, and the clocks stopping, and your voice. Only that. And they'll tell it, all of them, on camera, the same way. A miracle." His voice warmed. It was the first time Dec had heard any warmth in it at all. "And the Seventh will collect. Nine minutes of every station and every signal in Paris, and the markets in a panic. The fund's positions are..." A pause. "Of course. Forgive me. It's not my concern."

Another pause.

"The train? Oh, it worked beautifully. I got off at Saignelégier, Brother Kessler picked me up, we were at the tower in twenty minutes. They walked straight in. Mutuo datum, Brother."

He hung up.

Dec shifted his weight, very slightly, to ease the cramp in his leg, and his foot, in the German geologist's too-big boot, slid off the frozen log it was resting on, and the stack of logs gave a long, slow, creaking groan, and the top three rolled off it into the snow with a sound like somebody knocking on a door.

Fabien's head came round.

There was a door in the side of the hay barn, low and wooden, and it was not locked, and Dec went through it without thinking and pulled it shut behind him in the dark.

It was not a hay barn. It was a cellar. Stone steps went down, three, four, five, and at the bottom he was in a long low vaulted room, cold and damp and absolutely black, and the air was full of a smell so thick he could almost lean on it. Cheese. Hundreds of cheeses. As his eyes adjusted to the tiny grey light from a grating high in the wall he saw them, rows and rows of them on wooden shelves from floor to ceiling, tall round cheeses like little drums, each one sitting on its own board in the dark, ripening, in perfect silence.

He backed into the far end of the vault between two racks, and crouched down, and made himself as small as a man of six foot two can be made.

The door at the top of the steps opened. A torch beam came down.

It moved slowly along the rows. Over the cheeses. Over the boards. Over the floor, stone flags, wet and shining. It stopped a metre in front of Dec's knees, where his footprints went, dark on the damp stone, and stopped there.

Then it went away again, slowly, up the steps.

"Rien," said Fabien's voice above, to somebody. "Cheese."

A grunt. A very large, very low grunt.

The door shut. Dec heard the wooden bar drop across it on the outside, clunk, and then boots in the snow, and the deep cough of the four-by-four's engine starting, and lights sweeping across the grating high in the wall, and then going away down the valley.

He sat among the cheeses in the dark for a long time.

"Locked in," he said to them, finally. "Again. Brilliant. That's three times this week. That's a personal best."

The cheeses did not reply.

He got up, and went up the steps, and felt the door all over in the dark. It had a bar on the outside and nothing on the inside, not even a handle. No keyhole. He sat down on the top step with his back to it and thought, well, at least it's not minus twenty, and then, it's about minus two, though, and then, I'm going to die in a cheese cave, and they'll put that on my headstone in Galway.

He was still sitting there twenty minutes later when he heard an engine. Not the four-by-four: something older and louder and clattering, with a slipping fan belt that screamed on the corners. It came up the track and stopped outside. A door slammed. Boots crunched. Then the wooden bar lifted, and the door opened, and Dec fell backwards out of it into the snow at the feet of Inès Varga, who was holding a torch in one hand and, for reasons he never did establish, a ski pole in the other.

Behind her in the dusk, with its engine running and its headlights on, was a battered blue farm van with Fromagerie Cattin painted on the side, and in the driver's seat, in her enormous boots and her cardigan, with a cigarette in her mouth, sat Mère Clothilde.

"How," said Dec, from the snow, "did you..."

"I went back to your room," said Inès. "To tell you about the page. There was chalk in your wardrobe. There was sick in Madame Jobin's geraniums. And your window was open, and there were footprints in the snow going away from it, and they were the German geologist's boots, and they went up the street. So I followed the footprints." She looked down at him. "They stopped at the logs. Then they went in here. Your feet are very easy to follow. They're very big."

Dec sat up in the snow.

"They know," he said. "Inès. They know we know. And they've wiped my phone."

"Get in the van," said Mère Clothilde through the window, not unkindly. "Arguing in the snow is for Protestants."

They drove down to Delémont through the dark in the van that smelled of cheese and diesel, with Mère Clothilde driving the switchbacks at a speed that made Dec hold on to the door handle and Inès hold on to Dec. There was a night train to Basel at nine. There was a first train to Paris in the morning.

In the back of the van, in the dark, sitting on a pile of empty cheese crates, Dec told her everything. The tea, the geraniums, Fabien and his keys and his screwdriver, the phone, the call by the woodpile. The minister. The prefect. The wine at half past two. They'll remember only the miracle. The fund. And Morel will have the word by Sunday. And then, as the rule says.

When he had finished, she was quiet for a long time.

"Your phone," she said at last.

"Wiped. Every picture."

"Mine isn't." She held it up in the dark. The screen lit her face, very pale, and her glasses. "I charged it at Clothilde's this morning. It died in the tower, but it kept what was on it." She scrolled with her thumb. "The ledger. The REMIT page, the last lines before the burn. And the two pages of the notebook you took on mine in the tower before it ran down, before you switched to yours: the list of tests. Vincennes, the quai de la Rapée, Austerlitz." She put it away. "Not the flyleaf. Not the Panthéon page. Those were on yours." A pause. "It's not enough. It's not nothing."

"It's not nothing," Dec agreed.

The van went round a hairpin. The crates slid. She slid with them, against him, and stayed there.

"I was frightened," Dec said, into the dark, quietly, so that Mère Clothilde in the front would not hear. "In the room. Lying there. When he took my hand to open the phone. I've never been so frightened in my life."

Inès did not say anything for a moment. Then she said something in Hungarian, low and quick, three or four words.

"What does that mean?"

"Nothing," she said. "It's Hungarian. It doesn't count."

But her hand, in the dark, on the crate beside his, had found the sleeve of his jacket, and was holding on to it, and it did not let go all the way down the mountain.
