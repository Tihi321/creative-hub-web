The Vigil

At seven o'clock on the morning of Wednesday the first of April, a security guard in a black fleece looked at Dec's pass for a long time, and at Dec, and at the pass again, and at the three wooden crates on the trolley behind him, which were stencilled FRAGILE and CECI N'EST PAS UNE BOMBE, which had seemed funny in the workshop in Montreuil in February.

"Escape room," said Dec helpfully. "For the guests. In the crypt. Pour s'amuser."

The guard looked at the stencil.

"It's a joke," said Dec. "A French joke. Magritte. The pipe." He mimed a pipe. "Ceci n'est pas une pipe. It's art."

"It's a crate," said the guard, and waved him through, because the pass said ACCÈS TOUTES ZONES in red, and because it was seven in the morning, and because, Dec thought, nobody in the history of the world has ever wanted to argue with a man about Magritte at seven in the morning.

The Panthéon at seven in the morning was the biggest room he had ever been in.

He had been inside once before, in February, a tourist with a ticket, and had walked round with his mouth open like everybody else. It was different empty. The nave went away from him in every direction, pale stone under pale stone, columns as thick as oak trees going up and up into vaults painted with saints and kings in faded gold and blue, and above it all, at the crossing, the dome: an enormous hollow of light so high that the painted figures on its underside were the size of thumbnails. And from the very top of the dome, from a point so high he could not see it, a single steel cable came down, straight and thin as a pencil line, sixty-seven metres, all the way down to a brass sphere the size of a football that swung, very slowly, a hand's breadth above a ring of brass on the floor.

The pendulum went past him with a soft, deep, patient rush of air. It went away. It came back.

Dec stood and watched it for a long time.

The Earth turning, the sign said beside it, in four languages. Foucault, 1851. The pendulum keeps its own plane of swing. It is the floor, and the building, and the city, and you, that move beneath it.

The vigil people were already at work. Caterers' vans had been let into the side court, and men in white jackets were carrying in folding tables. A television crew was laying cable along the north aisle and arguing about light. In the middle of the nave, a few metres from the pendulum's brass ring, a low stage had been built, carpeted in grey, with a lectern on it and a microphone, and behind the stage, on a tall easel draped in grey cloth, a painting-shaped shape.

At the foot of the main stair to the dome, the wide one in the south transept, roped off with a red cord and a sign, ACCÈS INTERDIT, a huge bald man in a black wool coat was standing with his hands folded in front of him, perfectly still, like a piece of the building.

He looked at Dec across the nave. Dec looked back.

Neither of them did anything at all. Then Dec pushed his trolley towards the stairs to the crypt, and the bald man watched him go, and did not move, and the pendulum went past between them, and came back.

He finished the cell by eleven. The last of his three crates, the one with four hundred spare brass gears in it for the players to rummage through, would not fit in the chapel, so he left it in the narrow passage between the catering and the south transept, by the wall. It was a good room. He would allow himself that, later. A clockmaker's cell from the thirteen hundreds, three metres by four, built into the old north-east chapel of the crypt: stone-effect walls, a workbench covered in brass gears and tools, a little window of painted parchment, a wooden door with a real iron lock, and a clock on the wall with nine hours on its face. That had been in the brief. He had thought it was a quirk of the client's. He looked at it now and felt sick.

At a quarter past eleven, alone in the room with the door shut, he knelt on the floor, lifted out the panel he had built into the planks in February as a maintenance hatch for the wiring, and lay on his stomach and reached down into the dark, past the new timber, to the old iron underneath.

The grille's lock was every bit as rusted as it had looked from below. It took him nine minutes. He counted them, because he found he had started counting everything in nines and could not stop. When the bolt went back he did not open the grille. He left it closed, unlocked, under his floor, and put the hatch back, and sat on the floor of his own room for a moment with his back against the wall of his own making.

A way out. Nobody would need it. But it was there.

The guests began to arrive at half past one.

The light was already going strange. It was a bright day, a hard blue April sky, and the sun came down through the high windows of the Panthéon in long slanting beams full of dust. But there was something wrong with it. It was too sharp. Every shadow had gone thin and crisp and slightly blue, as if the whole building had been redrawn with a finer pen. Outside, through the great bronze doors standing open on the square, Dec could see people gathering: hundreds of them, in the sunshine, in coats, holding up little cardboard glasses with black film in them and pointing them at the sky.

Inside, the people were different.

He watched them come in from the shadow of a column in the south aisle, with his contractor's pass round his neck and a clipboard he had brought for the purpose of looking busy. They came in pairs and threes, in dark coats and good shoes, and the guards at the door did not ask them for their passes because the guards knew their faces from the news. A tall grey man with a tired face whom Dec recognised from somewhere, a minister of something. A round confident man in a uniform with a great deal of silver braid, who must be the prefect of police, and who glanced once round the nave as if checking it for exits and then smiled at everyone. Two men in very similar navy suits with very similar haircuts who talked only to each other. A priest in a black cassock with a purple sash who moved like a man who was used to people getting out of his way. Women in pearls. A famous television presenter with a famous orange tan. Perhaps a hundred of them, standing about the nave in the strange sharp light, talking in low voices and laughing at things, and looking up, every now and then, at the dome.

And moving among them, greeting each one, silver-haired and calm and smiling, in a grey suit with a silver chain across the waistcoat, Auguste de Vaulmont.

Dec watched him the way he had once watched a very good locksmith in Dublin work on a safe: with a professional's sick admiration. He never hurried. He never raised his voice. He touched elbows, briefly. He bent his head to listen. He laughed at the right moment and not too long. Every person he spoke to came away from him standing a little straighter, looking a little pleased, as if something had been lent to them.

Then Inès came up out of the north aisle with a clipboard of her own, and Dec stopped watching Vaulmont.

She was wearing a dark green dress that he had not known she owned and her navy coat over it, and the mustard scarf, of course, and her grey ankle boots, of course, and the tortoiseshell glasses. She had her condition report on a clipboard and a pair of white cotton gloves in her pocket and the expression of a woman who would personally kill anyone who put a wine glass within two metres of her painting. She took up a position beside the draped easel behind the stage and stood there with her clipboard held to her chest like a shield, and every so often she glanced at the pendulum as it went past, and away, and back.

She did not look at Dec. That was the arrangement. But once, when the pendulum swung between them, she lifted her eyes and met his, just for a second, across the whole width of the Panthéon, and then went back to her clipboard.

He found he had stopped breathing again. He made himself start.

The catering was in a side chapel off the south transept, behind a screen. Dec had found it in the morning. He went back at two o'clock with his clipboard and stood in its doorway looking busy, and watched.

There were six people in white jackets. Five of them were what they looked like: waiters from an agency, young, bored, underpaid, carrying out trays of tiny things on spoons. The sixth was a thin, pale, fair young man in a white jacket that did not suit him, standing at a long trestle table at the back with his back to the room, and Dec's stomach turned over when he saw him, because he would have known that narrow straight back anywhere.

Brother Fabien.

The catering was the Fondation's own, Moussa told him later; the fair young man had come up from Switzerland on Monday and taken charge of the wine himself.

On the trestle table in front of Fabien there were two kinds of wine. One kind was ordinary mulled wine, red and steaming, in big steel urns, with a stack of plain glasses beside it and a printed card: Vin chaud. The other was on the far end of the table, by itself, in four tall jugs of hammered pewter: a wine darker than the mulled wine, almost brown, with a faint grey sheen on its surface like the bloom on a plum. Beside the jugs stood rows of small pewter cups, a hundred at least, each one with a clock face engraved on it, and on each clock face, at the top, IX. A card in beautiful copperplate: Le vin de la Veillée. The wine of the vigil.

The whole side chapel smelled of cloves.

Dec turned away before Fabien could turn round, and went back out into the transept, and found that he had walked straight into one of the five underpaid waiters, a tall, gangly young man of about twenty with a shaved head and a silver tray of spoons, who said, "Pardon, monsieur," and then, looking at Dec's face, "Ça va? You're white."

"I'm Irish," said Dec automatically. "We're always white." Then he looked at the waiter properly. He had a badge on his white jacket: MOUSSA. "Moussa. The pewter cups. The brown wine. When does it go out?"

Moussa looked at him for a moment, then glanced back at the side chapel, then lowered his voice.

"Half past two. On silver trays. Only to the guests on the list, the man said." He jerked his chin towards Fabien's back. "Not to staff. Not to press. Only the list. He's very particular. He said if any of us drinks it we're sacked." He shrugged. "I smelled it. Like a church at Christmas. And this morning I saw him put something in the jugs from a little paper bag. Grey. Like ash." He looked at Dec. "Why? What is it?"

Dec thought of six different lies, and was too tired to tell any of them.

"It's a drug," he said. "It makes you lose a day. They gave it to an old clockmaker last week, and a judge, and a priest. And they're giving it to the minister and the prefect at half past two so they'll remember what they're told to remember and say it on the telly." He swallowed. "I need to swap the trays. Before half past. And I need one of those jugs, full, for the police. And I can't do it on my own."

Moussa looked at him for a long moment, holding his tray of spoons.

"How much," he said slowly, "do you think I'm paid for this?"

"Not enough."

"Eleven euros forty an hour," said Moussa. "Before tax. And he shouted at Fatou this morning because she put the napkins the wrong way." He looked at Fabien's back again, and something in his young face set hard. "What do you need?"

What Dec needed, it turned out, was a collapse.

At twenty-two minutes past two, a wooden crate stencilled CECI N'EST PAS UNE BOMBE, which had been parked by a young man with a shaved head in the narrow passage between the side chapel and the south transept, was knocked over by the same young man, with great force and theatrical surprise, and burst open. Four hundred brass gear wheels came out of it. They came out in a wave, bright and ringing, across the stone floor of the passage and into the side chapel and under the trestle table, rolling and spinning and clattering, hundreds of little bright wheels, a sound like all the coins in the world being dropped at once.

Every waiter went down on hands and knees. Fabien spun round from the table, white with fury, and came round it shouting, and went down too, scrabbling at the gears before they could roll out into the nave where the minister was. For forty seconds every eye in the side chapel was on the floor.

It was enough. Dec was a man who had built rooms where forty seconds was a whole puzzle. He came in through the door behind the screen, past the backs of the waiters, and took the four pewter jugs off the end of the trestle table two at a time and put them in the empty crate behind the screen, and took the four steel jugs Moussa had filled with ordinary mulled wine from the urns at a quarter past and stood them where the pewter ones had been. The mulled wine was red, not brown. He tipped into each jug a spoonful from a jar of the cocoa powder the catering had for the hot chocolate, and stirred it with a pewter cup, and it went brown-grey and dull, and from three metres away under a pewter lid it would do. Then he went out again behind the screen, and shut the crate, and sat on it.

When Fabien stood up again, red-faced, with his hands full of brass wheels, the four jugs stood at the end of the table where they had always been, and the side chapel smelled of cloves, and a young waiter called Moussa was apologising to him, very sincerely, at great length, in French and Wolof.

At half past two, the trays went out.

Dec watched from behind his column in the south aisle. He watched Moussa and the four other waiters carry the silver trays of little pewter cups out into the nave, into the strange thin light, and offer them, with a small bow, to the guests on the list. The minister. The prefect. The priest with the purple sash. The two men in navy suits. The famous presenter. He watched them take the cups and look at the clock engraved on the side and smile, and lift them, and drink.

He watched Vaulmont, across the nave, beside the stage, watching them drink. And he saw Vaulmont's calm face relax very slightly, the way a man's face relaxes when the last wheel of a lock falls into place.

In his pocket, his phone buzzed. A text. A Paris number. Where are you. Pichon.

He typed back with his thumb, not looking. Inside. Swapped the wine. Have 4 jugs of the real thing in a crate, south transept, behind the screen. Need you. Now.

The answer came at once. Lunch, it said. And then, a second later: Coming.

At twenty to three the light began to go. Not totality; that was still eleven minutes off. The deep end of the partial phase, the sun a thin bright sickle behind the moon.

It did not go out. It drained. As if someone were slowly turning down a dimmer switch on the whole of Paris. The long beams of sun through the high windows went from gold to a thin, sick, silvery yellow, and the colours of the painted saints in the vaults went grey, and in the great bronze doorway the crowd in the square outside went quiet, one person after another, like a wave going out. The air in the nave got colder. Dec felt it on the back of his neck.

Vaulmont went up onto the low grey stage. He stood at the lectern under the dome, with the steel cable of the pendulum going up past him into the darkening light, and the brass sphere sighing past behind him, and the television camera's red light on, and he did not tap the microphone or clear his throat. He simply began to speak, and the whole of the Panthéon went still to listen.

"Time," he said, "is not ours."

His voice in the microphone filled the nave. Warm oil. Poured slowly.

"We speak of spending it, of wasting it, of saving it. We are right to. Every hour of every life is lent. Lent to us. And today, my friends, for a few minutes, the light that measures our hours will be taken away, and we will stand together in the dark and remember that it was never ours to keep."

Dec looked away from him. He looked across the nave, across the stage, at the draped painting behind it, and at Inès beside the painting with her clipboard held against her chest.

And he looked past her, at the foot of the dome stair, where the bald man stood behind his red cord. And he saw, as he looked, a thin pale figure in an ill-fitting white jacket come quickly out of the south transept and up the aisle along the wall, not hurrying, but not strolling either, towards the stair. Fabien. Fabien with his narrow face set and turned, scanning the crowd. Scanning the columns. Scanning the south aisle.

The narrow face found Dec's.

It stopped. It did not change. But Dec saw the young man's lips move, and saw him lift his hand to his collar, where a wire ran up to his ear, and speak into it.

At the foot of the dome stair, Kessler's great bald head turned, slowly, towards the south aisle.

It was fourteen forty. Dec knew because he looked at the clock on his phone, and then, without meaning to, at the brass sphere of the pendulum as it went by, as if it could tell him.

When he looked back at the stage, Inès was gone.

He found her a second later. She had put down her clipboard on the easel. She was walking, without hurrying, without looking at anyone, along the far side of the nave, along the north aisle, in her green dress and her mustard scarf, in the dying light. She was walking towards the other stair. Not the roped one in the south transept that Kessler stood guard on. The one on the north side, behind the pillar, the narrow service stair to the gallery of the dome that went up and up and up inside the wall, sixty metres, round and round, with a rope for a handrail and nothing at all on the other side.

The stair she was most afraid of.

She did not look back at him. She did not need to. She put her hand on the rope, and set her grey boot on the first step, and went up into the dark.
