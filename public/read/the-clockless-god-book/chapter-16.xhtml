Totality

The stair went round to the left, and up, and round to the left, and up.

It was cut inside the thickness of the wall, a stone spiral barely wider than her shoulders, lit every twenty steps by a small caged bulb, and the rope that served for a handrail was fixed to the outer wall with iron rings and was old and greasy with a hundred years of hands. On the inner side there was no wall. There was the newel, the stone column the steps turned round, and between the newel and the edge of each step, a gap, a hand's width, and through the gap, if she looked, darkness going down.

She did not look.

She counted. That was what she had decided, on the first step, with her hand on the rope and her heart going so hard it shook her vision: she would count steps the way she counted stitches, the way she had counted round Vey's wheels with a pencil at midnight. One step, one number. Nothing else. No down. No dark. Just the next number and the next stone and the rope.

Forty-one. Forty-two.

Her legs were shaking by sixty. By a hundred her breath was coming in short dry gulps, and the old cold tightness was in her chest like a fist, and the stair had begun, very gently, to tilt, the way the roof on the Île Saint-Louis had tilted. She stopped and leaned her forehead against the outer wall, against the cold stone, and closed her eyes.

Basszus. Basszus. Basszus.

Her father had taken her up the bell tower of Saint-Jean in Lyon when she was nine. She had made it to the first landing and frozen, and he had not carried her down. He had sat on the step beside her, a big man in his bus driver's jacket, and sung under his breath in Hungarian until she stopped shaking, a song about a horse, a long one, and then he had said, Well? Are we going up or down? and she had said down, and he had said All right, and they had gone down, and he had never once mentioned it again.

She was not nine.

She opened her eyes and went up.

A hundred and forty. A hundred and forty-one.

The stair came out, at last, through a low arch, onto the gallery of the dome, and Inès put her hand on the arch and did not let go of it for a long moment.

It was a walkway. A ring of stone a metre and a half wide that ran all the way round the inside of the dome, high up, where the curve of the great painted ceiling began. On its inner edge there was a balustrade of carved stone, waist high, with fat round balusters like chess pieces, and gaps between them. And beyond the balustrade, there was nothing. There was the whole inside of the Panthéon, falling away. Sixty metres of air, grey and darkening, going down to a floor so far below that the people on it were the size of grains of rice. The stage. The tiny lectern. The little red eye of the television camera. And in the very centre, directly below the crown of the dome, the brass ring on the floor and the brass sphere of the pendulum sliding across it, no bigger from up here than the head of a pin.

The steel cable came up out of that depth past her, a few metres beyond the balustrade, in the empty middle of the dome. Straight and thin and humming faintly. Up past the painted saints. Up to the very top, to the small dark circle at the crown where it was fixed.

And from the crown, coming down the curve of the ceiling to the gallery on the far side of the ring, she saw a second cable. Black. Thicker. Not humming. Clipped to the ribs of the dome every few metres with new steel brackets.

It ran down to a cabinet.

The cabinet stood against the wall of the gallery, a third of the way round the ring from her, where the dome met the drum. It looked like a piece of furniture. That was the first thing she thought: someone has carried an antique up here. It was the size of a tall narrow wardrobe, made of brass panels, dark and gleaming, with engraved scrollwork round its edges and a domed top like an old clock case, and a pair of brass doors in its front. On the doors, set into the brass at chest height, a cylinder of nine thin wheels. And on the top of the domed lid, engraved in a circle of brass the size of a dinner plate, a clock face. No hands. Nine at the top.

Through the seams of the brass panels, faintly, a green light glowed. And from inside, very low, so low she felt it in her teeth more than heard it, came a hum.

Horlogerie Vaulmont Frères, said the permit in her coat pocket. Installation temporaire d'un dispositif d'observation de l'éclipse.

Inès let go of the arch.

She walked along the gallery towards the cabinet with her left hand on the wall and her right hand held out from her body a little, the way you hold out a hand on a boat, and her eyes on the wall, on the stone, on the curve of the floor ahead of her feet. Not on the balustrade. Not on the gaps between the balusters. Not on the long grey fall beyond them. She could feel it there on her right like a cold draught, the whole of that empty space, pulling at her, and she gave it nothing. The wall. Her hand. Her feet.

Below her, far down, Vaulmont's voice came up from the loudspeakers, enormous and tiny at once, filling the dome.

"...and when the light is taken from us, my friends, we will see, perhaps for the first time, what we truly owe..."

She reached the cabinet at fourteen forty-seven by her phone.

She stood in front of the nine wheels. They were brass, thin, finely cut, with the old alphabet running round each rim in tiny crisp letters: Roux's work, sharp and perfect. She had been right. There was no play in them at all. She put out her hand to the first wheel and her fingers were shaking so much she could not hold it.

She took a breath. She held her right wrist with her left hand, the way she did when she had to lay in a line of gold leaf on a halo and could not afford to shake, and she put her finger on the first wheel and began to turn it.

R.

Behind her, along the gallery, from the direction of the south stair, the one he had been guarding, she heard footsteps.

He came round the curve of the gallery out of the dim, and he had to stoop under the arch of the stairhead, and he had to turn his shoulders a little to fit between the wall and the balustrade, and he did not hurry. He never hurried. He stopped six metres from her and stood there, with his hands loose at his sides, filling the gallery from wall to rail, as he had filled the doorway of Lassalle's office, and the hatch of the tower.

Brother Kessler.

He did not look at the cabinet. He looked at her. His small pale eyes had no expression in them at all.

Inès took her finger off the wheel.

She did not decide to. Her hand simply came away, and then her feet were moving, backwards, away from him, away from the cabinet, along the gallery, her left hand trailing on the wall, and he came after her. One step. Then another. Slowly, as you follow a cat you do not want to frighten into the road.

She went round the ring. There was nowhere else to go. Away from the cabinet. Away from the stair she had come up, which was behind him now. Round the inside of the dome with the empty air on her right and Vaulmont's voice coming up out of it and the bald man's slow, heavy, patient steps on the stone behind her, and her whole body shaking now from her teeth to her knees, so hard she thought the shaking would throw her over the balustrade by itself.

"...nine minutes, my friends. Only nine. The hour of None..."

Down in the nave, someone gasped. Then another. Then the whole crowd, a hundred voices, and outside the great doors, in the square, thousands, a single long rising sigh, like wind through trees.

The light went out.

It went out of the dome first. One moment there was grey light coming in through the high windows of the drum, thin and silvery; the next, it was gone, as if a hand had been laid over each window, and the dome was black. Not night black. Something stranger. A dark blue, deep and glowing, like the inside of a mussel shell, with a faint ring of pale fire showing through the windows where the sun had been. The painted saints went out. The gallery went out. Inès could see her own hand on the wall and nothing else.

And in the dark, below her, the clocks began to die.

She saw it, because there was nothing else to see. Through the great open doors of the Panthéon, at the far end of the nave, a long rectangle of blue dusk with the crowd black against it, the traffic lights at the end of the rue Soufflot had been green, and they went out, and then all of them came back at once on red, flashing, flashing, the whole length of the street. On the façade of the Lycée Henri-IV to the side, the big lit clock face that had said a quarter to three went blank, then lit again, and said nine o'clock. In the nave, on the wall beside the stage, a large handsome station clock had been hung for the vigil, with FONDATION VAULMONT painted under it in gold: she had noticed it in the morning and thought it vulgar. Its second hand stopped. Its minute hand swung, all by itself, smoothly, all the way round, and stopped. Its hour hand followed. Nine.

People had their phones out. She could see the little white rectangles of the screens all over the nave like fireflies. And over the sigh of the crowd, faint, she heard someone say in French, high and wondering, The stations. Look. Every board at Montparnasse says nine. And Lyon. And the hospitals, look, they're saying the Pitié...

"Mutuo datum," said Vaulmont's voice out of the dark, soft and enormous. "Reddetur."

In the dark of the gallery, close beside her, something exploded.

It exploded into her face, all wings and noise and dust, and she threw her arms up and cried out and staggered sideways, and her hip hit the balustrade, and for one long endless second there was nothing on her right but the blue dark and sixty metres of it. She grabbed. Her hands found the stone of a baluster and closed on it and held, and she was on her knees on the gallery with her arms round a fat stone pillar like a child round a parent's leg, and her glasses were hanging off one ear, and the thing that had exploded was still exploding, an arm's length away, beating itself against the stone.

A pigeon.

A grey city pigeon, caught in the netting the conservators had strung across the gaps between the balusters to keep the birds out. Caught by a wing and a foot in the fine black mesh, in the dark, beating and beating against the stone, mad with terror, its small heart going so fast she could see its breast flutter.

Inès stared at it from a hand's breadth away.

Then she let go of the baluster with one hand.

She did not think about the sixty metres. That was what she would remember, after. Not that she was brave. That for a few seconds the sixty metres simply went away, because there was something in front of her that was more frightened than she was. She reached into the netting with her free hand, slowly, the way the bald man had reached on the roof on the Île Saint-Louis a week and a lifetime ago, and folded the bird's wings against its body, and felt it go still under her palm, all its heart. With her fingers she worked the mesh off its foot. Then the wing. It took a long time. Her hand was shaking. The bird let her.

She stood up with it held against her coat.

Kessler had stopped two metres away. He was a black shape in the blue dark, huge and still. She could not see his face. She could see the shape of his head, turned towards the bird.

Inès held the pigeon out to him. In both hands. Not throwing it. Holding it out, the way he had held out his hand to her in the tower for the notebook. Palm up. Waiting.

For a long moment nothing happened.

Then the huge shape moved. Two great hands came out of the dark and closed round the bird, very gently, over her fingers, so that for a moment her hands were inside his, and they were very warm. Then she let go, and he had it.

He stood there with the pigeon held against his chest. He did not move towards her. He did not move at all. He stood there in the blue dark in the gallery of the dome, with the little bird in his hands and his head bent over it, and he was still, utterly still, as still as a man in a church.

Inès went past him.

She went past him along the gallery, between his great shoulder and the balustrade, close enough to touch, and he did not put out a hand, and she did not look down, and then she was past, and running. Running round the ring in the dark, her left hand on the wall, towards the cabinet and its green-glowing seams and its hum, which she could feel now in the stone under her palm.

Ahead of her, at the stairhead on the far side, a torch beam swung up out of the arch and came onto the gallery, wild and bobbing, and behind it came a man, tall and lanky, gasping like a burst bellows, with his auburn hair stuck to his forehead and a contractor's pass swinging round his neck.

"Inès," Dec shouted.

"The cabinet," she shouted back. "Behind you. It's Roux's lock. You can't pick it. Use the word."

He swung the torch round. It found the brass doors, the nine wheels, the engraved clock with no hands. It found his face, white and wet, and she saw him understand.

And it found, coming up out of the same arch behind him, unhurried, silver-haired, a small dark pistol held loosely in one gloved hand, Auguste de Vaulmont.

Inès did not stop running. She had seen, as the torch swung, a third way down: a low iron door in the east pier of the gallery, standing open, with a sign on it for the conservators, and beyond it the top of a narrow stair going down inside the pier to the nave. And the nave was where the microphone was. And the camera. And the painting, under its grey cloth, with the word scraped out of it.

You look at the door. I look at the painting.

She turned in at the iron door, and did not look at the sixty metres, and went down the conservators' stair two steps at a time into the dark.
