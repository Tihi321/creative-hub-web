Mère Clothilde's Museum

Inès woke at six because the church bell across the road struck six, very close and very slowly, and lay under the enormous white quilt in the dark listening to it, and counted, and was relieved when it stopped at six.

She gave up on sleep. She got up and put on the grey jumper and two pairs of socks and sat at the little table by the window with her laptop, the screen turned down as dim as it would go, and opened the photographs.

This was the part she liked. She would not have said so to Dec, who liked doors and talking and jumping over things. She liked this: the quiet, a cup of something going cold, an image enlarged until it stopped being a picture and became a landscape of cracks and ridges, and the slow work of walking across it. She went to photograph twenty-three, raking light from the left, and enlarged it until the gap in the Latin line filled the screen.

MVTVO DATVM, then the gap, then HORA NONA.

The gap was not empty. That was the thing she had not had time to see in the atelier, with Morel watching through his glass wall. Whoever had scraped out the word had scraped down through the paint, but not through the ground beneath it, the chalky preparation layer the painter had laid on the canvas before he began. And a painter who letters a motto does not just paint the letters. He draws them first. He rules lines. He marks out the spacing with little ticks, so the word will fit.

The ticks were still there. Faint, in the ground, like footprints left after the snow has gone.

She counted them three times. Nine. Nine letters, evenly spaced, and at the start of the gap the faint ghost of a downstroke and a curve, which could be a P, or an R, or a B. At the end, a short horizontal at the foot of the last letter, which might be an E, or an L.

Nine letters. A word for what happens to a loan, at the ninth hour.

She wrote R/P/B _ E/L in her notebook and looked at it until the church bell struck seven, and she heard Dec through the connecting door, falling over something, and swearing, and then singing in the shower in a voice of astonishing confidence and very little tune.

His landlord had been right about that.

The Musée de l'Heure was in the old schoolhouse at the top of the village, a square stone building with a bell on the roof and a playground under the snow, and its door had a hand-painted sign on it, Ouvert quand c'est ouvert: open when it's open. It was open. Inside, it ticked.

It ticked the way Roux's shop ticked, only more so, because the clocks here were older and bigger and less polite. There were tall-case clocks from Jura farmhouses, painted with roses and tulips. There were watchmakers' benches with tools laid out on them as if the watchmakers had gone for lunch a hundred years ago and not come back. There was a glass case of pocket watches in rows like eggs. There was a whole wall of cuckoo clocks, which Dec stopped in front of with an expression of deep personal suspicion.

And there was Mère Clothilde.

She came out of a back room with a coffee pot in one hand and a cigarette she was not smoking in the other, a small, round, energetic woman of about seventy-five in a grey cardigan and a long grey skirt and a pair of enormous snow boots, with a small silver cross on a cord round her neck and short white hair that stood up like a dandelion clock. Her face was brown and creased from a lifetime of mountain sun, and when she saw them her eyes went bright and quick and amused, and Inès had the sudden strong feeling that she had been expected.

"The couple at Madame Jobin's," said Mère Clothilde in French, with the soft broad vowels of the Jura. "The honeymoon that isn't. Coffee?"

"We're not..." said Inès.

"Of course not. Nobody is, at first." She was already pouring into three cups that did not match. "Sit, sit. You're here about the priory."

Inès sat. Dec sat. Inès looked at the old nun over the rim of her cup.

"How do you know?"

"Because nobody comes to Les Bréviaires in March except for the priory," said Mère Clothilde comfortably, "and because the young man in the grey coat who has rented the forester's house, a Monsieur Ruel, Fabien, he signed the commune's register like a schoolboy, came in here yesterday afternoon and asked me whether two people from Paris had been asking about it. I said no. It was true, then." She lit the cigarette at last, from a match, and blew the smoke away from them politely. "I don't like him. He reads timetables for pleasure. What do you want to know?"

Inès took the silver watch out of her coat pocket and put it on the table between the coffee cups.

The old nun set her cigarette down on a saucer and folded her hands on the table, well away from the watch. The amusement went out of her face slowly, the way light goes out of a valley when a cloud crosses it, and what was left was older and much more tired.

"Ah," she said. "Yes. Then you'd better have the whole of it."

She had the whole of it, it turned out, the way some families have a whole silver service: polished, complete, kept in a drawer for centuries and taken out only for funerals.

"Combe-Noire was a little priory," she said. "Twelve monks, a few lay brothers, a mill, some sheep. Nothing. A hole in the hills. In thirteen forty-eight the plague came up the valleys from Basel, and by the spring of forty-nine half the plateau was dead. You know what people believed then? That God was angry. That it was punishment. And one of the monks of Combe-Noire was a clockmaker, which was a very new and very strange thing to be in those days, and his name was Anselm Vey."

"The lock," said Dec, very softly.

"The lock, the clock, everything. He was clever. Cleverer than anyone in the valley. And he was frightened, like everybody, and he had a new idea about why." Clothilde tapped her cigarette on the edge of a saucer. "He preached that God had not sent the plague in anger. He said God was a lender. That He had lent mankind time, every hour of every life, as a loan, and now the loan was being called in. Hour by hour. And that the only ones who would be spared were the ones who paid ahead."

"Paid how?" said Inès.

"In hours." The old woman's mouth went thin. "In obedience. In work. In money, later, a great deal of money. And Vey built a clock to keep the account. The Debt Clock. He put it in the priory tower so the whole valley could hear it, and it had nine hours on its dial, not twelve, because the ninth hour was the hour of the reckoning. He rang the hours for the valley. When to work, when to pray, when to eat, when to pay. Before the clock, the peasants had the sun. After the clock, they had Vey. Whoever holds the clock," she said, "holds the time of everyone who can hear it."

Inès thought of Roux, in his shop, saying every radio clock in France listens to the one voice. She kept her eyes on her cup. She could feel him not looking at her.

"And those who didn't pay?" she asked.

"They were given the Grey Communion." Clothilde said it flatly, as one says the name of a disease. "Datura seed, steeped in wine with cloves to hide the taste. And ash. Ash from the burnt accounts of those who had died owing, so that the debt went down into the living. That's what turns the tongue grey. A man drank it and lost a day. He woke with no memory of the day and the clock stopped at the ninth hour, and the monks told him the hour had been taken from him as payment, and he believed it. Of course he did. What else could he believe? He had the day missing. He had the taste in his mouth." She looked at the watch on the table. "And then he paid."

The cuckoo clocks on the far wall began, one after another, to strike ten. Nobody spoke until they had all finished.

"What happened to them?" said Dec. "The monks?"

"My order happened to them." Something that was almost a smile came back into Clothilde's face. "The Sisters of the Visitation had a house in Saignelégier. In sixteen hundred and ten the bishop sent us up to Combe-Noire with a great many soldiers and a letter, and the brothers were turned out, and the sisters walled up the clock in its tower and broke the stair. The Brotherhood didn't die. Such things never do. They went down to the cities. Basel, Geneva, Lyon, Paris. They stopped being monks and became bankers, which is, if you think about it, much the same thing with better shoes." She shrugged. "And my sisters spent four hundred years trying to forget them. I'm the last of the sisters in the Jura. So I suppose it's my turn to forget."

"You haven't, though," said Inès.

"No," Mère Clothilde agreed. "I'm very bad at it."

"There's a story," Clothilde said later, when the coffee pot had been refilled twice and Dec had wandered off to the far end of the room, "that Vey repented. At the end. That he was an old man by then and he'd watched what his Brotherhood had become, and he wrote one last line, a sort of confession, and they scraped it out. It's only a story. The sisters told it to each other in the evenings, I suppose, to feel better."

Inès's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"What was the line?"

"Nobody knows. That's the point of the story." The old nun smiled. "It was in his book. The great ledger of the Debt Clock, where he wrote the hours paid and the debts owed. My sisters burnt half of it when they walled up the clock. The other half they couldn't find."

Vey kept it in his book, Roux had said.

Inès put the cup down. She opened her notebook to the page with R/P/B _ E/L on it, and she showed it to the old nun, and told her about the painting in Paris: the clock on the sundial with a nine where its twelve should be, the Latin line under it, mutuo datum, hora nona, and the scraped word.

Clothilde looked at the notebook for a long time.

"The ninth hour," she said at last. "Hora nona. You know what that is, in a monastery? Not nine o'clock. The monks counted the hours from dawn. Prime, the first hour, six in the morning. Terce, the third, nine. Sext, the sixth, noon. And None, the ninth hour, three in the afternoon. That's when the brothers rang the reckoning. Three o'clock."

Inès sat very still.

Her hand went to the cream card in her pocket and stayed there. She knew it by heart. Panthéon. Le premier avril. Quinze heures précises.

Fifteen hundred. Three in the afternoon. The ninth hour.

"They've bought it," Clothilde said quietly, watching her face. "The priory. Did you know? Last autumn. The commune sold the ruin to a foundation from Paris for a song, a cultural heritage foundation, and everybody was very pleased, because the roof of the school needs doing. A foundation with a name like a wine. Valmont. Vaumont."

"Vaulmont," said Inès.

"That's it." The old nun reached into the pocket of her cardigan and brought out something heavy that clinked, and put it on the table beside the watch. It was a key: iron, black, as long as her hand, with a ring at one end and a heavy bit cut in a pattern like a little castle. "The abbess's key. To the tower. The new door they put in when they took the clock's stair down. They changed every lock they could last autumn, the foundation did, with a locksmith from Basel. But they couldn't change this one, because it's in the wall, and the wall's listed, and the commune wouldn't let them. I don't suppose it'll do you any good." She pushed it across the table. "But it's been in my drawer for forty years, and I'm tired of it."

Inès found Dec at the far end of the room in front of a small glass case she had not noticed, standing very still with his hands in his pockets, the way he had stood in front of Roux's drawings.

In the case, on a bed of faded red velvet, was a box. It was a reliquary, she thought: a little casket of dark wood bound with iron straps, the size of a shoebox, the kind of thing a monastery would keep a saint's finger in. And on its front, set into the iron, was a lock. A brass cylinder with nine thin wheels side by side on it, each wheel with tiny letters round the rim, worn almost smooth by hands that had been dead for six hundred years.

"It's his," said Dec, without turning round. "Vey's. It's the same as the drawings. It's the little brother of the one Roux made." His voice was strange: quiet and almost reverent, the way hers went in front of a painting nobody had cleaned for three hundred years. "Look at the wheels. Look how thin he cut them. With a file, by hand, by candlelight, in a plague." He leaned closer, until his breath fogged the glass. "And he's left it open. Look. The shackle's out."

"Can you read the word on it?" Inès asked. "If it's open, the letters are still set."

Dec was quiet for a moment.

"Somebody's spun them," he said. "All nine. After. You can see where the wear stops." He straightened, slowly. "This one doesn't tell you anything. It just tells you he was very good."

Mère Clothilde had come up quietly behind them in her snow boots, the coffee pot in one hand.

"The sisters found it in his cell," she said. "Open. Just like that. As if he'd opened it himself before he died, and left it." She looked at the little box with an expression Inès could not read. "My predecessor used to say it was the only honest thing in the whole priory. A lock that had given up."

Through the tall schoolhouse window, beyond the snow and the village and the dark ridge of spruce, the black tower of Combe-Noire stood against a sky the colour of pewter, and it was, Inès thought, a very long way up.
