Pichon Listens, Sort Of

Pichon listened to the whole thing through a plate of andouillette.

He did not interrupt. Dec had expected him to. He had expected sighs, and the business with the reading glasses, and Monsieur Hartley said in the voice of a man diagnosing a rash. Instead the commissaire sat behind his desk on the fourth floor of the glass building on the rue du Bastion at a quarter past twelve on Monday the thirtieth of March, with a napkin in his collar and a sausage on his plate that smelled, Dec thought, like a farmyard that had been through something, and ate, slowly, and listened, and kept his eyes on Dec's face the whole time.

Dec told him everything, from the chapel to the armchair in Vincennes. The notebook with ALS162 on its first page. The thirteen strokes of the bell. Morel saying Sunday to a wall. Pichon chewed through all of it, and stopped only once, at the Seventh will collect, when he laid his knife across the plate and sat with his jaw quite still until Dec had finished the sentence.

When he had finished, Inès put her phone on the desk, with the photographs of the ledger page and the two pages of the notebook. Then, from the inside pocket of her coat, she took a small memory stick, and put that beside it. Then she sat back in her chair with her hands in her lap and her face perfectly still, the way she had sat in front of Vaulmont, Dec supposed, and waited.

Pichon put down his fork.

He picked up the phone and went through the photographs, slowly, with his glasses on the end of his nose, enlarging each one. The ledger. REMIT. The list of tests. 26/03. Gare d'Austerlitz + bd de l'Hôpital. 07h51. 9 min. OK. Aucune réaction. He read that one twice, his lips moving on 07h51. Then he put the phone down and took off his glasses and let them fall on their cord.

"Morel," he said, "is at the Hôtel-Dieu. I went this morning. He does not know his name. The doctors say datura, a large dose, much larger than the others, larger than Monsieur Roux. He may come back. In a day. In a week." He spread his hands. "He may not."

Inès took off her glasses, looked at them, and put them back on.

"And Lassalle." Pichon reached into a drawer and took out a thin folder and opened it and left it lying under his hand, as though he already knew it by heart. "I asked for the full autopsy on Friday morning. After a station clock at Austerlitz said nine o'clock for nine minutes, and after a little text from a restorer who told me she was with her mother." He did not smile. "It came back on Saturday. Datura in the blood. Water in the lungs. He was alive when he went into the Seine, and he did not walk there. There are marks on his upper arms where two hands held him. Large hands." He closed the folder. "Gérard Lassalle was murdered. I have told my superiors so. In writing. On Saturday."

"And?" said Dec.

"And on Sunday morning," said Pichon, "I received a telephone call from the office of the prefect of police." He picked up his fork again and looked at it. "A very polite call. From a very polite young man. The prefect, I was told, takes the greatest interest in the case of the unfortunate notary and is confident that the heart attack will be confirmed. The prefect also takes a great interest in the cultural life of the capital and will be attending, on Wednesday, a private eclipse vigil at the Panthéon given by a foundation of the highest standing, whose president is a personal friend. And the prefect's office has received, from that foundation, a complaint." He put the fork down again. "About an Irish national who has been harassing its staff and trespassing on its property in Switzerland. Your name, Monsieur Hartley, is now on a list. Not my list. Theirs."

Dec felt his stomach drop the way it had on the zinc roof.

"So what happens now?"

"Now," said Pichon, and he stood up, and took the napkin out of his collar, and folded it, and put it on the desk, "I arrest you."

It was not like the first time.

The first time, in Roux's shop, there had been something almost comic about it: the napkin, the cassoulet, Dec on his knees with the cutlery out. This time Pichon walked round his desk without a word and opened the door of his office onto the big open-plan floor where twenty officers sat at twenty desks, and said, in a voice that carried to every one of them, cold and flat and loud, "Declan Hartley. You are under arrest for obstruction of a criminal inquiry and for interfering with witnesses. Stand up."

The room went silent. Twenty heads came up.

Dec stood up. He did not know what else to do.

"Commissaire," said Inès sharply, getting up too, "that is not..."

"Sit down, Madame Varga, or you will join him." Pichon kept his eyes on Dec. He took Dec by the upper arm, quite hard, and walked him out through the middle of the open-plan floor, past all twenty desks, slowly, so that everybody saw. A young officer with a sandwich halfway to his mouth stared. A woman on the phone stopped talking. At the far end, by the lifts, a man in a good suit who did not look like a policeman was standing with a coffee, and Dec saw him watch them go by, and saw him take out his phone.

In the lift, going down, Pichon let go of his arm.

He stood in the corner of the lift with his hands in the pockets of his raincoat and looked at the floor numbers going down, and his face had gone grey and tired and old, and Dec, who had been about to say a great many things, looked at him and said none of them.

The cell was in the basement, and clean, like everything else in the building: a bench, a steel toilet, a small high window of thick frosted glass, and a door of steel with a narrow slot at eye level and a lock on the outside. Dec looked at the lock as he went in. Electronic. A card reader. No keyhole.

"I hate this building," he said.

"Everybody does," said Pichon. "It was designed by a man who had never been arrested." He did not close the door. He stood in the doorway with his hands in his pockets. "I'm sorry for upstairs."

"Are you?"

"The man by the lifts with the coffee," said Pichon, "is from the prefect's office. He has been in this building since Sunday, visiting. He has been very interested in my lunch. Now he has seen me arrest you in front of the whole floor, and he will telephone that to his master, and his master will telephone it to his friend, and his friend will believe, for perhaps a day, that I am a sensible man who knows which way the wind blows." He shrugged. "A day is something. He made his call from the lift lobby while we were waiting for the doors; I watched him do it. That is all I needed. On paper you will have been held for questioning and released at one o'clock for lack of evidence, and I will sign the form after my dessert. For a day nobody else will come looking for you, because as far as the prefecture knows, I have already dealt with you."

Dec sat down slowly on the bench.

"You believe us," he said.

"I believed you on the first day, Monsieur Hartley. When you told me about the tongue." Pichon took his hands out of his pockets and looked at them. "It is not a question of what I believe. I have a notary drowned by a man with large hands, and a restorer in the Hôtel-Dieu, and a clockmaker, and Judge Delorme, and Madame Aubry, and the Abbé Girardin. And I have photographs of a notebook with no notebook, and a ledger with no ledger, taken in Switzerland by an Irishman with lock picks and a restorer who lied to me about being in Lyon. And on Wednesday afternoon the minister of the interior, the prefect of police, a cardinal's secretary, two bankers and a television crew will be standing under the dome of the Panthéon as guests of a man whose foundation has given eleven million euros to the hospitals of Paris." He looked at Dec. "If I go to my superiors with a radio and a sermon about a fourteenth-century monk, they will laugh, and then they will take my badge, and then they will put a younger man in my chair who likes his lunch less. And then there will be no one at all."

"So what do we do?"

"We," said Pichon, "do nothing. I am a police officer. You are a prisoner." He reached into the inside pocket of his raincoat and took out a folded sheet of paper and held it out. "But a police officer reads a great many boring documents. And sometimes he leaves them lying about."

Dec unfolded it.

A photocopy of an official form. A works permit, autorisation de travaux, from the Centre des monuments nationaux, for the Panthéon. Dated the sixteenth of March. Valid until the second of April. Contractor: Horlogerie Vaulmont Frères, Genève. Nature of the works: Installation temporaire d'un dispositif d'observation de l'éclipse. Temporary installation of an eclipse observation apparatus. Location: Galerie haute du dôme, à proximité du point d'ancrage du pendule.

The upper gallery of the dome. Near the anchorage point of the pendulum.

"The pendulum hangs from the very top of the dome," said Pichon. "Sixty-seven metres of cable. The anchorage is a steel point, in the crown, above the gallery. Up there, a cable that long is a very fine thing to hang other things from." He paused. "An aerial, for instance. If one were a radio amateur from Brittany who had been asked to check some figures."

Dec looked up from the paper.

"I know nothing about radios," said Pichon. "I'm a policeman. I know about lunch." He looked at his watch, a battered steel thing on a leather strap, with hands. "It is twelve fifty-one. I'm going to have my dessert. There is a very good tarte Tatin today in the canteen, and I'm going to have it slowly, because I have had a difficult morning arresting a dangerous Irishman. I shall be some time." He stepped back from the doorway. "Mind the door, Monsieur Hartley. It's heavy."

He walked away up the corridor. Dec heard his shoes on the stairs. He did not hear the door close, because the door did not close. It stood open, a steel door with a card reader and no keyhole, wide open on an empty corridor.

Dec sat on the bench and looked at it.

He felt, he would tell Inès later, a number of things in that moment, most of them to do with relief and gratitude and a strange warm ache in the chest towards a fat tired man in a raincoat who had just risked his whole career over a tarte Tatin. But underneath all of those, small and hot and unworthy, there was something else. There was a professional grievance.

He had been looking at that lock since he came in. He had been working out, in the back of his mind, where the card reader's wiring would run, and what was behind the faceplate, and whether a strip of the foil from a chewing-gum wrapper and a lot of patience might not, in the right place...

And the man had just left it open.

"That's not fair," Dec said to the empty corridor. "That's not fair at all. I'd have had that."

Inès was waiting on the steps outside, in the thin March sunshine, with her coat buttoned to the throat and her scarf wound three times round her neck. She was very angry. He could see it from fifty metres. She had the stillness she got when she was angriest, the one that made her look like a painting of herself.

"He let you go," she said.

"He let me go." Dec held up the photocopy. "And he gave me this."

She read it. Her face did not change, but her eyes went to the end of the line and back again, twice. Galerie haute du dôme. À proximité du point d'ancrage du pendule.

"The aerial," she said. "The pendulum cable. It's a sixty-seven-metre aerial hanging in the middle of Paris."

"That's what he said. Well. He said he knows nothing about radios."

"He knows everything about radios." She folded the permit and put it in her pocket. "What else?"

"Lassalle was murdered. The prefect's man's watching him. And I'm on a list." He shoved his hands in his pockets. "If anybody from the prefecture sees me near the Panthéon on Wednesday, I'm done. Pichon can't help me twice."

Inès was quiet.

"So you can't go," she said.

"I didn't say that."

"You just said..."

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He took it out. It was an email. He opened it with a feeling that he had stopped being surprised by anything in this city, and read it, and then read it again, and then started to laugh, quite helplessly, there on the steps of the police judiciaire in the sunshine, until Inès took the phone out of his hand and read it herself.

Dear Mr Hartley,

Further to our telephone conversation, we are delighted to confirm the installation of your Clockmaker's Cell for the guests of the Eclipse Vigil. As discussed, the room will be installed in the north-east chapel of the crypt of the Panthéon, where Monsieur de Vaulmont hopes our guests will enjoy it during the afternoon. Your access to the building on Tuesday 31 March from 08:00 and Wednesday 1 April from 07:00 is confirmed. Please find your contractor's pass attached; it should be printed and worn visibly at all times.

With our warmest regards and Monsieur de Vaulmont's personal good wishes,

Mlle C. Ferrand

Fondation Vaulmont

Attached was a pass. A white card with a photograph of Dec on it that he had sent them in January, smiling like an idiot, with his name in bold capitals, and the word PANTHÉON across the top, and under it, in red, ACCÈS TOUTES ZONES.

All areas.

Inès looked at the pass for a long time.

"He's on a list," she said slowly, "that a man from the prefecture is watching. And Vaulmont has given him a pass to every room in the building."

"Yeah."

"He wants you there."

"Course he does." Dec took back the phone. He had stopped laughing. "Where he can see me. In the crypt, at the bottom of the building, in a little room I built myself with a door that locks, while he's up the top of it under the dome with his radio and his minister." He looked at the pass on the screen, at his own grinning photograph. "He knows exactly what I am. He thinks I'm a man who builds rooms for other people to get out of." He tapped the red letters on the screen. "And it's the only way in. Nobody from the prefecture's going to arrest a man Vaulmont's put on his own guest list with a pass round his neck. Not on Wednesday. His pass is the trap, and it's the door. Same thing."

"And are you?"

"I am," Dec said. "I am, Inès. That's the thing." He put the phone away. "That's the thing he's forgotten. I know every way out of that room. I drew them."
