Under the Varnish

Inès Varga liked the atelier best before eight in the morning, when the skylight was still grey and the only sound was the radiator knocking like a polite ghost. At that hour nobody asked her how far she had got.

She had got, as of seven fifty-two on Monday the twenty-third of March, about a third of the way across the sky.

The painting stood on the big easel under the north light, a canvas a little under a metre wide in a frame she had taken off and wrapped in tissue on Friday. Allegory of the Sundial, said the paperwork, French school, circa 1650, oil on canvas, private collection. A woman in a blue dress sat in a garden with one hand resting on a stone sundial, and with the other she pointed at the sky, where the sun ought to be. Behind her stood a ruined arch, a peacock, a little dog asleep in the grass, and a great deal of brown.

The brown was not the painter's. The brown was three hundred years of varnish, candle smoke and somebody's pipe, layered over the original like the skin on cold soup. Under it, where Inès had cleaned, the sky came up a clear, cold blue, almost green at the horizon, and the woman's sleeve was not brown silk at all but a startling saffron. It always moved her, a little, this part. She would not have said so to anyone. She said instead, out loud, to the woman in the painting, "You had better not have been retouched in the eighteen hundreds, because I don't have time for it."

The woman pointed at the sky and said nothing. They had an understanding.

Inès pushed her glasses up her nose with one knuckle, the only part of her hand that was not tacky with solvent gel, and bent to the next square of sky.

The sundial was the odd thing. She had noticed it on Friday and had been not-thinking about it all weekend, the way you not-think about a word you cannot quite remember. It was a very correct sundial, carved stone, with the gnomon casting a crisp shadow and the hours marked round the dial in Roman numerals. But it had no hours. That was to say, the numerals were there, I to XII, but the painter had left the shadow falling on nothing: not on a numeral, not between two, but on a smooth blank band where the hours should have run. A clockless sundial. An allegory, presumably. Of something. The paperwork did not say of what.

"How far have you got?"

She did not jump, because she had heard his shoes on the stairs, four floors of them, and because Jean-Paul Morel always came up the last flight more slowly than the others, as if hoping the top might have moved.

"Good morning, Jean-Paul. A third of the sky."

"Only a third." He came and stood beside the easel, a thin man in his fifties with a good grey cashmere sweater gone thin at both elbows, and looked at the painting with an expression she could not read. He smelled of coffee and the cigarette he had promised his doctor he no longer smoked. "En somme, Inès, the client, the client is very... Lassalle telephoned again on Saturday. He wishes to know if we will be finished by the end of the month."

"We said the fifteenth of April."

"Yes. Yes, voilà, we said, but the client has an event. On the first. A sort of exhibition. He would very much like..." Morel lifted his hands, let them fall. "It would be good for the atelier. To be quick."

"It would be good for the atelier not to take a scalpel to a seventeenth-century sky because somebody is having a party." She kept her eyes on the sky. "Who is the client?"

"Lassalle doesn't say. A private collection. You know how they are." He leaned closer to the canvas. Too close. His eyes went, she noticed, not to the clean blue patch or the saffron sleeve, but straight to the sundial, and stayed there. "And the... there's nothing unusual? Underneath? Old repairs, overpaint, that sort of thing?"

Now she did look up.

"Why would there be?"

"No reason. No reason. A painting of this age, always something. Voilà." He straightened and patted his pockets for the cigarettes he did not smoke. "I'll be in the office. Let me know. If there's anything. Anything at all."

He went into his glass-walled office at the far end of the room and closed the door and sat down in front of a dark computer screen. She could see him through the glass, sitting very still with his hands flat on the desk.

Inès turned back to the painting and thought about that.

At half past eight she gave up on the sky and wheeled the UV lamp over.

It was not, strictly, the next thing to do. The procedure was the procedure: clean, consolidate, examine, document, and only then argue with anything you found. But a restorer who never broke the order of things was a restorer who never found anything worth arguing with, and in any case Morel had asked. Anything unusual. Underneath. People did not ask that question idly. They asked it about paintings they already knew the answer to.

She drew the blind across the skylight. In the dark the lamp hummed and the painting changed.

Under ultraviolet, old varnish glows a milky green, and anything painted on top of it, later, shows up as a dark patch, like a bruise. The sky glowed. The woman glowed. The ruined arch and the peacock glowed, and the little sleeping dog, and the sundial.

Except in one place.

On the face of the sundial, in the blank band where the hours should have run, the green glow was broken. There was a dark ring there, very faint, and inside it marks. Twelve little marks, spaced round the rim like the hours of a clock.

Inès went very still.

She switched off the lamp and opened the blind halfway, and set up the raking light instead, a small hard lamp on a stand that she could angle almost flat to the canvas so that every ridge and dip in the paint threw a shadow. Paint remembers. Whatever an artist puts down and covers up, the brush marks of it stay in the layers like footprints under snow, and a light from the side will find them.

The light found a clock.

It had been painted on the stone of the sundial, and then painted out, carefully, and varnished over, and varnished again. But the ridges of it were there. A circle. Twelve hour marks. Hands? No, no hands. And the numerals, raised in the old paint like Braille: she could pick out XII at the bottom, which was wrong, and III on the left, which was wrong, and at the top, at the very top, where XII belonged, the stubborn little strokes of an IX.

Nine at the top.

Beneath the dial, along the base of the sundial's stone, where a sculptor might carve a motto, there was more. Letters, in a painted Roman capital, also painted out. She bent so close her breath fogged the glass of her loupe and she had to wipe it on her scarf.

...MVTVO DATVM...

A gap. Not painted out. Scraped. Someone had taken a blade to the paint there, centuries ago by the look of the cracks, and scratched one word down to the ground, so that the raking light found nothing but a rough little valley in the paint.

Then: ...HORA NONA.

"Mutuo datum," she said aloud, softly. Given as a loan. And at the end, hora nona. The ninth hour.

She stayed bent over the canvas until her back hurt. Then she straightened, and took out her phone, and photographed it. Raking light from the left, from the right, from above. UV. Normal light. With a scale bar. Without. Close on each letter. Close on the gap. Forty-one photographs, numbered and logged in the little notebook she kept in her apron pocket, because a photograph without a note was a rumour.

She kept it, she noticed, to herself, and left Morel in his office.

She noticed, too, that he had not come out to ask why the blind was down. He was sitting exactly as before, hands on the desk, and he was not looking at her, very hard.

At nine thirty-five the radio on the shelf above the sink said her street.

It was not exactly her street. It was the rue des Gravilliers, three streets north, but the voice said Marais and horloger and she turned it up with a wet hand.

"...an elderly clockmaker found this morning in a confused state in his shop, rue des Gravilliers, by neighbours concerned that the shop had not opened. Police are at the scene. According to a neighbour, the man had, quote, lost a day, and all the clocks in the shop had been stopped. Residents of the area will remember last October's case in the eleventh arrondissement, where a retired judge... the so-called Stopped Hour, a superstition which some..."

Inès turned the radio off.

The clockmaker in the rue des Gravilliers was Anselme Roux. Everybody in the Marais knew Roux, or knew of him, because he had been rude to all of them at one time or another. He had once told Inès's mother, in the street, that her wristwatch was an insult to the wrist.

She looked at the painting. At the clock on the sundial with its IX where the XII belonged, painted out by somebody who had not wanted it seen.

All the clocks in the shop had been stopped.

She told herself several sensible things. That it was a coincidence. That she had a third of a sky to clean. That clocks were, in Paris, quite common. Then she pulled off her gloves, took her trench coat from the hook, wound her mustard scarf twice round her neck and went down the four flights of stairs at a speed that would have made her mother say her name in the warning voice.

"Inès?" Morel called from his office as she passed. "Inès, where...?"

"Coffee," she said, and kept going.

There was a police car in the rue des Gravilliers, parked half on the pavement, and a small crowd of the kind Paris always produces from nowhere when there is a police car: two women with shopping trolleys, a man walking a very old dachshund, a delivery boy on a scooter with the engine running, and an elderly lady in a fur hat explaining loudly to everyone that she had telephoned them herself.

Inès did not like the dachshund. It was looking at her. She went round the other side of the women with the trolleys.

The shop was the one she remembered: narrow, dark green paint, ROUX & FILS, HORLOGERIE in faded gold on the glass. Through the window she could see clocks, dozens of them, and something about them was wrong in a way it took her a second to name. Then she had it. They were all showing the same time.

The door opened and the bell over it rang, a fat brass clang.

A round man in a beige raincoat came out first, with a pair of reading glasses swinging on a cord on his chest. Behind him came two uniformed officers, and between them, being steered by the elbow and not resisting so much as continuously apologising, came a man.

He was tall, too tall for the doorway, and he had to duck, and he did it a beat late and caught his head on the bell, which rang again. He was perhaps thirty-five, lanky, with a mop of dark auburn hair going in four directions and two days of stubble, and a battered brown leather jacket over a green jumper that had been washed so many times it had given up being any particular green. His hands were behind his back. He was talking.

"...no, I understand, I do, I'd arrest me too, honestly, on paper I look desperate..."

He saw Inès. She did not know why he picked her out of the crowd: possibly because she was the only one not holding a shopping trolley or a dog. He stopped dead on the pavement, so the officers had to stop as well, and gave her a smile of enormous hopeful charm and said, in French, "Madame! Excuse me. Could you be very kind and telephone to my client? Je suis en retard pour mon client. I am being arrested. It is not my fault. J'ai un rendez-vous dans onze heures."

His French was appalling. It was not the vocabulary, which was more or less there. It was the grammar, which had been put together with great confidence by someone who had been told about French grammar once, at a party.

"Je vais être en retard," Inès said. "You're not late yet. And dans onze heures means eleven hours from now. You mean à onze heures. Your client will be waiting until tonight."

He blinked at her. He had very green eyes.

"Right," he said in English, in an accent that could only be Irish, and from the west. "Right. Thanks. That's, that's actually very helpful. I'll know for next time."

"Will there be a next time?"

"Ah, God, I hope not." He looked at the round man in the raincoat. "Will there?"

"Walk, Monsieur Hartley," said the round man, very tiredly.

They walked him to the car. As they put a hand on his head to fold him into the back seat, something fell out of the pocket of his jacket and rolled across the cobbles, ringing, and came to rest against the toe of Inès's grey boot.

It was a brass gear wheel, about the size of a two-euro coin, bright and new-cut, with very fine teeth.

The car door shut. The car pulled off the pavement with a bump. The man with the auburn hair turned in the back seat and looked at her through the rear window, and she could not tell whether he was apologising or asking for help, and possibly he could not tell either.

Inès bent down and picked up the gear.

Not an antique, and not even a good copy of one: machine-cut, the teeth too sharp, the kind of thing a person might buy to make a prop look like a clock. But it was warm from his pocket, and stamped on one face, very small, was a mark she would have missed if she had not spent the last hour looking at its twin.

A circle. Twelve tiny ticks round the rim. And at the top, where twelve should be, two strokes and a cross. IX.

Inès stood on the pavement of the rue des Gravilliers among the shopping trolleys and the old woman in the fur hat, with the dachshund watching her, and turned the little gear over and over in her solvent-stained fingers.

Then she put it in her coat pocket, and went to find out where the police judiciaire did its lunch.
