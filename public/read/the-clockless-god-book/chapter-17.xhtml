The One Lock That Matters

The torch was shaking. Dec put it down on the floor of the gallery, pointing up at the brass doors of the cabinet, so that at least the light would stay still even if he could not.

Fourteen fifty-six, said his phone.

Behind him, a step. Then another. Unhurried. A soft voice, a little out of breath from the stair and not at all troubled by it.

"Monsieur Hartley. Please don't touch that."

Dec turned round.

Auguste de Vaulmont stood three metres away along the gallery, in the blue eclipse dark, in his grey three-piece suit, with his silver hair a little disordered from the climb. He was holding a small pistol, dark and neat, in his gloved right hand. Not pointing it, exactly. Holding it the way a man holds his reading glasses at the end of a meeting: as a thing he might need in a moment. His face, in the up-glow of the torch, was as calm as Dec had ever seen a face. Below him, beyond the balustrade, the great hollow of the dome fell away into dark, and in the dark the people in the nave were a murmuring sea of little phone lights, and the loudspeakers were silent, because the man who had been speaking into them was standing here.

"It's a very good lock," Vaulmont said. "You'll have noticed. Monsieur Roux is a genius, and a very rude one. You could stand there with your little picks until Easter."

"Yeah," said Dec. "I know. I noticed."

"Then step away from it. Please. In four minutes it will be the ninth hour, and the debt will be called, and the whole city will have heard it. And after that, I promise you, nothing very bad will happen to you at all. You'll go back to Ireland. You'll build your rooms. You'll find, I think, that the contract with the Fondation pays rather better than you were told." He tilted his head. "We are not monsters. We simply keep accounts."

Dec looked at the pistol, and then at the man's face, and then back at the cabinet behind him, and its nine wheels, and its green glow, and its hum.

He did not step away from it.

"Can I tell you something about locks?" he said.

He had not planned it. He would say that afterwards to Inès, and she would look at him over her glasses and say of course you hadn't, and she would be right. He was frightened, and when he was frightened he talked, and there was a man with a pistol three metres away who seemed, for the moment, prepared to listen, and Dec found that the thing coming out of his mouth was the thing his father used to say to him on a bucket by a door in Salthill.

"My da used to say every lock's a story," he said, "about what somebody's afraid of."

He put his left hand behind him, on the cabinet, and felt for the first wheel with his fingertips. It was cold and fine and perfect, and it was already on R. Inès had got that far.

He left the first wheel where she had put it, and found the second, without looking. "This one's a monk in a plague. Six hundred and seventy-five years ago. He's built a whole religion on everybody owing everybody, and he's put the clock in the tower so the whole valley has to listen to him, and he's terrified. You know what of? Of the bill. Of his own bill. Of what he owes for all of it."

Four, from B. He had gone over it with Inès thirty times at the bench, the offset of every wheel, until he could do it in his sleep, and possibly had, on the sofa.

"So he builds a lock," Dec said, "the best he can, and he puts the one word in it he's most afraid of. The word he wants and doesn't think he'll get. And then he dies, and you lot keep the combination for six hundred years like a holy relic. Nine numbers. The nine numbers of the Reckoning." Third wheel. Fourth. "And you never read it. Did you? Not once. Six hundred years of bankers and bishops and not one of you ever counted round the letters."

Vaulmont had gone very still.

"What are you doing?" he said quietly.

"Opening it," said Dec. "With the word."

Fifth wheel. Sixth.

"There is no word," said Vaulmont. "There are numbers. The Rule..."

"Seventh," said Dec, and turned it. "Eighth." His fingers had stopped shaking. He did not know when. "You had a restorer looking for a word under the varnish of your painting, and a notary drowned for being afraid, and an old man with a day stolen out of him, all to find the word that was scraped off. And it was in your lock the whole time. You had it in your hands." He put his fingertips on the ninth wheel. "It's not pay, Monsieur de Vaulmont."

He turned the ninth wheel to E.

"It's forgive."

The bar inside the cylinder dropped. He felt it go, a small, deep, perfect click, like the last tumbler of a very good safe, and the two brass doors of the cabinet swung open an inch on their hinges, and the hum came out at him, loud now, and the green light.

Inside it was not a radio. Not the way he had imagined a radio: dials, valves, something from a war film. It was very neat and very modern, a rack of black metal boxes with little green lights on them, a thick cable coming in at the top from the dome, a car battery the size of a suitcase on the floor of the cabinet, and on the front of the middle box, in white paint pen, in the same neat slanted hand as the notebook, ALS162. And beside it, at the right height for a hand, a big red rotary switch, like the isolator on a boiler.

Dec looked at it for perhaps a quarter of a second.

Then he put his hand on it and turned it, hard, all the way round, until it clunked.

The hum stopped. The green lights went out, one after another, down the rack. The silence that came out of the cabinet was so sudden and so complete that it seemed to fill the dome.

He turned back round. The pistol was where it had been, and so was Vaulmont. He was looking past Dec, into the open cabinet, at the dark rack and the silent boxes. Then he looked at the nine wheels on the brass door, which still said what they said.

R E M I T T E R E.

His lips moved. Dec saw them. He was reading it. Spelling it, letter by letter, the way a child spells a word it has never seen.

And below them, through the open doors of the Panthéon, the traffic lights at the end of the rue Soufflot stopped flashing red and went, one after another, the whole length of the street, green. On the Lycée Henri-IV the big lit clock face went blank, and lit again, and said two minutes to three. On the wall of the nave, the handsome station clock with FONDATION VAULMONT painted under it in gold gave a small mechanical lurch, and its hands began to sweep round, smoothly, all by themselves, past ten, past eleven, past twelve, round and round, and stopped. Fourteen fifty-eight.

Two minutes short of None.

Down in the nave, a hundred people who had been looking up at the dark dome looked down at the clock on the wall, and then at each other, and a sound went through them, not a gasp this time but a sort of puzzled murmur, the sound of people who have been promised a miracle and have just watched it tidy itself away.

And then, out of the loudspeakers, at the lectern, there came a woman's voice.

She must have come down the conservators' stair inside the pier in the dark, sixty metres of it, at a run, and crossed the nave to the stage while everyone was looking up. Her voice was quick and precise, every consonant in its place, and it was a little out of breath, and it was the voice Dec would have known in the dark at the bottom of a quarry or the top of a tower or at the end of the world, and it was speaking, in very good French, into the microphone of a man who had a pistol in his hand sixty metres above it.

"My name is Inès Varga. I'm the restorer of this painting. I'm going to show you something."

Dec stepped to the balustrade, turned his back on Vaulmont and the pistol, and looked down.

Far below, on the little grey stage, in the beam of the television crew's light, which someone had swung round onto her, a small dark figure in a green dress and a mustard scarf had pulled the grey cloth off the easel. The painting stood there in the white light under the dome. The woman with her saffron sleeve and her clean blue sky. The sundial. And Inès had her phone held up to the camera's lens, close, so close it must have filled every television screen in France, and on the phone's screen was photograph twenty-three. Raking light. The gap in the Latin line. The nine ghost ticks in the ground.

"This line was painted under a clock with nine at the top," said her voice out of the loudspeakers, "by a member of the Brotherhood of the Ninth Hour, in about sixteen fifty. It's their founder's last words. Brother Anselm Vey. Somebody in the Brotherhood scraped one word out of it with a knife. Here." The phone's screen moved. "I've put it back. It says mutuo datum remittere hora nona." A pause, very short. "What was given as a loan, forgive at the ninth hour. That's what their founder wanted, and that's the word they scraped out."

Another pause. Then, in a slightly different voice, harder, flatter: "And the clocks that stopped just now were not a miracle. There's a radio transmitter in the gallery of the dome, in a brass cabinet with the founder's word on its lock, built by the Fondation Vaulmont, under a works permit from the sixteenth of March. Someone has just turned it off." Another pause. "The wine you were all given at half past two was supposed to have datura in it. It didn't. It was swapped. The real wine is in a crate in the south transept. The police can have it."

There was a silence in the Panthéon so complete that Dec, sixty metres up, heard the soft deep rush of the pendulum going past.

Then, somewhere in the nave, a man began to laugh. It was the minister, Dec saw. The tall grey tired-faced man. He was standing in the middle of the nave with an empty pewter cup in his hand, looking at it, and laughing, helplessly, the way you laugh when you find out the floor you have been standing on was never there. Beside him, the prefect of police, in his silver braid, had gone the colour of a church candle.

And the red light on the television camera stayed on.

When Dec turned round, Vaulmont had lowered the pistol.

He was standing where he had stood, three metres along the gallery, but something had gone out of him. Not his calm. His calm was still there, on his face, like a coat on a hook. But behind it, Dec thought, there was nothing holding it up. He was looking at the nine wheels on the open cabinet. He was still reading them.

"Forgive," he said, very quietly. Not to Dec. To the wheels. "Six hundred years."

"Yeah," said Dec. His voice came out hoarse. "Funny old thing."

Vaulmont looked at him then. For a long moment, the pale grey eyes rested on Dec's face, and Dec could not read anything in them at all, not anger and not fear and not grief, just a sort of enormous patience, the patience of a man who had always had time, considering a bill.

Then he turned the pistol round in his gloved hand, and held it out to Dec, butt first.

Dec took it, because he did not know what else to do. It was heavier than it looked. He held it the way he would have held a dead fish.

"I'll go down," said Vaulmont, "by the stair. You'll forgive me if I don't wait for you." And he walked away along the gallery, unhurried, his shoes quiet on the stone, his silver chain catching the last of the torchlight, and turned in at the arch of the south stair, and was gone.

By the time Dec got down, at three minutes past three, the light was coming back.

It came back the way it had gone, but faster, a long silver dawn through the high windows that turned the blue of the dome grey and then white and then gold, so that the painted saints came out of the dark one by one as if somebody were switching them on. Outside the great doors, the crowd in the square was cheering, the ordinary, delighted, foolish cheer of people who have watched the sun come back and are a little embarrassed by how relieved they are.

In the nave, everyone was standing in a ring.

At the centre of it, by the brass ring on the floor, under the steel cable going up and up into the brightening dome, Commissaire Albert Pichon was standing in his rumpled beige raincoat, a little out of breath, with four young officers behind him and a white linen napkin still tucked into his collar. He had evidently come straight from lunch. There was a small brown stain on the napkin, about where a man's tie would be.

In front of him, quite still, his hands held out in front of him with the wrists together, stood Auguste de Vaulmont.

Nobody had made him do it, Dec could see. He had simply walked down the stair and through the crowd to the policeman and held out his hands, the way a man holds out his ticket at a barrier.

Pichon looked at the wrists for a moment. Then he looked at the face above them. Then, slowly, almost reluctantly, as though it were a disagreeable task after a very good meal, he took a pair of handcuffs out of his raincoat pocket.

"Auguste de Vaulmont," he said, quite quietly, so that only the ring of people round him heard it, and the camera, whose red eye was still on. "I'm arresting you for the murder of Gérard Lassalle, and for the poisoning of Anselme Roux, Judge Jacques Delorme, Madame Marthe Aubry, the Abbé Luc Girardin of Saint-Médard and Jean-Paul Morel, all of whom, I'm happy to say, are alive and very angry. And for a great many other things which I shall think of over my dessert." The cuffs clicked shut. "I'm told you like to keep accounts. So do I."

The pendulum went past them, a hand's breadth above the floor, with its soft deep sigh, and away, and came back. It had not stopped. It had not, Dec realised, so much as hesitated, the whole time. It had gone on swinging through the eclipse and the dark and the clocks and the miracle, keeping its own plane, while the floor and the building and the city and all of them turned underneath it.

At the back of the ring, near the foot of the south stair, there was a movement. People stepped aside. Brother Kessler came out of the stair arch, stooping, huge and bald in his black coat, with his hands empty. He walked through the crowd, which parted for him like water round a stone, and stopped in front of the nearest of Pichon's young officers, who went white, and held out his own great wrists, without a word.

Somewhere in the south transept, a young voice was shouting in French, with great enjoyment, He's trying to take the crate! The thin one! He's trying to... and then a crash, and a yelp, and then the same voice, much more pleased: Monsieur l'agent, I'm sitting on him. Do you want him? I'm sitting on him.

Dec stood at the edge of the ring with the pistol still hanging from his hand. One of Pichon's young officers came round the ring to him, a boy with a fresh haircut and a careful face, said Monsieur, and took it out of his fingers, gently, the way you take a pair of scissors from a child who has forgotten he is holding them. Dec let it go. His hand stayed curled round the shape of it for a moment after.

He looked across the nave, past Pichon and the cuffs and the pendulum and the cameras, at the little grey stage.

Inès was standing beside the painting with her phone still in her hand. Her glasses were crooked. Her scarf had come unwound and was hanging down one side to her knee. Her face, in the returning gold light from the windows, was perfectly still, the stillness she had when she was most frightened and least willing to show it, and she was looking straight back at him.

He lifted the hand that was not holding the pistol, a little, uncertainly.

After a moment, very slightly, so that nobody else in the whole Panthéon would have seen it, she lifted hers.
