Roux Remembers Nothing, Loudly

Anselme Roux had reopened his shop by four o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, against the advice of two doctors, one neighbour and the woman in the fur hat, and he had wound every clock in it himself, and now the place was ticking again like light rain, and he was furious.

"A day," he said. "A whole day. Do you know how many days I have left, at eighty-one? I have counted. I have a notebook. I was going to spend that day on a Bréguet repeater that has been waiting for me since nineteen ninety-four. Now it is gone. Somebody has stolen a day from an old man, and the police send me a boy with a pimple to ask if I have been drinking."

"Had you?" said Dec.

Roux looked at him from under the great white awnings of his eyebrows for a long moment.

"You," he said, "are the thief who brought me back my water."

"That's me."

"And who is this?"

"Inès Varga," said Inès. "My mother is Hélène Varga. You told her in the rue des Archives in two thousand and six that her wristwatch was an insult to the wrist."

Roux considered this. "It was," he said. "Sit down. Both of you. Mind the cuckoo, he bites."

They sat on two wooden stools in front of the counter, under a wall of clocks all going about their business. It was strange, Dec thought, how much louder they seemed now than they had yesterday, now that he had heard them silent. The cat clocks were swinging their eyes left and right. The longcase stood beside the counter with its door shut and its pendulum going, and someone had wiped the chalk off the inside, and he found he did not like that either.

Inès had put the silver watch on the counter between them. Roux had not touched it. He had looked at it once, when she took it out of her pocket, the way a man looks at a dog he once got bitten by, and then not again.

"Monsieur Roux," Dec began.

"Anselme. You have seen me without my teeth. In the ambulance. Don't pretend."

"Anselme. You don't remember Sunday. That's grand, we know that. But do you remember anything before it? The week before? Anyone strange come in? Anyone ask you for anything you wouldn't do?"

"Everybody asks me for things I won't do. That is what a clockmaker is. A man who says no." He sniffed. "You asked me for a mainspring for forty euros."

"I did. I'm sorry. Anyone else?"

Roux was quiet for a while. His small hands, spotted and very clean, lay on the counter beside the watch, and the fingers moved now and then, as though they were remembering on their own.

"Two jobs," he said finally. "This winter. Both for the same gentleman. Or the same people; the gentleman came once, and after that only a young man in a grey coat with a parcel."

"What gentleman?"

"A gentleman." Roux shrugged. "Grey suit. Very good. A voice like warm oil. Silver hair, brushed back. He bought nothing in the shop, but he looked at everything, and he knew what he was looking at, which is rare. He had one of those." He tipped his head at the watch on the counter without looking at it. "On a chain. I noticed it because I always notice watches, and because I could not see the hands from where I stood, and I thought, that is a very good watch to have no hands."

Dec and Inès did not look at each other. It took some effort.

"The first job," Roux went on, "was a lock. A copy of an old lock. A letter lock, very old, fourteenth century, from drawings. Nine wheels. You turn each wheel to a letter, nine letters, and if they spell the word, the shackle drops. They made them in the old days for strongboxes, for the reliquaries in monasteries. Beautiful things. Terrible to make. Every wheel has a little gate cut in the inside rim, and when the nine gates line up, the bar falls through." He moved his fingers in the air, turning nine invisible wheels. "I made it in brass, the size of a brick. It took me six weeks. He paid in cash and the young man in the grey coat took it away in a box lined with felt."

"What was the word?" said Dec.

"I don't know."

"You made it."

"I made the wheels. The gates I cut where the drawings told me, and the drawings had no letters on the gates, only numbers, and the young man brought the last wheel's position in a sealed envelope and stood over me while I cut it." The old man's mouth went thin. "I was insulted. I said so. He said it was a matter of faith." He snorted. "Faith. In my shop."

"And the second job?"

Roux's hands stopped moving on the counter.

"The second job I didn't do."

"What was it?"

"They wanted me to take radio clocks apart. Not good clocks. Cheap ones. Station clocks, the kind on poles in the street, the receivers inside traffic signals, the big wall clocks in hospitals. You know how they work?"

"Not really," Dec admitted. "I'm more doors."

"They listen," said Roux. He held up one finger, as if to a class. "Every one of those clocks has a little radio inside it, and the radio listens to Allouis. You know Allouis? It's a transmitter in the middle of France, near Bourges. A huge thing. It sends out the time all day and all night, the real time, from the atomic clocks in Paris, on the long wave, and every radio clock in France listens to it and does what it's told. The stations. The hospitals. The traffic lights. The clock in your mother's kitchen, if she bought it in a supermarket. All of them listening to the one voice, like monks listening to the abbot."

"And they wanted you to..."

"They wanted me to change the receivers. So that they would listen to a different voice." Roux's eyebrows came down until his eyes were almost gone under them. "I asked what voice. The young man said it was a test. For the Fondation. For an exhibition. I said a clock listens to Allouis as a priest listens to God, and I would not teach them to listen to anything else, not for an exhibition, not for money, not for the Pope. And I put him out of the shop." He looked at the wall of clocks, all ticking. "That was a Thursday. Ten days ago."

Nobody said anything. The cuckoo clock over the workshop door gave a sudden mechanical whirr, and the little bird shot out of its house and said cuckoo four times, quite loudly, and went back in.

Dec, who had jumped, said, "Then on Sunday, someone takes a day off you. And stops every clock you've got."

"Yes."

"Because you said no."

Roux said nothing. He did not need to.

The drawings were in a cardboard tube under the workbench, behind a box of pendulum bobs. Roux pulled them out himself and spread them on the counter, holding the corners down with a carriage clock, a jar of screws, his loupe and, after a moment's thought, the handless watch.

There were five sheets. Copies, on modern paper, of something very old: the lines were faint and brown in places, as if the original had been drawn in iron-gall ink on parchment and photocopied badly. A brass cylinder, drawn from the side and from the end. Nine wheels on a spindle, each wheel with a ring of tiny letters round its rim, A to Z with a few odd ones missing and two Dec did not recognise. The inside of each wheel in section, with a little notch cut in the rim at a different place on each one. Measurements in an old hand, in a unit Dec did not know. And in the bottom corner of each sheet, very small, a maker's mark: a circle with twelve marks round it and IX at the top, and beside it, in Latin, Fr. Anselmus Vey me fecit, MCCCXLIX.

"Brother Anselm Vey made me," Inès read softly. "Thirteen forty-nine."

"Your Latin is better than mine," said Roux. "That's the plague year. Everybody was dying and this one made locks." He tapped the inside of one of the wheels with a fingernail. "Look. The gates. There's the position for each one, by number, from the top. But no letters on them. The word was never on the drawings. The man in the grey coat said so himself, when I complained. Vey kept it in his book." He shrugged. "Whatever book that is."

Dec bent over the drawings and went very quiet.

It was a beautiful thing. He could see that at once, the way you could see that a painting was good before you knew why. The wheels were thin, the gates were tiny, the bar that dropped through them was cut so close that any wheel a hair out of place would hold it. Somebody six hundred and seventy-odd years ago had sat in a cold room while half of Europe died and made this, slowly, for something that mattered to him more than anything.

He looked at the silver watch holding down the corner of the sheet, and its empty case, and the nine tiny engraved rings inside the lid.

"It's the same lock," he said. "On the watch. It's a picture of this lock."

"Of course it is," said Roux. "It is their sign. Like the scallop on a pilgrim's hat. Every one of them carries the lock, and none of them carries the word." He looked at Dec with something that was almost approval. "You see, thief? You are not so stupid."

"Can I have these? The drawings?"

"No."

"Can I photograph them?"

Roux thought about this for long enough that the cat clocks' eyes went left and right four times.

"Take them," he said. "Take the whole tube. I don't want them in my shop any more. Every time I open the tube I can taste cloves." He made a face. "Like licking a church roof."

Inès had not said much. Dec had noticed, over two days, that she did not say much when she was thinking, and that she thought most of the time, and that when she finally said something it was usually the thing.

She said it when Roux turned to put the tube in a carrier bag.

"Monsieur Roux. Anselme. Show me your tongue."

He looked at her. Then, with enormous dignity, the way a man of eighty-one sticks his tongue out for a doctor, he did.

It was still grey. Fainter than yesterday, but grey: a dull smudge across the middle, like the shadow of a thumbprint on a window.

Inès looked at it for a long time. Then she nodded, and Roux put it away, and she turned to Dec.

"I've seen that," she said in English, quietly. "Not on a tongue. In a picture. Three years ago I restored a shop sign for a museum in Lyon. An apothecary's sign, sixteen hundreds, painted on wood. It had pictures of all the medicines the shop sold, with their names and what they did, in rhymes. Little panels, like a comic. One of the panels was a poison. A man in bed with his eyes open and his tongue out, grey, and a jug of spiced wine by the bed, and cloves scattered round it, painted very carefully. And a plant in the corner with white flowers like trumpets." She pushed her glasses up. "The rhyme said something like, give this to a man and he will owe you a day."

"Owe you a day," said Dec.

"I thought it was a joke. Old apothecaries made jokes. I cleaned it and I didn't think about it again."

"The trumpets," said Roux unexpectedly. "White trumpets. That's datura. Thornapple. My grandmother grew it in Burgundy to keep the rats down and told me if I ever put the seeds in my mouth I'd see the Virgin and forget my own name." He looked from one of them to the other and his face changed. The anger went out of it, and what was left behind was an old man in a work coat in a small shop, who had sat in it for a whole day without knowing. "Somebody gave me that? In my coffee?"

"I think so," said Inès gently. "I'm sorry."

"And stopped my clocks while I sat there. And drew in my longcase." His voice cracked. "My father's clock."

Dec found he had put his hand on the old man's shoulder. It felt like a bird's.

"We'll find out who," he said. "I promise you."

He did not know why he had said it. It was a stupid thing to say. He had no idea how. He was a locksmith with a tube of drawings and a watch with no hands and a client who knew about him before the police did. But Roux looked up at him from under the eyebrows, and for the first time since the shop had gone silent, the old man's mouth twitched at one corner.

"Thief," he said. "You promise like a thief."

Outside, the light was going. The rue des Gravilliers was full of people coming home, and somewhere a scooter was being revved by somebody who loved it more than his neighbours.

They stood on the pavement with the carrier bag, not being partners.

"He said no," Dec said. "The man with the warm-oil voice asked him to make the clocks of Paris listen to a different voice, and he said no, and they stopped him. And Lassalle sent me the gear, and sent you the painting, and now Lassalle's in the river. And they're having a party at the Panthéon on the first of April at three o'clock, in the middle of an eclipse, and they want my room finished for it, and nothing can move."

"Yes."

"I don't know what any of that means."

"No," said Inès. "Neither do I."

She took the scrap of notebook paper out of her coat pocket and unfolded it under the street lamp. Barrière d'Enfer. La neuvième niche.

"But I know where it is," she said.

"Under the ground," said Dec. "With the bones."

"With six million of them, more or less."

"Grand," said Dec. "Brilliant. I love that. Have you ever been down there?"

"Once. With school. In the part for tourists. There are turnstiles and a gift shop." She folded the paper up again. "The ninth niche won't be in the part with the gift shop."

"So how do we get to the part without the gift shop?"

Inès took out her phone and scrolled, and found a number, and looked at it for a moment as though it were a wasp that had landed on her hand.

"My mother's hairdresser," she said, "has a cousin who goes down there at night. Illegally. She is a philosophy student. They call her La Taupe."

"The Mole."

"Yes."

"Is she good?"

"I've never met her," said Inès, and pressed call. "My mother's hairdresser says she is very good, and also completely insane."
