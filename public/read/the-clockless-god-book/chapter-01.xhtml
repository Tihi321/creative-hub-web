Two Hundred Clocks at Nine

The bell over the door of Roux & Fils, Horlogerie, did not ring. Dec Hartley noticed that first, because he was the kind of man who noticed bells, hinges, latches, the small honest machinery of doors. The bell was there, a fat brass thing on a coiled spring, and the door hit its clapper on the way in, and the clapper did nothing. Someone had wedged it with a twist of paper.

The second thing he noticed was the silence.

He had been in the shop twice before, the first time to introduce himself and the second to haggle, and both times the place had sounded like the inside of a beehive that had learned arithmetic. Two hundred clocks, at least, on every wall and shelf and windowsill: cuckoos and carriage clocks, regulators in walnut cases, a ship's clock on a gimbal, a row of kitchen clocks shaped like cats with eyes that went left and right. All of them ticking, none of them together, so that standing among them felt like standing in light rain.

Now there was nothing. Not one tick. The cats' eyes were fixed, every one of them looking left.

"Monsieur Roux?" Dec said. "C'est moi. Le Irlandais. For the gears."

His French was a thing he had built himself out of a school textbook, two summers picking grapes near Bordeaux and a confidence nobody had ever earned. He was aware of this in the way you are aware that you are about to fall off a ladder.

Anselme Roux was sitting behind his counter, upright on a stool, in his brown work coat with his loupe hanging on its chain. He was eighty-one years old and about the size of a ten-year-old, bald as a doorknob, with white eyebrows so big and so far out over his eyes that he looked permanently sheltered from weather. His hands were flat on the counter. He was staring at the wall.

"Monsieur Roux?"

"It's Sunday," said Roux.

"It's Monday," Dec said. "Morning. Half nine, near enough. I said Monday, didn't I? For the escapement wheels and the, the, the brass bits. The list." He patted his jacket for the list and found his phone, his keys, a receipt for a croque-monsieur, and the roll of lock picks he kept in the inside pocket because it was the one place he never lost them. "I've a list somewhere. They sent me a list."

Roux turned his head, very slowly, as though it were a heavy thing on a stiff hinge. "Who are you?"

"Dec. Declan Hartley. I was here Thursday. You called me a thief because I offered you forty euro for a mainspring."

"You are a thief," said Roux, with some relief, as if he had found at least one thing in the room where he had left it. Then the relief went. "It is Sunday. I closed the shop on Saturday night and I went to bed, and now it is Sunday, and I have come down to wind them, and they are all..." He lifted one hand and let it fall. "They are all at nine."

Dec looked.

He had thought, coming in, that the clocks had simply run down. They had not. Every face he could see said nine o'clock. The regulators said nine. The carriage clocks said nine. The cat clocks had their little black hands pointing straight up and straight left, nine exactly, and so did the ship's clock and the long walnut longcase clock beside the counter and the cheap plastic wall clock over the workshop door, the one Roux used for himself because, he had told Dec on Thursday, he could not bear to look at his good clocks while he was eating.

Two hundred clocks, maybe more, all stopped at nine. Not drifted. Not run down at random. Stopped.

A cold feeling went up Dec's back, the one he always got in the last room of an escape game when the players realised the clock on the wall was not decoration.

"Right," he said. "Right. Okay. That's, that's a bit much. That's a lot of nine."

"Is it Sunday?" Roux asked again, and this time it was not an announcement. It was a question, from a very old man, and his voice cracked on it.

Dec came round the counter. Close up, Roux smelled of machine oil and pipe tobacco, as he always had, and also of something else, something sweet and dusty. His eyes were wet and pink at the rims.

"It's Monday the twenty-third," Dec said gently. "Of March. Have you eaten anything, Monsieur Roux? Have you had a drink of water?"

"I had my coffee." Roux's mouth worked. He stuck out his tongue a little, the way a child does at a bad medicine, and pulled it back. "Everything tastes of cloves. Cloves and slate. Like licking a church roof."

His tongue, in the moment it was out, had been the wrong colour. Not pink. Grey, a dull smeared grey, as though he had been sucking a pencil.

Dec saw it and did not know what to do with it, so he put it away in the back of his head where he kept odd things, the way his father had taught him to keep the screws from a lock in the order they came out. Grey tongue. Cloves. Slate. Then he did the sensible thing, which was to fill a glass from the tap in the workshop and bring it back and wait while the old man drank.

"Who has been in here?" Roux said, between mouthfuls. "Somebody has been in here. They have been touching my clocks."

"Did you hear anything? Last night, or..." He did not want to say the night before. "Did anyone come to the door?"

"Nobody comes to the door. I lock the door." Roux pointed with the glass, spilling. "I lock the front, I lock the back, I go upstairs, I sleep, I come down. I always come down. And now it is Sunday."

"Monday."

"Don't correct me, thief."

That, Dec thought, was more like it.

He left Roux with the water and went to look at the clocks, because he could not help himself and because it was better than standing there being frightened. The longcase clock was nearest. It was a big, plain, honest thing, a provincial clock in oak, the sort you would find in a farmhouse hallway with a hat hung on it. Its pendulum hung dead behind the little glass window in the trunk. The hands said nine.

He opened the trunk door. It was not locked; old longcase clocks almost never were, the little brass keys having been lost a hundred years ago down the backs of sofas. The pendulum bob was steady, and there was something wrong with the way it hung, so he leaned in and looked up into the dark of the case with his phone torch.

A sliver of brass, no bigger than a matchstick, had been pushed between the crutch and the pendulum rod. It held the pendulum off to one side, just enough. Someone had wound the clock, set its hands to nine, and then jammed it so it could not go.

And on the inside of the trunk, on the bare oak, in white chalk, was a drawing. A circle, like a clock face. Twelve little marks around the rim. And at the top, where twelve should have been, the numeral IX.

Dec looked at it for a long time.

It was not a big drawing, and it was not a frightening drawing, taken by itself. It was neat. Careful. Whoever had done it had done it with the patience of a person who had all the time in the world, and that was what made the back of his neck go tight.

"Monsieur Roux," he called, "did you draw something in your clock?"

"I don't draw. I mend."

"Grand. Right. Just checking." He did not touch the chalk. He took a picture of it with his phone, because something in him that had watched a lot of television said don't touch the chalk, and he closed the door of the trunk very gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping baby or a bomb.

The workshop at the back was narrower than the shop, a long bench under a strip light, every inch of it covered in tiny trays of tiny parts, springs and screws and wheels laid out like jewels in a box. At the far end was the back door, which opened onto the courtyard where the bins lived. It was shut.

Dec tried it. It was locked.

He looked at it with the interest of a professional, and then with a slightly different interest.

It was the wrong lock. The rest of the shop was old: the front door had a big iron mortice lock older than the Eiffel Tower, a lovely thing with a key the size of a soup spoon. This one was new. A cheap brass-plated cylinder, the kind you bought in a blister pack in any hardware shop in Europe, fitted into the old door with fresh screws whose heads still had the bright scratches of a screwdriver on them. And around the keyway, on the face of the cylinder, there were marks. Fine bright lines, all of them leading into the keyhole, like the tracks of tiny skis.

"Ah, now," Dec said aloud. "Ah, now, that's not right at all."

"What?" Roux shouted from the counter. He was a little deaf, and shouted most things. "What's not right?"

"Your back door." Dec raised his voice. "When did you put a new lock on your back door?"

"I didn't. That door has the old lock. My father's lock."

"Well, it hasn't now, it has a thing out of a blister pack, and somebody's been at it." He crouched. "See these scratches? That's a pick. Somebody picked this. Badly, mind you. Look at the state of that, he's been dragging a rake in and out of it like he was raking leaves. That's not a professional. A professional leaves you nothing. You'd not know I'd been here." He was aware he was explaining a lock to a man who had just lost a day of his life, and he could not stop. It was what his mouth did when he was frightened. "But here's the thing. Here's the thing, Monsieur Roux. It's locked. From the inside. It's a cylinder with a thumb-turn on this side, so you lock it from in here by turning the knob, and from outside you need the key. So whoever was in here, and somebody was in here, they've gone out of it and left it locked behind them from the inside. Which you can't do. Not with a thumb-turn. Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless they never went out that way at all," Dec said slowly, "and only want it to look like somebody came in that way. Somebody picked it from the outside to leave the scratches. Then came in the front with a key, or you let them in, and locked this behind them so it'd look like a break-in that... no, that doesn't work either, because then why lock it..."

He took out the roll of picks, because he thought better with his hands, and unrolled it on his knee. Twelve picks and two tension wrenches in a canvas roll his mother had sewn for him the year he finished his apprenticeship, each in its own pocket, like cutlery. He called them the cutlery, which his ex-girlfriend had found charming for about a month.

"If I pick it from this side," he said, choosing a hook, "and look at the pins, I'll see if they're scored. Then we'll know whether..."

He had the tension wrench in the keyway and the hook going in after it, and he was kneeling on Roux's workshop floor with his tongue between his teeth, when the bell over the front door, which someone had evidently unjammed, rang loudly and clearly, and a voice said, in a French so slow and so tired that even Dec could follow every word:

"Do not move, monsieur. And please do not finish whatever it is you are doing to that door."

Dec looked round.

There were three of them. Two were uniformed police officers, young, alert, with their hands resting on their belts in a way that made Dec very aware of his own hands. The third was a round man in a rumpled beige raincoat, late fifties, with a grey moustache and a pair of reading glasses hanging on a cord on his chest. He had a white linen napkin tucked into his collar. There was a small brown stain on the napkin, about where a man's tie would be, that looked like gravy.

"Commissaire Pichon," said the round man, as if apologising for it. "Police judiciaire. And you are?"

"Dec," said Dec. "Declan Hartley. I'm, look, this isn't what it looks like."

Commissaire Pichon looked at it. He looked at Dec on his knees at the back door of an old man's shop. He looked at the hook in the keyhole and the tension wrench beside it and the open canvas roll on Dec's knee with ten more picks in it, laid out like surgical instruments. He looked at the old man at the counter, who was waving his empty water glass and shouting, "That is the thief! He wanted my mainspring for forty euros!"

"Monsieur Hartley," Pichon said. He said the name the way a doctor says the name of a rash. "At eleven minutes past nine, a neighbour of Monsieur Roux telephoned to say that his shop had been broken into. At nine twenty-two my colleagues arrive. And at nine thirty-one, I find you here, inside, at the back door, with burglary tools. I was at breakfast. I would like to go back to breakfast. Please make it easy for me."

"I'm a locksmith," Dec said.

"Of course you are."

"No, I am. Genuinely. I build escape rooms. You know escape rooms? You lock people in a room and they have to find the clues to get out, and they pay you for it, it's very popular, it's for team building." He was babbling. He could hear himself babbling. "I'm building one now. For a foundation. The Fondation Vaulmont, they're very fancy, they're doing a whole medieval clockmaker's cell, that's why I'm here, I was buying gears. He'll tell you. Monsieur Roux! Tell him I was buying gears!"

Roux considered this.

"He is a thief," he said finally. "But I don't think he stopped my clocks. He can't even get the price of a mainspring right."

"Thank you," said Dec.

"Stand up, please," said Pichon. "Slowly. Leave the little knives on the floor."

"They're picks, they're not knives, they wouldn't cut butter, look..."

"Monsieur Hartley."

Dec stood up, slowly, leaving the cutlery on the floor. One of the young officers came and took his arm, not roughly, and turned him towards the wall.

"Can I just say one thing," Dec said to the wall. "Before you, you know. One thing. The back door's locked from the inside. With a thumb-turn. Someone's picked it from the outside, badly, to make it look like a break-in, but the door was locked behind them from in here, so they never went out that way. And there's a drawing in his clock. In chalk. A clock with nine at the top. And the clocks have all been stopped on purpose, I've seen one, there's a bit of brass jammed in the pendulum. And his tongue's gone grey. You should look at his tongue."

There was a pause behind him.

"His tongue," Pichon said.

"It's grey. And he says everything tastes of cloves."

Another pause, longer. When Pichon spoke again his voice was different, not friendlier exactly, but less tired, as though some part of him had sat up.

"A clock," he said, "with nine at the top."

"In the longcase. Inside the door."

Dec heard the commissaire walk across the shop. He heard the little door of the longcase clock open, and then nothing at all for what felt like a long time, only the strange absent silence of two hundred stopped clocks, and somewhere out in the street a delivery van reversing with its beeper going.

Then the door closed again, softly.

"Monsieur Hartley," said Pichon, close behind him now, and very quietly, "you are under arrest."

"For what? I've just told you..."

"Exactly." The commissaire took the napkin out of his collar at last, folded it neatly into four, and put it in his raincoat pocket. "You have just told me a great deal more than you should know. Come. We will talk about it over lunch."
