The Chapel Under the Barrière d'Enfer

La Taupe was about twenty-four, small and wiry, with a shaved head under a woollen hat and a voice that went at the speed of a moped. She met them at eleven on Wednesday night by a gap in a fence above an old railway cutting in the fourteenth arrondissement, wearing chest waders, a headlamp and a backpack held together with gaffer tape, and the first thing she said, looking Dec up and down, was, "He's too tall."

"I'm what?"

"For the galleries. Some places it's one metre forty. You'll crawl. You'll hit your head. You'll complain. The tall ones always complain." She turned to Inès. "Your mother's hairdresser says you're afraid of everything."

"Not everything," said Inès stiffly. "Heights. Dogs."

"Underground there's no heights and no dogs. Only water and the dead. You're fine with the dead?"

"I restore paintings from the sixteen hundreds. Everyone in them is dead."

La Taupe considered this and seemed to find it acceptable. "Bon. Waders." She pulled two pairs out of the backpack, folded flat, smelling powerfully of old rubber. "Put them on over your trousers. Headlamps. A spare torch each. Don't drop it. If you lose me, stand still and shout. If you lose me and you don't stand still, I'll find you in about three weeks, and you won't be interesting any more."

Dec looked at Inès over the top of his waders as he hopped into them.

"Your mother's hairdresser," he said, "has a very complete family."

They went down through the railway cutting, under a dripping brick bridge, and into the mouth of a tunnel that had not seen a train in eighty years. A hundred metres along, in the dark, La Taupe stopped by a low hole at the foot of the wall, half hidden by a fall of rubble, and without a word lay down on her back and slid into it feet first, like a letter into a postbox.

Inès went next, because if she thought about it she would not.

It was not as bad as she had feared. It was worse in some ways and better in others. The hole led into a narrow passage cut through the living rock, pale yellow limestone, so low that she had to walk bent double, and then so low that she had to crawl, and then, quite suddenly, it opened out and she could stand, and her headlamp swung across a gallery as tall as a church nave, the walls smooth and pale and sweating, and the floor under her waders a sheet of black water a few centimetres deep that broke into rings round her feet.

On the wall at the corner, at head height, a stone plaque had been carved into the rock. Rue Saint-Jacques. And under it a date: 1781.

"The street above us," said La Taupe. "When they dug out the stone to build Paris, they left the holes. Then the holes started eating the streets. So the king sent engineers down to prop it up, and they named every gallery for the street on top of it, so they'd know where they were. You're under the rue Saint-Jacques. Up there, someone's eating a kebab."

Behind them Dec came out of the crawl on his hands and knees and stood up, and hit his head on the ceiling of the gallery, which was, at that point, exactly as tall as he was not.

"Ow," he said. Then, "No, it's fine. It's grand. I'm not complaining."

"He's complaining," said La Taupe to Inès.

"He's Irish," said Inès. "It's how they say thank you."

They walked for an hour. Inès lost all sense of direction within ten minutes and stopped trying to keep it. La Taupe walked fast and talked faster: about Spinoza, who she thought had been badly treated by everyone including Spinoza; about drainage, which she thought was the most important and least respected art in Paris; about the police unit that patrolled the quarries and whose officers she knew by their boots. She would stop at a junction, look at a plaque, look at the ceiling, say something about Spinoza, and turn left. Twice they waded through water up to their knees. Once they climbed a rusted iron ladder in a shaft and went along a gallery so narrow that Dec had to turn sideways and hold his breath, and he did not complain once, and Inès, behind him, found herself almost sorry.

Then the bones began.

The first was a single skull, sitting on a ledge at a corner, someone's joke, with a candle stub on its head. Then, in a side gallery, a wall of them: the long bones stacked like firewood, ends outward, brown and smooth, and set into the stack in a row at chest height, a line of skulls, all facing the same way.

"The overflow," said La Taupe, more quietly. "When they emptied the old cemeteries, there were too many. The tourist part's full. So they put the rest anywhere there was room." She turned her headlamp along the wall. "This is the Barrière d'Enfer section. The old tollgate's right above us."

Inès found she had stopped walking.

She had looked at a great many dead people in paint. She had cleaned the faces of Christs and martyrs and the drowned, and once a whole field of plague dead in a Flemish panel, every one of them a centimetre high. She had not expected this to be different, and it was. It was the quiet, and the way the skulls all looked the same way, patient, as if they were waiting for a bus.

"Niches," said Dec beside her, softly. "Look."

Along the gallery, set into the walls of bone at intervals, were recesses: arched hollows a metre high where the stack of bones had been built round a gap, like little chapels, each with a stone sill. Some held a cross scratched in the rock. One held a jam jar with a dead flower in it.

"The ninth niche," said Inès.

They counted. La Taupe counted with them, out loud, in French, as if counting sheep. At neuf she stopped.

The ninth niche was empty. No cross, no jar. Just a hollow in the wall of bones, with a smooth stone sill and smooth stone sides and a back wall of yellow limestone, sweating in the lamplight.

Dec put his hand flat on the back wall. He moved it, slowly, the way Inès moved her fingertips over a canvas to feel where the paint changed. Then he stopped, and pushed, and something in the rock went clunk, deep and heavy, like a big key turning in a big door.

The back of the niche swung inwards.

"Oh," said La Taupe, "putain," and for the first time that night she sounded about twelve years old.

It was a chapel.

Cut from the rock, round, about the size of Inès's atelier, with a domed ceiling that her headlamp could just reach. The floor was dry, swept and smooth. Round the walls, at regular intervals, there were nine tall niches, carved into the stone and empty, each with a little stone step in front of it, like a place for a person to stand. Opposite the door was an altar, a single block of limestone, and over it, carved into the rock in deep relief and painted a dull silver that caught the lamplight and threw it back, was a clock face a metre across. Twelve marks round the rim. No hands. And at the top, where twelve should be, a great carved IX.

On the altar, on a wooden stand, lay a book.

Inès went to it before she could think, the way she went to any book that old. It was a ledger, bound in leather the colour of dried blood, as long as her forearm. She kept her fingers off the pages and turned them with the end of her pencil, as she would have in an archive, and her headlamp moved over them in a small hard circle of light.

The first half was old. Columns of names in brown ink, in hands that changed every few pages, back and back into the seventeen hundreds and earlier. The headings at the top of each page were in Latin and then, later, in French. Horae solutae. Heures payées. Hours paid.

The second half was new. Black ink. A single neat hand. And a different heading.

Débiteurs. Debtors.

Delorme, Jacques, magistrat honoraire. Octobre. Une heure.

Aubry, Marthe, libraire. Janvier. Une heure.

Girardin, Abbé Luc, Saint-Médard. Février. Une heure.

Roux, Anselme, horloger. Mars. Une heure.

And under it, the ink of the last entry so new it still seemed wet:

Lassalle, Gérard, notaire. Mars.

There was no une heure after Lassalle's name. Instead there was a single word, written very small. Remis.

"Delivered," Inès whispered. "Or returned. Or..." Her voice did something she did not authorise. "Paid off."

Dec was reading over her shoulder. She heard him swallow. He put his hand on the edge of the altar, and she saw his knuckles go white.

At the bottom of the page, separate from the names, there was a note in the same neat modern hand, copying an older line. Selon la règle de l'Horloge de la Dette. Frère Anselme Vey. Prieuré de Combe-Noire, Jura.

"By the rule of the Debt Clock," Inès read. "Brother Anselm Vey. Combe-Noire Priory. The Jura." She looked up at Dec. "Vey. The lock on the drawings. Vey kept it in his book."

"Inès," said La Taupe from the door, in a very different voice. "Turn off your lamps. Now."

She heard it then. Far off in the galleries, getting nearer: footsteps in water. Many footsteps, slow and even. And voices, low, in a murmur that rose and fell, and it took her a moment to understand that they were not talking. They were chanting.

There was nowhere to hide but the niches. La Taupe pushed the stone door almost shut, leaving a gap the width of a finger, and the three of them pressed into the deepest of the nine niches behind the altar, in the dark, so close that Inès could feel Dec's heart going through the back of his leather jacket.

The door swung open. Light came in.

They came in pairs. Grey robes with deep hoods, and under the hoods nothing she could see but shadow and the occasional pale chin. They carried candles. There were eighteen of them, she counted, two by two. Eight pairs stopped in front of eight of the niches, each pair on its little stone step. The ninth pair went on to the altar. The niche nearest Inès, the one with three idiots in waders pressed into the back of it, they left empty.

The chanting stopped.

Somebody at the altar turned the pages of the book. She heard them turn. She held her breath until her chest burned.

"Lassalle," said a voice, "has been remitted."

A quiet voice, a man's, cultivated and very calm: the voice of someone who had never had to shout, because people had always leaned closer to hear it. It made Inès think, absurdly, of warm oil, poured slowly.

A voice like warm oil, Roux had said. Silver hair.

"He was afraid," the voice went on, gently. "Fear is a debt like any other. He has paid it. Pray for him." A pause, while the grey hoods bowed. "Brothers. Our restorer is close to the word. Our clocks are ready. In seven days the moon will cover the sun, and at the hour of None, the voice from Allouis will speak for us, and the city will hear its debt called in. And the Seventh will collect." Another pause. "Mutuo datum. Reddetur."

"Reddetur," said eighteen voices, low.

Given as a loan, thought Inès. It shall be repaid.

Our restorer. Our restorer is close to the word.

She felt Dec's hand find hers in the dark, and grip it, and she gripped back, hard enough to hurt both of them.

The candles went out of the chapel the way they had come in, pair by pair, and the chanting went with them, down the galleries, fainter and fainter, until it was gone. The last pair stopped in the doorway. One of them was a great deal larger than the other. Inès saw him, briefly, in the candlelight as he turned back to pull the stone door shut: the same bald head she had seen on the roof of the Île Saint-Louis, the shoulders that filled the arch, the little blue cuckoo clock on his neck.

Then the door closed, and there was a heavy, final, iron sound from the other side, like a bolt going home.

"We're locked in," said La Taupe, in the dark. "We're locked in a cult's bedroom under the fourteenth arrondissement and nobody knows we're here."

"Ah, now," said Dec. "Locked's my department."

He turned on his headlamp.

The stone door would not move. Dec put his shoulder to it and his whole long body and it did not move by so much as a whisper; there was a bar across it on the other side. He came back to the middle of the chapel, breathing hard, and swung his lamp round the walls, the empty niches, the clock with no hands. And stopped.

Behind the altar, low in the wall, half hidden by the block of stone, there was a grille. Old wrought iron, red with rust, set into the rock over a black opening the size of a dog kennel. Through the bars came a cold breath of air and the sound of running water.

The grille had a lock on it. A big square box lock, eighteenth century, rusted almost solid.

Dec knelt in front of it and unrolled his canvas roll on the floor of the chapel of the Brotherhood of the Ninth Hour as if he were laying a table for Sunday dinner. His hands, Inès noticed, were shaking. She noticed too that by the time he had the second pick in, they were not.

"Old lever lock," he said, to nobody. "Two levers. Rust everywhere. She's not been opened since Napoleon was a boy. She's going to be stiff. She's going to be a right..." The lock made a sound like a rusty gate being kicked. "...there she is. Good girl."

The grille swung open with a scream that went round the chapel three times.

Beyond it was a tunnel, round and brick-lined, and in it black water, moving fast.

"Drainage," said La Taupe, with deep and sincere love. "Eighteenth-century. Mon Dieu, it's beautiful. That runs down to the old collector under the boulevard. We'll be wet to the neck. Follow me exactly. Exactly."

They were wet to the neck.

They came out forty minutes later through a manhole in a closed courtyard behind a school in the fourteenth, under an orange street lamp, at ten past two in the morning, and stood on the cobbles with the water running out of their waders onto their boots, and for no reason at all all three of them began to laugh. Not quietly. They laughed so hard that a light came on in a window above them, and La Taupe had to sit down on a bin, and Dec bent double with his hands on his knees, and Inès stood with her hand over her mouth and her glasses steamed solid and laughed until it turned into something else, and then turned back.

When it stopped, she wiped her glasses on the only dry thing she had, which was the inside of her collar, and put them back on.

"I'm going to the Jura," she said.

Dec straightened up. He was soaked to the skin, his hair was plastered flat, there was a long smear of yellow quarry mud across his forehead and he had never looked so pleased in his life.

"Right," he said. "Right. Well. I'm going with you."

"Why?"

"Professional reasons."

"What professional reasons?"

"It's a lock," said Dec. "Vey's lock. On the drawings. If his clock's in the Jura, his lock's in the Jura, and I want to see it." He paused. "Purely professionally."

"Purely professionally," said Inès.

"Totally."

From the bin, La Taupe looked from one of them to the other, and said something under her breath about Spinoza that Inès chose not to hear.
