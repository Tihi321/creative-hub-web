The Commissaire's Lunch

The police judiciaire of Paris lived in a building that looked as though it had been designed to sell very expensive mineral water. It stood at the edge of the seventeenth arrondissement, beside the ring road, all glass and pale stone and sharp corners, and Dec, who had been expecting something with gargoyles, found it a disappointment.

The interview room was worse. It was clean. There was nothing in it to look at except a table, three chairs, a camera in the corner and a window onto a corridor, and Dec had spent the first forty minutes looking at all of them in turn, and then at his own hands, and then at the door, which had a magnetic lock with a card reader and no keyhole, which he took personally.

At twelve fifteen the door clicked and Commissaire Pichon came in carrying a tray.

On the tray was a cassoulet in a brown earthenware dish, still bubbling at the edges. There was half a baguette, a glass of red wine, a glass of water, a paper napkin and a folder. Pichon put the tray down on the table, sat, tucked the paper napkin into his collar with the care of a man laying a foundation stone, and broke the crust of the cassoulet with a spoon. Steam came out. It smelled of duck fat and garlic and the beans.

Dec had not eaten since a croissant at seven.

"You don't mind," said Pichon. It was not a question. "If I don't eat at twelve I become unjust. My wife says so. She is right about most things." He took a spoonful and closed his eyes for a moment. Then he opened them, and opened the folder, and put on the reading glasses from the cord round his neck. "Declan Patrick Hartley. Born in Galway, Ireland, thirty-four years ago. Locksmith. Company dissolved, twenty twenty-two. Now..." he turned a page, "...designer of experiential puzzle environments."

"Escape rooms."

"So I understand. Your landlord says you are polite and you sing in the shower. Your bank says less than your landlord. You have been in Paris for six weeks. You have no criminal record in France, nor in Ireland, which surprises me a little, given your hobbies." Another spoonful. "Tell me about the back door."

So Dec told him about the back door.

He told him properly, because he could not do it any other way. He told him about thumb-turn cylinders and mortice locks and the difference between them. He told him that you could buy that cylinder in any hardware shop from Lisbon to Lublin for nineteen euros ninety-nine and that it had five pins and three of them were not worth the brass they were cut from. He told him about the scratches round the keyway: bright, fresh, every one of them going in, and a professional would never leave a mark like that because a professional did not rake a lock, he read it, one pin at a time, like reading braille, and left the face of the cylinder as clean as he found it.

"So the scratches are put there on purpose," Dec said. "For show. Somebody's dragged a pick in and out to make it look like a break-in. That's the first thing."

"And the second thing?"

"The second thing's the thumb-turn." Dec leaned forward. He had forgotten about the cassoulet. "You lock that door from inside by turning the knob. From outside, only with the key. Right? So say I'm your burglar. I pick it from the yard, badly, scratch scratch, I'm in. I do whatever I do to the clocks. Then I leave. How do I leave? If I go out the back, I can't lock it behind me unless I've a key, and if I've a key, why did I pick it? If I go out the front, then the back door was only ever locked by somebody standing inside. Either way it doesn't work. It's a door that's telling you a story, and the story's wrong."

"What story should it tell?"

Dec sat back.

"That somebody came in the front," he said slowly. "Easy as you like. Rang the bell, maybe. He's a clockmaker, people ring his bell. Or they had a key. And then they put a brand-new cheap lock in his back door, which is a funny thing to do in the middle of a crime, and then they picked it, badly, from the outside, so the police would find a burglary, and then they went back in and locked it from the inside out of pure habit, because people lock doors. And then they left by the front." He shook his head. "That's mad. Who changes a lock in the middle of the night to pretend they broke it?"

"Someone who wishes the police to find a burglar," said Pichon. "And go home."

He said it mildly. He ate another spoonful. But Dec, watching him, had the sudden feeling that the commissaire had heard this story before, and had not liked it the first time either.

"How did you know about the drawing?" Pichon asked.

"I saw it. In the clock. I opened the door of the clock."

"Why?"

"Because the pendulum was hanging wrong. Off to one side. You don't get that with a clock that's run down, it hangs straight down, dead. That one was being held." Dec held up a thumb and finger. "Bit of brass, about this size, jammed in the crutch. That's the part that pushes the pendulum. Somebody wound it, set it to nine, and jammed it. And then they drew on the inside of the case, and it's not a scribble. It's careful. Like they were signing it."

Pichon put his spoon down.

He took a photograph out of the folder and turned it round and pushed it across the table. It was a colour print, a little blurred, of the inside of a different clock case: a different wood, darker, with a brass maker's plate screwed to it. On the bare wood beside the plate, in white chalk, was a circle with twelve marks round it and IX at the top.

"October," said Pichon. "A retired judge in the eleventh. Monsieur Delorme. He woke on a Tuesday thinking it was Monday. Every clock in his flat at nine. A little burglary at the kitchen window, nothing taken." Another photograph, another clock: a cheap kitchen clock with its back off, the chalk mark on the white plastic. "January. A bookseller, Madame Aubry, rue de la Roquette. The same." A third: a church clock's mechanism, all iron and grime, and the chalk mark on a beam. "February. A curate at Saint-Médard. The same. All three said everything tasted of cloves. All three were sent home by the doctors with advice to rest and drink less. One of the three, the judge, drank nothing at all."

Dec looked at the three photographs and then at Pichon.

"None of this," Pichon said, "was in the newspapers. Not the chalk. Not the cloves. The newspapers have had the Stopped Hour, a ghost story, a joke for a quiet week. So when I find a man on his knees in the fourth case with his little knives in the lock, who tells me about the chalk and the cloves before I have asked him one question, you understand, Monsieur Hartley, I become interested in that man."

"I just looked," said Dec. "That's all. I looked at what was there."

"Yes," said Pichon. "That is the trouble. Most people don't."

There was a knock at the door of the interview room. A young officer put his head round it, looking embarrassed.

"Commissaire. There's a woman downstairs. She says she has something belonging to the prisoner. She says she won't give it to anyone but you. She says," he consulted a slip of paper, "that she has been waiting for forty minutes and that the coffee machine in the lobby is a crime and you should arrest it."

Pichon looked at Dec over his reading glasses.

"Friend of yours?"

"I don't have any friends in Paris," Dec said. "I've a landlord who likes my singing."

"Bring her up," said Pichon, and went back to his beans.

It was her. Dec knew it would be before the door opened again, and he could not have said how he knew. The woman from the pavement in the rue des Gravilliers came into the bare little room as if she had come to inspect it and found it wanting. Short black hair, cut sharp at the jaw. Round tortoiseshell glasses. A navy trench coat and a long scarf the colour of English mustard, and grey ankle boots, and her fingertips stained at the ends in faint colours, yellow and blue, like a child who had been at the paints. She was about thirty and she had a face that did not move much and eyes that missed nothing at all.

She looked at Pichon's napkin. She looked at the cassoulet. She looked at Dec, briefly, and he had the strong impression of having been weighed and found to be roughly what she expected.

"Commissaire Pichon. My name is Inès Varga. I'm a paintings restorer at the Atelier Morel, rue Vieille-du-Temple." Her French was quick and precise, every consonant in its place, and she switched to English for Dec without being asked, which was either kindness or efficiency, and he suspected she would not have been able to tell you which. "This fell out of your pocket. Outside the clock shop."

She put the little brass gear on the table between them.

"Oh," said Dec. "Oh, thanks. I didn't even notice. That's the pattern piece. They sent it with the parts list."

"Who did?" said Pichon and Inès Varga at exactly the same moment, and then looked at each other with what Dec thought was mutual irritation.

"The Fondation Vaulmont. My client. It came in the post with the list of parts. Please match all wheels to this pattern. It's their logo, isn't it, stamped on it there, I thought it was their logo..." He picked the gear up and turned it to the light, and saw, really saw for the first time, the tiny stamped circle with its twelve ticks and the IX at the top.

He put it down again.

Pichon set his spoon in the dish. In the corridor outside, someone laughed and a door banged.

Inès Varga leaned over the table, very close to Pichon's photograph of the judge's clock, so close that her glasses slid down and she pushed them back up with one knuckle without seeming to notice she had done it.

"I've seen this mark," she said quietly, and the English had gone flat and careful. "This morning. Under the varnish of a painting from about sixteen fifty. Someone painted this same clock on a sundial, and then someone else painted it out, and wrote a line in Latin under it about a loan and the ninth hour, and then scraped out one word of the line with a knife." She straightened. "I have photographs."

Pichon looked at her for a long moment. Then he took off his reading glasses and let them fall on their cord, and pressed the bridge of his nose between finger and thumb, and for the first time that day he looked his age and a little more.

"Of course you do," he said.

They let Dec go at twenty past two.

Pichon walked them down himself, which Dec suspected was not usual. In the glass lobby, by the coffee machine Inès had wanted arrested, he stopped and buttoned his raincoat and looked at them both with an expression that was almost, but not quite, fatherly.

"Monsieur Hartley. I have no evidence against you except bad luck and a bag of little knives, and in France neither is yet a crime. Madame Varga. Thank you for the gear. I'll keep it. You will send me your photographs." He paused. "And then, both of you, please, go home. Mend your painting. Build your rooms. This is a ghost story for a quiet week, and that is all it is going to be, and if I find either of you in the fifth case of it, I will be very sad, and I will eat a very large lunch, and then I will put you in a cell."

"There's a fifth?" said Dec.

"There is always a fifth," said Pichon, and went back upstairs.

Outside it had started to drizzle, a fine Paris drizzle that did not so much fall as hang about. Inès Varga stood on the steps and wound her mustard scarf round her neck once more and looked at the ring road as though it had personally disappointed her.

"Thanks," Dec said. "For coming. You didn't have to."

"I didn't come for you. I came for the mark."

"Right. No, I know. Still." He shoved his hands in his jacket pockets. "So that's that, then."

"Yes."

"We should go home. Like he said."

"Yes."

"We're not, I mean, it's not as if we're, you know." He made a vague gesture that took in both of them and the ring road and the drizzle. "Partners or anything. In the..."

"No," said Inès firmly. "We are not partners."

"Grand. Glad we sorted that."

"Good."

"Good."

They stood there in the drizzle not being partners. A bus went by.

"What did it say?" Dec asked. "The Latin. Under the varnish."

She looked at him sideways. For a moment he thought she would not answer.

"Mutuo datum," she said. "Given as a loan. Then a word I can't read, because someone took a knife to it. Then hora nona. At the ninth hour."

His phone rang.

He nearly let it ring. The number was a stranger's. But it was a Paris number, and he had a client in Paris, and a bank that said less about him than his landlord, so he did.

"Monsieur Hartley?" A woman's voice, young, smooth, in an English as polished as a shop window. "This is Mademoiselle Ferrand at the Fondation Vaulmont. Monsieur de Vaulmont asked me to call. We heard about this morning's unpleasantness at Monsieur Roux's shop. We do hope you are all right."

Dec opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

"You were collecting our parts," the voice went on, kindly. "From the list we sent. Monsieur de Vaulmont chose Monsieur Roux's shop for you himself, you know; he admires his work enormously. We just wanted to remind you that the clockmaker's cell must be finished and installed by the thirty-first. The vigil is on the first of April. It is the day of the eclipse. It cannot move." A tiny pause, like a held breath. "Nothing about it can move, Monsieur Hartley. You understand."

"I, yes," said Dec. "Yes. Of course."

"Wonderful. Monsieur de Vaulmont sends his regards."

The line went dead.

Dec lowered the phone and found Inès Varga watching him, very still, with the drizzle beading on her glasses.

"Who," she said, "was that?"

"My client," said Dec. "The ones with the logo." He looked at the phone, and then up at the clean glass front of the police judiciaire, where somewhere on the fourth floor a commissaire was finishing his wine. "They knew I'd been arrested. Before I'd told a soul."
