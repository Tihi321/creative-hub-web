The Seventh of the Nine

The lights were on in the atelier.

Inès saw them from the street at ten past eleven on Sunday morning, coming round the corner of the rue Vieille-du-Temple with her bag of books and no sleep: the skylight on the top floor glowing a hard white against the grey Paris sky. Nobody worked on Sundays at Atelier Morel. Morel himself had a rule about it. Paintings need a day off, Inès, en somme, like people.

She went up the four flights without making a sound, which she had not known she could do.

The door of the atelier was ajar. Through it she could see her own bench under the north light, and the big easel, and on the easel the Allegory of the Sundial, and in front of it, on her stool, in her apron, with her UV lamp wheeled up beside him and her raking light angled flat across the canvas and a scalpel in his hand, Jean-Paul Morel.

He was working on the sundial. On the gap in the Latin line. He had a swab in one hand and the scalpel in the other, and he was bent so close to the canvas his nose was almost on it, and his thin shoulders were hunched up round his ears in the old grey cashmere, and he was so absorbed that he did not hear her come in. She stood in the doorway and watched him for perhaps half a minute. He was not good at it. She could see that from the door. He was going too deep, too fast, with too much solvent, in the place where the ghost ticks of the painter's spacing marks lay in the ground. In a quarter of an hour, at that rate, there would be nothing left of them at all.

"Jean-Paul," she said. "Stop. You're taking off the ground."

He jumped so violently the scalpel went skittering across the floor. He turned on the stool. His face was grey and wet and old, and he looked at her as if she had come up out of the floor.

"Inès. You're... you said a week. Your family."

"I lied," said Inès. "So did you."

He did not try to deny it. She had expected him to, and had prepared, on the first train from Basel at six that morning, after a sleepless night in a station hotel, with Dec asleep on her shoulder, a list of things to say, in order, beginning with the handwriting on the flyleaf of the notebook and ending with our restorer. She did not need any of them. He looked at her, and his mouth worked, and then he put his face in his hands, there on her stool in front of the painting, and began to cry. Not loudly. The way a tap drips.

She waited. She picked up the scalpel and put it in the tray. She switched off the UV lamp, because it was bad for the paint. Then she pulled up the other stool and sat down, and waited some more.

"The gallery," he said at last, into his hands. "In two thousand and nineteen. You weren't here yet. I opened a gallery, a selling gallery, in the eighth, with a partner, and the partner went to Brazil, and then there was the virus, and then there was nothing. I owed everybody. The bank. Three banks. My wife doesn't know. My wife still thinks the flat in Vincennes is ours." He lifted his face. "And then one day Lassalle came to see me. Gérard Lassalle. He'd been my notary for years. He said he had a client who was a patron of the arts. Who liked to help people in difficulties. Who could buy my debts. All of them. And all I had to do was a few small favours, now and then, for his foundation."

"Vaulmont," said Inès.

Morel flinched as if she had hit him.

"What favours?"

"Little things. At first. A valuation. A loan of a painting for a party. And then last autumn they asked me if I still remembered my radio." A cracked, terrible little laugh. "I was a radio amateur when I was a boy. In Brittany. I built transmitters in my father's shed. Long wave. I told Lassalle that once, over a dinner, years ago, as a joke. And last autumn they gave me a list of figures and asked me to check them. Power. Frequency. Range. For a project. A heritage project, they said, a son et lumière. I checked them. I wrote some notes. I wrote my name in the front of the notebook, Inès, like an idiot, like a schoolboy, because it was a nice notebook." He wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I didn't ask what it was for. You understand? I didn't ask. That's what I was being paid for. Not to ask."

"And the painting?"

"The painting was this year. They had it in a vault somewhere. They said there was a word under the varnish. A word that mattered to them, very much, for an old tradition. Someone had scraped it out, centuries ago, and they needed it back, for their ceremony, on the first. They needed a restorer who could find it without anybody else seeing." He looked at her with an awful sort of pleading. "I'm not good enough, Inès. You know I'm not. I'm a businessman with a diploma. So I gave it to you. I thought you'd find the word and I'd take it to them and that would be the end of it, and you'd never know what it was, and nobody would be hurt."

"Gérard Lassalle is in the river."

"I know."

"Anselme Roux lost a day."

"I know." His voice broke completely. "I know. And on Friday a man telephoned me, a young man, very polite, and said that Maître Lassalle, who had worn their watch for twenty years, had been afraid at the end, and that fear is a debt like any other, and that he had paid it. And that he hoped I was not afraid." He looked at the painting, the woman in the saffron sleeve pointing at the sky. "And that he would expect the word by Sunday night."

Inès looked at the sundial too. At the gap in the Latin line, and the little ghost ticks of the painter's spacing, nine of them, which Morel had nearly wiped off the face of the earth in a morning.

"Did you find it?" she asked quietly.

"No." He shook his head. "No. I can't see anything. I can't see what you see. I never could."

She believed him.

She did not tell him about the ledger in the tower, or the burnt page, or REMIT, or the nine ticks in her notebook with an R at the start and an E at the end. She did not tell him anything at all. She was sitting there, deciding not to, when the bell at the street door rang four floors down, a long, polite, unhurried ring, and Jean-Paul Morel's face went the colour of old paper.

He came up the stairs alone.

She heard him all the way: four flights of an old Marais staircase, and the footsteps were even and unhurried on every one of them, and when he reached the top he was not out of breath. He stopped in the doorway of the atelier and looked round it with real interest, at the easels and the lamps and the jars of pigment on the shelves, the way a man looks round a room he has paid for and is glad to see at last.

He was about sixty. Silver hair, brushed straight back from a high forehead. A face that was calm in the way deep water is calm, smooth and pale and still, with fine lines at the eyes that looked as though they came from smiling, though he was not smiling now. A grey three-piece suit of a cut so good it made the room look shabby. A dark tie. And across the grey waistcoat, a silver chain, and on the end of it, in the small pocket, the round edge of a silver watch.

"Madame Varga," he said. "Forgive me for coming on a Sunday. Auguste de Vaulmont."

It was the voice. Of course it was. Inès had known it would be from the first footstep on the stairs, and it went through her anyway, like cold water. Warm oil, Roux had said. Poured slowly. She had heard it in the dark under the Barrière d'Enfer, saying Lassalle has been remitted.

"Monsieur de Vaulmont," she said. Her own voice came out quite normal. She would be proud of that, later.

"I've come for the Allegory. We spoke of it, Jean-Paul, you remember." He kept his back to Morel. "It's needed for the vigil on Wednesday, the first of April, and I'm told the cleaning is far enough along to show. My people are downstairs with a van." He crossed to the easel and stood in front of the painting with his hands clasped behind his back, and took it in, the woman and the sundial and the saffron sleeve, and the clean square of cold blue sky where Inès had worked. "Beautiful," he said softly. "You have very good hands, Madame Varga. That blue. I've owned this picture for thirty years, and I never knew the sky was that colour."

"You," said Inès. "You're the anonymous buyer."

"Owner, not buyer. Poor Gérard liked his little fictions; a notary is never happier than when he stands between two people who must not meet." His eyes stayed on the painted sky. "I asked him to find me the best pair of hands in Paris without telling them whose painting it was. He did. I'm grateful to him for that, at least."

"It isn't finished."

"Nothing is ever finished. That is rather the point of it." He turned to her. His eyes were grey, very pale, very clear, and they rested on her face with an attention that was almost gentle. "You were away, I think. In the mountains. The air on the plateau is very good in March. Very clear. One can hear bells for miles."

She said nothing. Her hands were in the pockets of her apron, and she found they had closed into fists.

"I knew your father," Vaulmont said.

The room went very quiet.

"Not well. I want to be honest with you. A little. Zoltán Varga. He drove the twenty-eight in Lyon, the line that goes past the Hôtel-Dieu, for many years. I sat on the board of a foundation that supported the hospital there, and I would take his bus from Part-Dieu, when I was younger and still took buses. He used to sing under his breath in Hungarian at the red lights. He had a very fine baritone." The pale eyes did not leave her face. "I was so sorry to hear about his illness. To lose one's days like that, one by one, and never to know which ones. It's the cruellest of all the debts, I think. The ones we pay without knowing we owe them." He paused. "Grief is a kind of interest, isn't it, Madame Varga? We go on paying it long after the loan is gone."

Inès kept her fists where they were, in the apron pockets, and stood quite still. She did not trust anything she might do if she moved.

He knew. He knew where she had been and what she had seen and what she had heard in the dark and what her father had sung at red lights. He had not threatened her. He had not said a single thing that a stranger could have called a threat. He had simply told her what he held.

"Your Monsieur Hartley," Vaulmont went on in the same gentle voice, "is building us a very charming room. I've seen the drawings. A medieval clockmaker's cell. He has real talent. I do hope nothing interrupts his contract; it's so hard for a young foreigner to find his feet in Paris. And Jean-Paul's affairs, of course, which are so complicated." He did look at Morel then, for the first time, briefly, and Morel shrank on his stool. "And this atelier. Such a beautiful room. It is mortgaged, you know, to a bank on whose board I sit." He smiled, at last, very slightly. "Time, Madame Varga. We all hold a little of each other's time. That's all I've come to say. We're all of us in debt to someone."

He took out the silver watch. He opened its case, the way any man looks at the time. He looked into it for a moment at nothing, at a white enamel face with no hands. Then he closed it and put it away.

"Did you find the word?" he asked, very kindly.

"No," said Inès.

He looked at her for a long time.

"No," he agreed. "I don't suppose you did."

They took the painting away at a quarter to twelve. Two men in grey overalls came up and wrapped it in blankets and carried it down the four flights between them, and at the bottom of the stairs, by the van, waiting, with its back doors open, stood Brother Kessler, huge and bald and silent, and he lifted the painting into the van as gently as if it were asleep.

Vaulmont buttoned his coat at the street door, got into a long dark car with a driver, and was gone.

Inès stood at the atelier window and watched the van turn the corner, and then she went and sat on her stool in front of the empty easel and looked at the place where the painting had been. She had her photographs. Forty-one of them, numbered and logged, in her phone and in the cloud and, since Saturday night, on a memory stick in the inside pocket of her coat that she had not taken off since. She had the nine ticks in her notebook. She had an R and an E. She had REMIT.

She did not have the painting. And in three days a man who had once taken her father's bus was going to stand under the dome of the Panthéon at three o'clock in the dark and tell the whole of France that God had stopped the clocks.

Behind her Morel said, very quietly, "What do I do, Inès?"

"Go home," she said, without turning round. "Lock your door. Don't drink anything anyone gives you."

She telephoned him at ten that night. She told herself it was to check. There was no answer. She telephoned again at half past, and at eleven, and at eleven she telephoned Dec instead, and he was outside her building on a hired bicycle in nineteen minutes, which was not possible, and they went to Vincennes together in a taxi that she paid for and he apologised about all the way.

The concierge of Morel's building let them in, in her dressing gown, frightened and furious. The door of the flat on the third floor was not locked.

Jean-Paul Morel was sitting in his armchair in his living room in his grey cashmere sweater, with his hands flat on the arms of the chair, staring at the wall. His eyes were open. They stayed on the wall when Dec and Inès came in. He seemed to have no idea they were there. His wife, the concierge said, was away at her sister's in Rennes; she had gone on Friday. On the low table by his chair there was a cup and a saucer, and in the cup, a finger's depth of cold tea.

All round the room, on the mantelpiece and the bookshelves and the wall above the television, the clocks said nine. A carriage clock. A kitchen clock through the door. The little clock on the oven. The cable box under the television, its green digits frozen. 09:00.

Dec went to the mantelpiece and looked at the carriage clock without touching it, and then crouched and looked at the old brass clock in the hall, and opened its little door, and Inès did not need to ask what he had found.

She knelt in front of Morel's chair and took his hand. It was cold and limp and did not close on hers. She looked into his face and he looked past her at the wall.

"Jean-Paul," she said. "Jean-Paul, can you hear me?"

His mouth moved. It made a sound.

"Sunday," it said.

She pressed his wrist and found the pulse, slow and steady and far away. She looked at his tongue when his mouth fell open. Grey. Darker than Roux's. Much darker.

Behind her, Dec was already on the phone to the emergency services, in his terrible French, spelling the address twice. Inès stayed on her knees by the armchair, holding the cold hand of the man who had given her a job when nobody else in Paris would look at a restorer from Lyon with a Hungarian name, and who had sold her to the Brotherhood of the Ninth Hour because he was frightened, and who had told her not to go home but had gone home himself.

Morel will have the word by Sunday, Fabien had said on the phone by the woodpile. And then, as the rule says.

He had not had the word by Sunday night. And now Sunday night had come, and he would not have anything at all for weeks, perhaps for months, possibly ever.

They were not punishing him, Inès understood, kneeling there. It was not anger. It was housekeeping. A debt that had stopped being useful had been written off.

The Brotherhood was cleaning up.

And she was the only restorer they had left.
