Bail Before Dawn

At nine o'clock on Thursday morning, in the narrow green shop in the rue des Gravilliers, Anselme Roux stood on a stepladder in his brown work coat with a winding key in his hand and two hundred clocks around him, and every one of them was stopped.

He had stopped them himself. He wanted that understood.

"Yesterday," he said from the top of the stepladder, to Inès and Dec on their stools below, and to Madame Ferré from the bakery next door, and to the old lady in the fur hat, who had come in uninvited and refused to leave, "at three o'clock, I was sitting here in my shop, listening to the radio, and the radio said that in the middle of Paris the stations had all stopped at nine, Austerlitz and Montparnasse and the Gare de Lyon, and the traffic lights from here to the Seine had all gone red, and the hospitals, and the man on the radio was frightened. And I looked at my clocks." He pointed the winding key round the walls. "And my clocks did not stop. Not one. Because not one of my clocks listens to Allouis, or to anybody. My clocks listen to me." He sniffed. "So this morning I stopped them all, by hand. At nine. To see how it felt to do it to myself."

"And how does it feel?" asked Inès.

"Stupid," said Roux. "Watch."

He reached up to the regulator on the top shelf, the big walnut one, and touched its pendulum with one finger, and it began to swing. And then he came down a step and started the carriage clock, and the next, and the next, quickly, moving along the shelves with his small spotted hands, setting each one going with a touch or a turn of the key, and at the bottom of the stepladder he did the longcase, his father's clock, with its chalk long wiped away, and opened the little door in its trunk and gave the pendulum a push, very gently, the way you push a child on a swing.

The shop filled with ticking. It came up from all sides like rain starting on a roof, one drop and then a dozen and then all of them, none of them together. The cat clocks swung their eyes left and right. The cuckoo over the workshop door whirred and shot out and said cuckoo once, as if clearing its throat, and went back in.

Roux stood in the middle of it all with his winding key and his eyebrows and listened, with his head on one side, until he was satisfied.

"There," he said. "That's the only miracle I've ever approved of."

The old lady in the fur hat burst into tears. Madame Ferré from the bakery gave her a croissant.

Pichon took them to lunch at half past twelve. It was, he informed them in the taxi, a very serious lunch, at a very serious restaurant in the sixth that had been serving the same eleven dishes since nineteen fifty-two, and he would not discuss the case until the second course, because he had been awake for thirty hours and was not, at present, a just man.

At the second course, which was a blanquette de veau in a sauce so white and smooth Inès could have used it as a ground for gilding, he put down his fork, and tucked his napkin more firmly into his collar, and told them.

"Fabien Ruel," he said, "has confessed to everything. The tea at the inn in Les Bréviaires, Monsieur Hartley. Madame Aubry's flat. The curate. Monsieur Roux's coffee, on the Sunday before last, here in Paris, four days before he took the train to Switzerland after you. Monsieur Morel's tea, and the staging of the clocks every time. He confessed in great detail and with some pride and in the form, I'm afraid, of a long sermon, which took my sergeant four hours to type. He believes he will be forgiven. He was very struck, I think, by the word on the lock." Pichon took a sip of wine. "Your young waiter is pressing no charges for the bite."

"He bit Moussa?" said Dec.

"Moussa was sitting on him." Pichon's moustache moved very slightly. "The Centre des monuments nationaux, by the way, has asked me to tell you that it would like to keep your clockmaker's cell in the crypt of the Panthéon until the end of the summer, as an educational exhibit. It wants to know your fee. I told them it was very high."

Dec opened his mouth and shut it again.

"Kessler," Pichon went on, "has said nothing at all, which I'm told is his custom. His hands match the marks on Maître Lassalle's arms. That will do. He did ask one thing, last night, in his cell, through an interpreter, because it turns out he speaks only Swiss German, and not very much of that." He looked at Inès over his glasses. "He asked whether someone could make sure the netting in the dome of the Panthéon was checked for birds."

Inès looked down at her blanquette and pushed a mushroom slowly round the sauce with her fork until she trusted her face again.

"Jean-Paul Morel," said Pichon, more gently, "woke up at six o'clock this morning at the Hôtel-Dieu, and asked for his wife, and then asked what day it was, and when they told him, he wept. The doctors think he'll be well. He would like to speak to me. At length. He would also like, he says, to speak to you, Madame Varga, when you can bear it." He paused. "I told him that might be some time."

"It might not," said Inès. She was surprised to hear it.

"And the minister of the interior," Pichon said, picking up his fork again, "has telephoned the prefect of police this morning at seven to thank him for his long and distinguished service and to accept his wish to spend more time in Normandy. The prefect did not know he had that wish until seven o'clock this morning. He has it now." He ate a piece of veal. "And the wine. Four jugs. The laboratory says datura, clove, and the ash of burnt paper. They're still working out what paper."

"And Vaulmont?" said Dec.

Pichon put his fork down again.

He took his time. He reached into the inside pocket of his raincoat, which was hanging on the back of his chair, and took out a small clear plastic evidence bag, and put it on the white tablecloth between the bread basket and the salt.

Inside it was a silver pocket watch on a short chain. Plain. Heavy. Its face was white enamel, with twelve black numerals and no hands.

"He was released," Pichon said, "at four o'clock this morning. On bail. The examining magistrate set it at two million euros, because she is a sensible woman and she wished to keep him. It was paid in full, by bank transfer, at three fifty-one, from a foundation registered in Liechtenstein that did not exist on Tuesday and does not exist this morning." He looked at the watch in its bag. "He walked out of the building on the quai des Orfèvres into a car that was waiting for him, and the car drove away, and I'm told by my colleagues at the airports and the stations that he has not taken a plane or a train. He has simply gone." He turned the bag over with one finger. "He left this. His own watch, the one he wore on his waistcoat chain at your atelier and under the dome. They took it from him at the Panthéon with his tie and his belt, and when they gave him back his things at four this morning, he did not ask for it."

Inès picked up the bag and opened the back of the watch through the plastic with her thumbnail, the way she had on the Pont de Sully, a week and a lifetime ago.

Inside the lid, where the nine little engraved wheels had been on Lassalle's watch, there was something else. A single line, engraved very finely into the silver, in a plain old-fashioned script.

VII de IX.

The seventh of nine.

She closed the case. Dec set his glass down on the cloth, very carefully, as if it might go off. At the next table a man laughed at something, and a waiter dropped a spoon, and in the kitchen someone was singing.

"So there are eight more," said Dec at last.

"There are eight more," Pichon agreed. "The Swiss police searched the forester's house at Les Bréviaires last night. They found the black notebook and the prototype board in the stove, half burnt. Vey's ledger was not there. Fabien says it went to the Seventh." He touched the bag with one finger. "So it went with him. And one of the others, I'm told, is called the Keeper of the Last Hour, though I'm also told that's only a story that nuns tell each other in the evenings." He took the bag back and put it in his raincoat. "I'll be keeping this. As a reminder. I have a very bad memory." He picked up his fork. "Now. I am going to have the cheese, and then the tarte Tatin, and then I'm going to go home and sleep for nineteen hours. And neither of you is going to be in the next case of anything. You hear me?"

"We hear you," said Dec.

"Good," said Pichon, who did not believe him, and called for the cheese.

Mère Clothilde telephoned at four, while Inès was on the stairs up to the atelier with a bag of groceries and Dec behind her carrying a toolbox.

"They've taken the wall down," said the old nun's voice, delighted, crackling with distance and snow. "The commune. This morning. The fire brigade did it. They've unbricked the dial so you can see the whole thing from the village, all nine hours, and Madame Jobin's nephew has been up the ladder with a pot of grease and a library book, and he's put the old twelve-hour wheel back in, the one my sisters took out in sixteen ten." She laughed. "It struck four just now. Four times. Not thirteen. The whole valley came out to listen. I cried. I've been drinking plum brandy since lunch." A pause. "Is he there? Your not-husband?"

"He's carrying a toolbox," said Inès.

"Good," said Mère Clothilde. "Give him a kiss from me. On the forehead. Don't let him make it into anything." And she rang off.

The atelier, at five o'clock on Thursday, was full of the low gold light that came through the skylight on clear spring evenings and lay across the benches and the easels and the jars of pigment like varnish.

The Allegory of the Sundial was back on its easel. It was evidence now, sealed with a strip of police tape across the corner of the frame and a tag on a string, and it would stay evidence for months, possibly years. But the examining magistrate, who was a sensible woman, had agreed that a seventeenth-century canvas with half its varnish off could not wait for the courts, and that the restorer who had already been working on it was the best person to go on, under seal, with a police officer looking in now and then. Pichon had told Inès this over the cheese, and she had felt something in her chest come loose that she had not known was tight.

She stood in front of it now in her apron. The woman in the saffron sleeve. The two-thirds of brown sky still waiting. The sundial, with its clock painted out and its gap in the Latin line, and the nine faint ticks in the ground.

She would not paint the word back in. That was not what a restorer did. She would stabilise the scrape, and clean round it, and document it, and leave the gap honest, so that anyone who looked closely, properly, would see where someone had taken a knife to the thing they were most afraid of.

Behind her, Dec was on his knees at the atelier door with the toolbox open and the lock half out, the old lock that had stuck every winter for four years and that Morel had never fixed. He had been on his knees at it for twenty minutes, humming. He was very happy. She could tell from the humming.

"This is a terrible lock," he said, with deep satisfaction. "Who put this in? It's a crime. It's a crime against doors. I'm going to put in a five-lever mortice with a..."

"Dec."

"Hm?"

She kept her eyes on the painting. "We're not partners."

There was a pause behind her. A screwdriver stopped.

"No," said Dec carefully.

"But this building," Inès said, "has fourteen doors. And every one of the locks is terrible. And I'm going to need somebody who can get into places." She turned round at last. He was sitting back on his heels on the floor by the door with a screwdriver in one hand and his mouth slightly open and the gold light from the skylight in his untidy hair. "It's a job. That's all. It pays very badly."

He looked at her for a long time.

"How badly?" he said.

"Very."

"Will there be tea?"

"There might be tea."

Dec Hartley put down the screwdriver and grinned, the whole ridiculous enormous Galway grin, the one that had looked at her through the back window of a police car eleven days ago and not known whether it was asking for help or apologising.

"Ah, go on, then," he said. "Purely professionally."

"Purely professionally," said Inès.

The bell at the street door rang, four floors down.

It was a courier. A young man in a motorcycle helmet who would not come up the stairs and would not say who had sent it, and who held out a parcel and a tablet to sign and was gone before Dec, coming down the last flight at a run, could ask him anything at all.

The parcel was small and square and wrapped in plain brown paper, and it was addressed, in a neat old-fashioned hand in grey ink, to two people. Mme I. Varga. M. D. Hartley. Atelier Morel, rue Vieille-du-Temple.

They took it up to the bench under the skylight and stood and looked at it for a while without touching it. Then Inès put on her white cotton gloves, because it was what she did, and slit the brown paper with her scalpel, very carefully, along one edge.

Inside, in a nest of grey tissue, lay a clock face.

It was old, enamel on copper, the size of a saucer, the kind that comes out of a carriage clock when the carriage clock is broken up for parts. Twelve black marks round its rim. No hands. No hole in the middle where the hands would go. And at the top, where twelve should be, a black and careful IX.

Under the IX, on the white enamel, in the same grey ink as the address, someone had written a single word.

HUIT.

Eight.

Neither of them said anything. Through the skylight the gold light was going amber, and somewhere across the rooftops of the Marais a church bell began to strike six, slowly, and Inès found herself counting, and found that Dec beside her was counting too, under his breath, in English, and they both stopped at six, and looked at each other, and went on looking.

"Well," said Dec at last, quietly. "That's not a partnership. That's an invitation."

Inès put the clock face back in its grey tissue and closed the box, and took off her gloves, and pushed her glasses up her nose with one knuckle.

"Get your coat," she said.
