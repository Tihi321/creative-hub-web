Frozen Hours

By six o'clock it was dark, and by seven Inès could no longer feel her feet, and by eight she had insulted Dec in Hungarian more times than she had insulted anyone in her life, including her brother, and he had not understood a word of it and seemed to find it comforting.

"Say the one with the horse again," he said, through his teeth, which were chattering.

"It isn't a horse."

"It sounded like a horse."

"It's about your mother and a horse," Inès admitted. "I'm sorry. I don't know your mother."

"She'd love you," said Dec. "She'd be terrified of you."

They were sitting on the floor of the clock chamber with their backs against the wall, as far from the slits as the room allowed, which was not far. The wind came in through the slits in long freezing gusts and brought the snow with it, a fine dry powder that settled on everything and did not melt, because nothing in the tower was warm enough to melt it. The iron cage of the clock loomed over them in the dark, a black shape against a darker one. They had turned off Dec's phone torch at six to save the battery. Hers had died at five past four.

Dec had tried the trap. Of course he had. He had lain on his stomach for half an hour with his ear to the boards and his fingers in the crack, and he had tried to get at the hasp with the thinnest pick he owned, and then with two picks tied together with a bootlace, and then with a strip of brass he had pulled off the bottom of the clock frame. The padlock was underneath, on the outside, out of reach. There was no keyhole on their side. There was nothing on their side but wood and cold.

"I can open anything," he had said finally, sitting up, with splinters in his fingers. "Anything with a keyhole. You give me a keyhole and a bit of time and I'll have it. But they've not given me a keyhole. They've given me a floor." He had said it lightly, but she had heard the thing underneath it, and had pulled her knees up inside her coat and waited, and after a while he had come and sat down next to her against the wall.

Minus twenty, Fabien had said.

She did not know what temperature it was now. She knew that her fingers did not want to bend and that the air hurt the inside of her nose when she breathed and that she was very, very tired, in a heavy, warm, sweet way that she knew from somewhere was bad. Her mother had read her a story once, when she was small, about a girl who sold matches in the snow. She had cried for an hour. She had not thought about that story in twenty-five years. She thought about it now.

"Talk to me," she said.

"What?"

"You always talk. Talk now. Don't let me go to sleep."

"Right," said Dec. "Right. Okay." He was quiet a moment. "I've a story about a door in Salthill."

It was a long story about a door in Salthill. It involved a Mrs Kilbride, a stuck mortice lock, a cat called Pádraig, and a misunderstanding about a sandwich. It was not funny, exactly. But it went on for a very long time and he told it with his whole body, waving his hands in the dark, and halfway through she found that she had stopped thinking about the girl with the matches.

When it was finished he was quiet for a while, and then he said, "Your turn."

"I don't have stories."

"Everybody's got stories."

"Not funny ones."

"Doesn't have to be funny." His shoulder was against hers. She could feel him shivering through two coats. "Tell me something true."

She did not decide to. That was what she would think afterwards. She did not decide to tell him; she was simply too cold and too tired to decide not to.

"My father lost days," she said.

Dec turned his head towards her in the dark.

"At the end. He had Alzheimer's. He was a bus driver in Lyon for thirty years, and he knew every street, and he taught me to swear in Hungarian and to look at things. Properly. Really look. Ne csak nézd, lásd. Don't just look. See." She pushed her glasses up and found they were frozen to her nose at the bridge, and left them. "At the end he'd wake up and it would be the wrong day. He'd think it was a Tuesday in nineteen ninety. He'd get dressed for work. And I'd have to tell him. Every morning. And every morning it was new to him, and he was so frightened." She stopped. "And these people. These people do that on purpose. To an old clockmaker. To a judge, to a priest. They take a day out of someone like you'd take a coin out of a fountain. And they make it a religion."

Her voice had gone up. She brought it down again.

"That's why," she said. "That's why I came."

Dec rubbed his frozen hands together, slowly, twice, and blew into them.

"I'm sorry," he said finally. "About your dad."

"Thank you."

"Mine just dropped dead in the van," Dec said. "Outside a chipper on Shop Street, with the engine running. Heart. Didn't feel a thing, they said. I was in Dublin. I'd moved to Dublin for a job, a proper job, with an office and a lanyard, because I thought the shop was a bit, you know. Small. And I wasn't there." He was quiet. "So I went back and took over the shop. To make it up to him, I suppose. And I ran it into the ground in a year. Couldn't charge anybody for my time. Every old dear in Galway had me round for tea and I'd fix her back door for the price of the part and a slice of brack." A noise that was almost a laugh. "I came to Paris because there was nothing left in Galway with my name on it. That's the truth. Escape rooms. Locking people in rooms to see if they can get out. Because I couldn't."

The wind came in through the slits and went over them and out again.

"You're not like that," Inès said.

"Like what?"

"A man who can't get out of a room." She turned her head, though she could not see him. "I watched you open that lock. For forty minutes. Without the word. You're the most stubborn person I've ever met, and I've met my mother."

He did not say anything. But she felt his shoulder, against hers, stop shivering for a moment.

They were quiet. It was not the bad quiet. It was the other kind.

Then, a long time later, he said, "We'll not mention any of this. After."

"No," said Inès. "Never."

"Grand."

At half past ten, by Dec's phone, she remembered the bell.

She had seen it when they first came up, and forgotten it, and the memory came back the way things come back when you are almost asleep: sideways, all at once. Above the clock chamber, through a square opening in the timber ceiling, the bell hung in the dark of the belfry. A big bell, green-black, with the iron hammer of the clock poised beside it on a long arm. And round its lip, cast into the bronze, there had been letters.

"Dec. The torch. Point it up."

He pointed it up. The beam went through the square hole in the ceiling and found the bell, hanging huge and silent, and the letters round its rim, worn but clear.

HORAM PULSAT QUI TRAHIT

She read it twice. Then she laughed, out loud, a short cracked sound in the freezing dark, and Dec swung the torch round to her in alarm.

"What? What does it say?"

"The hour is struck by whoever winds." She was already getting up. Her legs did not want to. She made them. "Trahere, to pull. To draw up. The weight. Whoever winds the clock, rings the bell. It's a motto. Vey's motto. Whoever holds the clock holds the time. Clothilde said." She put her frozen hand on the iron bars of the cage. "Dec. Can you make this go?"

He looked at the cage. At the huge wheels inside it, and the great stone weight on its rusty chain going down through the floor into the dark of the tower, and, at the side of the frame, the square end of an iron shaft sticking out, with an iron crank handle lying on the floor beneath it, where some clock-keeper had left it four hundred years ago.

Slowly, the way the sun comes up, he began to grin.

"Ah, now," he said. "Ah, now."

It took both of them.

The crank went onto the square end of the winding shaft, and turning it drew up the great stone weight from the bottom of the tower, link by link, on its chain. It was the hardest thing Inès had ever done with her body. The crank handle was as high as her chest and the weight felt like the whole tower and every turn took everything she had, and they did it together, Dec's hands over hers on the iron, both of them pushing and grunting and sliding on the frozen boards. At first she was colder than ever. Then, after fifty turns, she was not. After a hundred her face was hot and her glasses had steamed and the sweat was freezing in her hair. Somewhere in the cage a ratchet clacked, clack, clack, clack, like a huge slow heart.

"The striking side," Dec panted. "There's two trains in a clock like this. One for going and one for striking. That's the striking weight we're winding. When it's up, I let off the..." he groped in the cage, "...this, this lever here. And then she strikes. And if I hold the lever..."

"She keeps striking."

"She keeps striking till the weight runs down. Or I let go."

"Don't let go."

They wound until they could not wind any more. Then Dec reached into the iron cage with both arms, up to the shoulders, and found the lever, and looked at her, and she nodded, and he pulled.

Above them, in the dark, the hammer lifted, and fell.

The sound went through Inès like a train. It was so loud it was not a sound at all but a blow, a great bronze BONG that she felt in her teeth and her ribs and the soles of her frozen feet, and the whole tower hummed with it, and snow fell from the beams in a cloud. Before it had finished the hammer lifted again.

BONG.

She put her hands over her ears and laughed. She could not stop. Dec was laughing too, she could see it, his mouth wide open, though she could not hear a thing he said. He held the lever. The hammer rose and fell. Seven. Eight. Nine. It went out through the slits in the stone and down the valley in the dark, over the snow, over the black trees and the sleeping farmhouses and the village, where, Inès thought, every single person was now awake in bed and staring at the ceiling.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Thirteen.

Dec let go of the lever. The hammer stopped. The silence afterwards rang.

It was while they were waiting, in that ringing silence, that she found the page.

She had sat down against the cage, shaking with the effort, and put her hand down on the boards to steady herself, and her fingers had touched paper. Not on the floor. Under it. Wedged into the gap between the bottom bar of the clock frame and the floorboards, where it must have fallen or been pushed long ago, and where the winding, the shaking of the whole frame, had pushed it out an inch.

She drew it out with the end of her pencil and Dec held the torch.

A single leaf of parchment, brown and stiff, its bottom edge charred black. A page from the ledger, from Vey's book, from the half the sisters had not managed to burn. This one held no names and no hours. Nine small drawings ran down it in a column, each one a mark, a sort of seal: a key, a wheel, a closed eye, a pair of scales, a bell, a ladder, a hand held open, a mouth, a crown. Beside each one a Roman numeral, I to IX. And beside each numeral, in Vey's cramped Latin, a title.

She read them under her breath. She did not understand all of them. She understood enough.

I. Custos Horae Ultimae. The Keeper of the Last Hour.

And further down, beside the open hand:

VII. Exactor. The Collector.

"The Seventh will collect," said Dec softly, reading her face.

"The Nine," said Inès. "It's a list of the Nine." She looked at the column of little seals in the torchlight, the key and the eye and the crown. "These aren't people. They're offices. Like a government. Somebody holds each one. Now. Today."

Far down the valley, very small, an engine was coming. A high, angry, buzzing engine, getting louder. And with it, sirens.

Mère Clothilde arrived on a snowmobile at twenty past eleven, with a thermos of coffee laced with something that was not coffee strapped to the back of it, and behind her, on foot and on two more snowmobiles, came the volunteer fire brigade of Les Bréviaires, eleven men and two women in yellow helmets, one of them Madame Jobin's nephew, all in a state of great excitement, because the bell of Combe-Noire had not rung since sixteen hundred and ten and the whole village had come out into the street in its nightclothes.

They cut the padlock on the trap with a pair of bolt-cutters as long as Inès's arm. Madame Jobin's nephew put a foil blanket round her, and another round Dec, and Mère Clothilde climbed up the steel ladder in her snow boots at seventy-five as if it were the stairs to her own bedroom, and came up through the trap, and looked at the two of them sitting wrapped in foil against the clock with the crank still on the shaft, and at the little brown page in Inès's lap, and at their faces.

"Well," she said. "You've woken the dead. I suppose that's something." She unscrewed the thermos. "Drink. Don't argue."

They drank. It was coffee and plum brandy in about equal measure, and it went down Inès's throat like a lit match, and she felt it arrive in her stomach and then, slowly, in her feet.

It was later, in the back of a fire brigade Land Rover going down the valley with the heater roaring, that Dec took his phone out from inside his jumper, where it had been all night against his skin, and turned it on. Six per cent. He opened the photographs. He went through them in silence, page after page of the black notebook, graphs and tests and the line about the Panthéon. Inès watched him, half asleep against the window, wrapped in foil like a sandwich.

He stopped.

He went back one photograph, and enlarged it with two fingers, and then turned the phone round and held it out to her without a word.

The first photograph he had taken: the inside cover of the notebook. The flyleaf. Ruled lines, blank, and in the top corner, in small, neat, slanted blue biro, the way a person writes their name in a notebook in case they leave it on a train, a name.

J.-P. Morel.

Inès looked at it for a long time. She knew that handwriting. She had seen it every day for four years, on invoices, on condition reports, on the little yellow notes Jean-Paul left on her bench in the mornings. Inès, client called again, voilà, could you possibly...

Our restorer, the warm-oil voice had said, in the dark under Paris, is close to the word.

Not her. Him.

She handed the phone back to Dec, and turned and looked out of the window at the dark and the snow going past, and did not say anything at all, all the way down to the village.
