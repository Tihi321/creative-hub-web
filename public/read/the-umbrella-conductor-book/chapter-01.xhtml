The Umbrella Conductor

Rain fell on Zagreb that November evening. Soft rain, steady rain, the sort that pats the roof like a careful hand. Tap, tap, tap. It did not care who heard.

Along the shining cobbles came the number 12 tram with its yellow lights on. Breath had fogged the windows from the inside. The brass bell rang out, ding, ding, to say, here we come.

At the back of the tram, on a seat of her own, rode Ana Horvat, the conductor. Navy cap on her head. Navy coat with a brass badge pinned to it. Up her nose went her round glasses whenever a thought was coming. Often came her smile, and when it did you saw the small gap between her front teeth.

Ana knew a great many things. She knew which stop smelled of fresh bread in the morning. She knew which step was loose underfoot. She knew which child liked to ring the bell. Every face that ever rode her tram she knew. Every umbrella too.

Behind her seat stood a thin wooden rack with little brass hooks, and from those hooks hung the umbrellas people had forgotten.

A green one with white spots hung there. Beside it hung a black one, very tall and very serious. A tiny pink one belonged, surely, to somebody small. A blue one smelled a little of peppermint, because its owner kept sweets in the pocket.

"Nobody goes without their umbrella for long," Ana liked to say. "They come back for it. Sooner or later, they always come back."

And back they came. A lady in a red scarf came running for the green one with spots. A boy came with his grandfather for the pink one and said thank you in a tiny voice. Ana smiled her gap-toothed smile and handed each umbrella over. Never once did she let on that she had known exactly whose it was all along.

Tonight, though, one umbrella was left.

It hung on the very last hook, at the very end of the rack. Yellow it was, as yellow as a duckling, and its handle was carved in the shape of a duck's head, with a small round eye and a small round beak.

Ana looked at it. Three winters the yellow umbrella had hung there. Three winters of wet evenings. Three winters of nobody coming back.

"Well," said Ana, softly. "Well, well, well."

The tram stopped at the square. Ding, ding. A few people stepped down, and a few stepped up, shaking the rain from their coats. Ana lifted the yellow umbrella off its hook, and for the first time she really looked at it. Over she turned it, and over again, the way you might look at a letter with no name on the envelope.

Something small and white was tucked into the duck's neck. She eased it out between two careful fingers. It was an old tram ticket, soft as a leaf, and printed on it was a year: 1989.

"Goodness," said Ana. "That is a long time ago."

She opened the umbrella just inside the tram, where the rain could not reach. And there, on the yellow cloth, ran tiny stitches in blue thread. The stitches spelled two words: Savski Most.

"That is the name of a stop," said Ana. "Somebody loved this umbrella, to sew in where it was going."

Into her pocket went the ticket. Under her arm went the umbrella, and Ana made up her mind.

"Tonight," she said, "this umbrella will go home."

The tram went ding, ding, and rolled on. At the next stop, by the flower market, where the stalls were covered up for the night, Ana held the umbrella high.

"Does anyone know this umbrella? A yellow umbrella, with a duck?"

The passengers looked. A man with a newspaper looked. Two girls with a bag of warm chestnuts looked. They all smiled, and they all shook their heads.

"It is a lovely duck," said one of the girls. "But it is not mine."

"Thank you," said Ana. "Ding, ding."

At the next stop, by the old stone church, up went the umbrella again.

"Does anyone know this umbrella? A yellow umbrella, with a duck?"

An old woman with a basket looked. A boy with a dripping hat looked. They smiled, and they shook their heads.

"I would remember a duck," said the boy. "But it is not mine."

"Thank you," said Ana. "Ding, ding."

At the next stop, by the corner with the lamp that always flickered, she held it up a third time.

"Does anyone know this umbrella? A yellow umbrella, with a duck?"

Nobody looked for long. A sleepy dog looked. The sleepy dog wagged his tail, which was kind of him, but he did not know the umbrella either.

Ana laughed. "Thank you, dog," she said. "Ding, ding."

And so the tram rolled on, past the shops with their windows glowing like little lanterns, past the wet bright lamps, and the rain went on, soft and steady. One by one the passengers stepped down into the night. One by one they said goodnight, and soon the tram was almost empty.

At the very last stop of the night, the tram stopped with a sigh, and the doors folded open.

A man stepped up.

He was tall and thin, in a long grey coat and a brown felt hat with raindrops on the brim. His moustache was as white as milk, and he walked with a careful stride, one foot, then the other, as if the floor of the tram were something precious.

Off came his hat to Ana. "Good evening," he said. "Is this the last tram?"

"It is," said Ana. "The very last. Where are you going?"

The man gave no answer. His eyes had gone past her, to the rack behind her seat, to the empty hook at the end of it, and to the yellow umbrella with the duck's head, which Ana still held under her arm.

He stood very still. Then he took his hat off, and put it back on, as if he did not know what else to do with his hands.

"That umbrella," he said, in a quiet voice. "That is my wife's umbrella."

Ana set the umbrella down very slowly. "Your wife's?"

"Her name was Mira," said the old man. "My name is Kovac. She loved this tram, you know. She loved the number 12 more than any other. On rainy evenings she would say, Kovac, let us ride to the end and back, only to hear the rain on the roof."

"That sounds like a good thing to do," said Ana.

"It was." Mr Kovac smiled, and his moustache went up at the corners. "Savski Most. That was where we met, a long time ago. She sewed the name inside, so she would never forget it. And the ticket is from the first day we rode this line together."

Ana held out the umbrella. "Then it is yours," she said. "I have kept it safe for three winters, Mr Kovac. I never knew it was yours."

Mr Kovac did not take it. He looked at the duck, and the duck looked at him.

"I left it here," he said, "on purpose."

"On purpose?" said Ana.

"After Mira went away," he said, gently, "I could not carry it. It is a heavy thing, an umbrella, when there is only one person under it. So I left it on the tram, where she was happiest. I thought, perhaps someone kind will find it. Perhaps someone kind will keep it dry, and let it ride, and let a little bit of Mira keep riding too."

Ana said nothing for a moment. She pushed her glasses up her nose, because they had gone blurry.

"I think," she said at last, "that someone kind did."

Mr Kovac nodded. "I think so too."

Outside, the rain hummed on the roof as softly as a lullaby.

"Mr Kovac," said Ana. "This is the last tram of the night, and I am going on to the depot, and the depot is near your home, I think. Would you like to ride along? Both of you, you and the umbrella? Just to hear the rain on the roof?"

The old man's eyes were shining. "I would like that very much," he said.

So Ana rang the bell. Ding, ding. The doors folded shut, and the tram rolled away through the night, slow and warm and golden.

Mr Kovac sat down on the seat by the window. Ana sat beside him, and between them, on their knees, they opened the yellow umbrella together. It glowed like a small sun.

They did not talk very much. They did not need to. The tram rocked, and the lamps went by, and every drop on the roof sounded like a small, kind hello.

"Mira would have liked this," said Mr Kovac, after a while.

"I think she is liking it now," said Ana.

The tram rolled on, past the church, past the flower market, past the lamp that always flickered, and the sleepy dog, who was still there, lifted his head and wagged his tail once more.

At last the tram slowed down, near a quiet street with a green door. "This is my stop," said Mr Kovac.

He stood up slowly, and he put on his hat. Ana held out the yellow umbrella.

But Mr Kovac closed her fingers around the duck's head, very gently.

"Keep it, Conductor," he said. "Keep it for one more winter. And on rainy evenings, perhaps, I will ride to the end and back, and say good evening to it."

Ana smiled her gap-toothed smile. "You will always be welcome," she said. "Both of you."

Mr Kovac stepped down into the rain. He lifted his hat, and he walked away along the shining street, slowly, and at his green door he turned and waved.

Ana waved back. Then she rang the bell, ding, ding, and the tram rolled on.

At the depot she hung the yellow umbrella on its very own hook, right at the front of the rack, where she could see it from her seat. The duck's round eye seemed to twinkle.

"Goodnight, umbrella," said Ana.

Outside, the rain went on, tap, tap, tap, soft and slow, softer and slower, until it sounded like the very first beat of a dream.

And Ana, who knew every face and every umbrella, turned off the lamp, and went home to sleep.
