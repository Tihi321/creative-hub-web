Four Payments

The letter was wedged under the coffee pot. That meant Teo had found it first and put it there to soften the blow. Juno Halloway read it standing in the galley of the Ninth Hour while the harbor fog pressed its gray face to the porthole.

Four payments behind. Eleven thousand, four hundred and some dollars. The bank did not care about the some. The tug would be seized in forty days. Not sold. Seized. Taken off the water and set up on a hardstand somewhere while a clerk with clean shoes decided her future from a chair. Her father's name was still welded to the transom, HALLOWAY in steel letters he'd cut himself, and no clerk was going to chisel it off without a fight.

She read the letter twice. The first time for the numbers. The second time for the words, which were worse: "despite repeated notice," "final opportunity," "regrettable action." Words that had been waiting for her for months, patient as weather.

Then she folded it into quarters, twice more, until it was small and stiff, and put it in the inside pocket of her coat where it sat against her ribs like a stone. She'd carried stones before. The trick was to keep moving so it didn't press in one place too long.

Teo came down the ladder backward, wiping his hands on a rag that had been clean in some other decade. He was a compact man with a gray mustache and forearms like knotted rope, and he moved through the engine room with the easy confidence of a man who'd been crawling out of it for thirty years.

He saw the empty coffee pot where the letter had been. He saw her face.

"Don't," Juno said.

"I wasn't going to say a thing."

"You were going to say a thing about the injector."

"That too." He nodded at the porthole, where the fog moved like something breathing. "And the letter."

"The letter's nothing new."

"Eleven thousand's new. Last month it was nine."

"Last month was last month." She held his eye and said the rest of it flat, so he would hear it once and stop. "It's the note. Four payments on the note. That's all it is, and that's all I'll say of it."

Teo folded himself onto the bench across from her. The Ninth Hour rocked gently, a big patient animal at her mooring. Through the hull, water slapped the fenders and the tide ran its slow business, and somewhere above them a gull quarreled with the foghorn and lost.

"Juno. I can keep her running. You know I can. Give me wire and a prayer and I'll keep her running past the day they melt her for scrap. But I can't argue with a bank. A bank's got lawyers and lawyers don't listen to a man with a wrench."

"Nobody's asking you to argue."

"Then what are we going to do?"

She stood. Put her coffee down half-drunk, which she never did (her father had said a captain who wasted coffee wasted everything), and stood with her hand flat on the galley table, feeling the old wood's grain under her palm.

"Work," she said.

The injector on the old diesel had been failing for a week, coughing white smoke and dropping a cylinder whenever the load came on. The third injector in as many months and the last spare in the tin box, and the tin box was rusting through at the corner.

Teo fixed it the way he fixed everything: with the fourth thing to hand and no complaint about the first three.

The fourth thing was a crab pot.

It had come up in a net three seasons back and nobody had claimed it, so it had lived in the engine room ever since, a cage of galvanized wire with a few dry claws still trapped in the corners. He cut the wire off it with side-cutters, twisted it into a loop, and threaded the loop through the spring collar where the injector seated. He worked by feel, his head down in the engine room's dim yellow light, one cheek ticked with concentration, cursing softly in two languages.

"Hold the light steady," he said.

Juno held it steady. The light swung when the hull moved and she steadied it again.

"Your father would've used baling wire," Teo said, not looking up. "Baling wire and spite. Spite's what we're short on lately."

"We've got plenty of spite."

"You've got it. I'd like some back." He twisted the wire tighter, tested it with a thumbnail, and grunted. "This here's marine-grade crab pot wire. Zinc over steel. We're moving up in the world."

"Will it hold?"

"It'll hold until it don't." He set the collar, pulled the wire taut, and wiped his hands. "Give her throttle."

She climbed to the wheelhouse and opened the throttle. The diesel coughed like an old man clearing his throat, caught, dropped a beat, caught again, and settled into a hard clean rhythm that she could feel through the soles of her boots. The whole tug seemed to straighten, to remember what it was for.

Teo gave her a thumbs-up through the engine-room hatch, teeth white in his soot-streaked face.

"You see?" he called up. "Wire and a prayer."

"Don't spend it all at once."

The tow job was a barge of dredge spoil two miles up-channel, and it paid four hundred dollars, which was an insult dressed as money. Two men could have done it in half a day. A boy with a rowboat could have watched it drift.

Juno took it anyway. She took it with the bank letter in her coat pocket and a tide table in her head and the Bellmouth's fog closed around the tug like a hand.

The channel narrowed as they came out of the harbor. They passed the little white sign on the headland, the one that listed the wrecks on the reef in small letters worn thin by salt, and Juno kept her eyes off it. She'd read it enough times. Every name on it read like something she owed.

Teo came up to the wheelhouse and braced himself in the door, watching the foghorn buoy slide past. He had the barge's papers in one hand, flipping them against the wind.

"Big flat-hulled thing, that Meridian Dawn," he said, and nodded out at the gray. "I saw her in the harbor back in the summer. Paint already peeling off her bows like old skin. Halyard Shipping runs her, out of the coast. Big company. She's due the east dock today with a load of agricultural lime."

"Lime's a strange cargo in October. Nobody spreads lime in October."

"Everything's strange in October." He clipped the papers to the board. Then he stopped, his head half-turned. "You smell that?"

She did. The fog carried it in on the flood tide: cold, thin, a green metal tang under the diesel. Salt water where salt water had no business. Running early this year. The autumn surge usually came at the end of the month, pushing hard up the estuary and sitting in the flats like an unwanted guest. This year it had started with the first cool nights and had not stopped since.

"Surge's early," she said.

"Surge is early." Teo rubbed his jaw. "I don't like it early. Early means it's not done deciding what it wants to be."

The radio crackled at her elbow.

"Ninth Hour, Ninth Hour, this is Reach Harbor. Pell."

She freed the mic. "Reach Harbor, Ninth Hour. Go ahead."

"Juno. You outbound through the strait?"

"Tow of spoil for the east dock. Dredge owes me four hundred dollars."

The radio laughed, dry and long. "You're running the Bellmouth for four hundred? Child, I've known you to turn down four thousand."

"I've had a quiet autumn, Pell."

"Quiet's not the word I'd use." A pause, and she heard him shift in his chair, the squeak of a man who sat too much. "Listen. The surge is up early and it's not gentle. Came in at the mouth this morning pushing two knots against the ebb. My tide board's got it arriving at the flood tonight and staying through to morning, call it forty hours of bad water before it eases. Forty hours, Juno. Write that down, because I'll be saying it to the council before the week's out and I don't want to be the only one who said it. If it keeps up as far as I think, the salt line's going to kiss the estuary mouth. You know what's under this town."

"Fresh water. I know."

"My wells draw off the lens. It's the only water we've got between here and the ridge. The seawater's heavier than the freshwater. It slides in underneath like a thief, under the good water, and it doesn't leave when the tide goes out. If it gets the lens, Kestrel Reach is drinking from a tanker for a year, maybe two, and some of these people can't afford a tanker. My grandfather's the one who dug the first well. I don't want to hand it to my granddaughter gone bad."

"Pell. Dredge spoil. Four hundred dollars."

"Just keep your eyes up, is all. Tonight. Forty hours." A breath. "If the surge meets a vessel aground on the Bellmouth reef—"

"Then I'll charge double for the tow."

He laughed again, shorter this time. "Your father's mouth. God rest him, and you."

"Reach Harbor out."

She set the mic down and felt Teo looking at her.

"You didn't tell him," he said.

"Tell him what?"

"About the letter."

"He's got a town to keep. Not my debts."

"You're carrying it around in your pocket like it's a shell you found." He shrugged when she gave him nothing. "All right. All right. Just so long as I'm the one who knows."

"You're the one who knows," she said, and turned the wheel a quarter-turn, and gave the old diesel a little more throttle to hear it run clean.

The fog thinned at the strait's mouth and the Bellmouth opened before them, gray water running fast between two low headlands. A rippled pewter channel, narrow as a jug's neck, and beyond it the hidden reef that had taken more hulls than the town cared to list on its little white sign. The tide was pushing in hard, the water full of its own hurry. Juno put her hand on the wheel and felt the Ninth Hour answer. Her father's tug. Trim and ugly and alive.

She had been running this channel with him in the wheelhouse beside her since before she was tall enough to see over the console. He had taught her the reef the way other children learn their spelling: by heart, and under threat.

"Keep her in the throat," she said aloud, to herself, in his voice. "The reef is patient. You be patienter."

They were three-quarters through the channel when the radio spat static.

"This is Meridian Dawn, Meridian Dawn—" A pause. "Meridian Dawn to any vessel, we are hard aground on the Bellmouth reef, position—" The voice cut out, then returned, calm and flat as a doctor's. "We are on the reef. Requesting assistance. We are—" Another pause, too long, as if the next words cost something. "We are the freighter Meridian Dawn."

Teo was already on the ladder. "Meridian Dawn. That's the Halyard boat."

"It's a freighter on the reef is what it is."

"That's a freighter on the Bellmouth and the surge coming tonight and we're towing a barge of dredge muck." He looked at her. "Juno."

"Find the charts." She said it and then she didn't need them, because they were in the drawer under her hand where they had always been, and she was already pulling them out, already turning the barge's tow toward the east dock, already calculating in her head: the channel, the tide, the reef at slack, the light left in the day.

"Juno," Teo said quietly. "The barge."

"The dock'll take it. They'll take it floating and they'll take it free." She said it and heard how it sounded, four hundred dollars waved away like a fly. She reached for the radio and brought the east dock up instead of letting it lie. "East dock, East dock, this is Ninth Hour. I'm putting your spoil barge loose at the north buoy and I'm not bringing her in. There's a freighter aground on the Bellmouth. You want your barge, you send a man out for it, and you tell your office the four hundred's off my contract, not theirs to keep. I'll answer for it."

The dock came back after a silence, a tired man's voice. "You're leaving a loaded barge at a buoy?"

"I'm leaving her tied and floating and I'll answer for it. If she drags, I'll pay the drag. Log that too."

"Reach is going to hear about this."

"Reach is going to hear about a lot tonight." She hung the mic. Her hand wanted to shake and she did not let it.

Teo was watching her with his arms crossed and his head tipped. "There," he said. "Now it costs you something and I believe you. Say the next part right."

"The next part?"

"Why we're going. Not the bank. Say it because it's true."

She looked at the chart under her hand. The reef was a smudge of stipple at its center, unlit and unnamed, and the channel turned around it like a question.

"A freighter on that reef with the surge coming will break up and empty her guts into the strait," she said. "Whatever she's carrying goes into the water that runs into our estuary, and then into the wells, and then into every cup in Kestrel Reach. I want her off that rock before the salt line comes in. And if the court pays me for it, good, and if the court don't, I'll still sleep. That's the truth and there's no better one. Now stoke her."

"Then that's what you get." Teo went, taking the ladder in two steps.

She brought the mic up. First to the harbor. "Reach Harbor, Ninth Hour. We're taking the spoil barge off contract and running for the reef. Meridian Dawn's aground."

"God and his angels," Pell said. "Juno, there's a reef and a surge and you're one tug."

"I've got the tug. I've got the reef. I've got Teo. I'm claiming salvage before anyone else gets there and makes it a race. Reach Harbor, you hear me? I claim her. I want it on the log."

"I'll log it." A pause. "High-order salvage's a fool's wage on a night like this—"

"It's not night, Pell."

"It will be. Forty hours of it." And then, quieter: "If the surge meets that freighter on the reef, you'll be working in surf in the dark. You think about your father before you go out there. Not what he'd do. What he'd say if you came home wrong."

"He'd say I came home."

"That's what I'm afraid of. Reach Harbor out."

Then she clicked over to the freighter's channel. Static, then the flat voice, then nothing. She waited for the gap between voices.

"Meridian Dawn, Meridian Dawn, this is salvage tug Ninth Hour. We are inbound to your position from the south through the Bellmouth. We're one tug and one crew. Hold your people together. We're coming."

A long silence. Then the flat calm voice again, and something underneath it, something Juno could not name. Not fear, not quite. Something with weight to it.

"Understood, Ninth Hour. We'd about given up on being seen."

"We see you," Juno said, and hung the mic, and put the wheel over, and drove her father's tug out of the channel and into the fog with the bank letter folded against her ribs and the tide running under her, fast and cold and rising.
