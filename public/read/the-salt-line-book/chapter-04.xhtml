Halyard's Man

The torch went in at the seam. The paint bubbled, blistered, ran. Teo worked the bolt heads one at a time, and the smell threaded out of the wall, thin at first, then stronger as the paint let go.

Juno stood back with her hands on her belt and let him work. Past the beam of her torch the lower hold was dark and the steel was cold, and under the floor plates the water tipped and settled and made small sounds. Every so often the hull groaned and the plates rang under her boots.

“Bolt four,” Teo said, and moved to the next.

“Hear that?” He stopped, torch up.

She heard it. A deep engine note coming down the strait through the fog, low and steady, a good deal bigger than the Ninth Hour’s. It climbed through the steel and into her heels.

She climbed the shaft and crossed the deck at a walk. The note grew until the plates carried it up through her bones. Fog lay on the water in gray wool. The white water on the reef showed and hid, showed and hid. Then a deck light swung once across the fog and steadied on the freighter’s flank.

“That’s the Iron Gannet,” Teo said at her elbow, his torch still hot in his hand.

“I know what it is.”

“He’s run her in close. In this fog. He’ll be on the reef.”

“He won’t. He’s got the sounder and the old chart, same as me.” She watched the light swing and steady. The big tug’s hull came out of the gray behind it, wide and high and built for work, her paint a hard yellow, her stacks amidships, her name across the bow in letters a foot tall. Iron Gannet. Doran Rusk’s boat. A hundred and twenty tons of winch and money, and a master who never came anywhere just for a look.

The Gannet’s engines came down and she eased toward the Dawn’s stern. Her crew came out on deck in matching coveralls, and Rusk came out last among them, stood with his boots apart and his hands on his hips, and let his voice carry across the fog.

“Ahoy the freighter! Ahoy the Ninth Hour! Juno Halloway, is that you behind that tub?”

“Morning, Rusk,” Juno called back. “You’re a long way off your patch.”

“Same water as anybody.” He looked her boat up and down, then the freighter, and put his head back and laughed, a big warm roll of it. “Juno. Juno. Barely abeam of the reef and a freighter full of nothing and a crew of three.” He spread his hands. “Here’s a fine thing. Halyard Shipping’s been on the phone since the small hours. They’re worried about their ship. They want her off the reef and they want the sea out of her.” He tapped his chest. “So they called me. I’ve got the contract, I’ve got the gear, I’ve got six-inch wire and a winch like a church organ. What have you got?”

“A claim lodged and first light on the vessel.”

“First light.” Rusk sighed, the sigh of a man who had done this often and found it dull. “You know what first light’s worth? Nothing in the morning and less by afternoon. What’s worth anything is the award, and the award is what the court says it is, and the court listens to the man who saved the most ship. Not the woman who sat on it.” He walked along his own rail and looked across the gap at the Dawn, then at Juno, and grinned. “Here’s what I’ll do. Stand down. Take your line off her and take your boat back up the harbor. I’ll write you a cheque for two thousand for your trouble and put you down as assisting salvor.”

“Two thousand.”

“Token. I said it was token.” His voice went up a notch; he was enjoying himself. “It’s a token for a token claim. What have you got, Juno? A hull with nothing in it and a crew playing dumb. If I can’t move her by nightfall I’ll eat my hat, and if I can’t move her at all I’ll report your claim as interference and let the court sort out who was in the right of it.” He put a boot on the rail and rested his elbow on his knee. “You know what a fine does to a woman who hasn’t made her last four payments? It takes the boat.” His grin spread. “I hear things, captain. Harbor’s a small place. You’re four behind, aren’t you. Eleven thousand and change.”

Teo had gone very still beside her. The cold came up through Juno’s boots, and she kept her face the way she kept it when a tow rope parted.

She went up the freighter’s rope ladder and Rusk came across from the Gannet by his own, and they met on the Dawn’s afterdeck with the fog swirling and the reef grumbling under the plates.

“Here’s the way of it,” Rusk said. “You’re going to tell me about the abandoned-vessel rule, and I’m going to tell you that ship had a master aboard when you boarded her. No master present, you told Pell.” He held up a finger. “And then your three sailors let slip she’s aboard, and the harbor office log will say so, and I’ll have my lawyer pull it. So she wasn’t abandoned. She had a master and she had a crew, and a salvor who boards a crewed ship needs the master’s say, and I don’t hear a master saying anything.”

“The crew hid their master from me,” Juno said. “Three of them stood on that deck and lied. I boarded in the belief she was abandoned and I lodged my claim on it, and it stands.”

“It stands until it falls.” He turned and looked fore and aft along the deck. “Where is she, then? If she’s aboard, let’s have her out.”

“Ask her crew. They won’t say.”

“Then she’s not a master. She’s a stowaway.” He laughed, big and easy, and rubbed his hands. “Here’s the truth of it, and I’ll say it plain because I like you and always have. You’ve got a tug nine years past its pension and an engineer who fixes it with crab wire. I’ve got a winch that’ll pull a lighthouse off its rock. Halyard wants their ship off the reef. I’m the man can do it. You’re the woman who found her. Take the two thousand, sign the assistance paper, and go home.”

“I’ll sign nothing that puts your hook on this ship.”

“Then it’s the court.”

“It’s the court.”

Rusk looked at her a long moment, and his grin went thin and stayed, and he shook his head slowly as if at a stubborn child.

“Nightfall,” he said. “If that tub’s still on that reef at dark, I move her, and I move her the way I like, and I put the whole business in front of a judge, and you’ll be lucky to keep your license, never mind your boat.” He turned his back and walked to the ladder, then stopped and looked back. “And Juno. Whatever’s down her holds that’s got these people lying, you don’t want to be standing next to it when it comes up.”

She stood at the rail after he had gone, breathing, looking at the water, when a voice came out of the lee of the deckhouse, close and low, and it was not a voice she knew.

“Captain Halloway.”

Juno turned. The fog had curled in along the deck and filled the space between the structures so that she could see the shape of a woman in a heavy gray coat and a ship’s apron, and a length of jaw under a cap pulled low, and nothing else. The woman stood with the fog on her like weather on a rock.

“You’re her,” Juno said.

“I’m aboard.”

“Then step out and say it to me properly.”

“Not yet.” She said it without heat, the way a skipper reads a sounding she does not like. “You’ve a claim and a court and a town up the estuary. You’ll get my name and my word when I’ve thought it through.”

“Rusk will have your name off the harbor log by dark and he’ll put his line on your stern and take you out to sea and scuttle you, and your thinking will have a short run.”

“Yes,” the woman said. “It will.”

“If I pull you off the reef, will she come whole?”

The gray shape did not move. “She’s whole at the forefoot. She’ll not stay whole long.” A pause, and the fog moved through where her face should have been. “Tell your engineer this, and it is all you get from me today. What water she’s taken is forward of her collision bulkhead. The bulkhead holds her still. The bulkhead will fail before the bottom does.”

“Then why won’t you come aft and say the rest?”

“Because the rest gets people hurt. Mine first, and then the people up the estuary, who never asked for any of it.” The voice had a wrong note in it now, fear held absolutely still, and Juno heard the woman swallow. “You’ve a choice, captain. You didn’t want it. But you’ve got it, and it isn’t the one Rusk is offering.”

“What is it, then?”

“Not yet,” the woman said, and the fog took her, and there was only the sound of a boot on wet steel, going forward, and then no sound at all.

Juno went after her three steps and stopped. The foredeck was empty. The hatch of the chain locker stood open and dark and she was not going down there blind on a ship whose crew had already lied to her once. She stood with her fists at her sides and the fog moving round her and her own breath loud in her ears.

Teo had the panel open. One bolt to go. He turned it and pulled the door wide, and the smell came out of the hole in the wall all at once, sweet and sharp and chemical, and Juno put her sleeve to her mouth and took the torch from him and held it in.

Drums. Row on row of them, low and wide, stacked tight in a steel compartment no bigger than a larder. Every one painted the same dull blue and stenciled with a code in black block letters, and on the drum nearest her the paint had gone black and bubbled and crept down its side. A slick lay in the coaming under them, thin and bright, threaded with green and yellow. One drum, near the middle, had a wet mouth at its seam.

“Well,” Teo said, very quietly, behind her. “There’s your lime.”

“How many?”

“Forty, maybe more. Three tiers, four, that’s—” He stopped and did the sum and did not like it. “That’s a hold full of something that came aboard where it shouldn’t have and has been riding in this hull ever since.”

“Get a sample. Safe. In a jar, sealed, gloves on. We’ll need it for the court and there’s no telling when we’ll have this ship to ourselves again.” She held the torch steady on the leaking seam and the slow bright bead of it coming down. “And get me the ship’s papers. Manifest, deck log, everything in her desk. We’ve got one thing now and it isn’t the hull. It’s the record. Rusk wants this ship gone out to sea with all of it in her.”

Teo went at once. Juno stood in the doorway with the torch up and breathed through her sleeve.

She thought it through once, coldly, and did not think it again. Whatever was in a drum was not her cargo and not her salvage. The commission in Bellmouth cared about the ship, and the ship was a thing a court could value, and the drums were a thing a court would want explained before it valued anything. A salvor who opened a hold and found a crime in it did not become a smuggler by finding it. A salvor who opened a hold and found a crime in it and said nothing and hauled the ship out to deep water at another man’s asking, that salvor had chosen a side. Rusk had offered her two thousand dollars to be that salvor. He had been quick about it, too.

“Juno,” Teo called. “Juno. The papers.”

He came back along the passage with a crate in the crook of his arm, and a folder, and his face done up in a way she had not seen on him before. He set the crate down and opened the folder on top of it and turned the papers to the torchlight and put his finger on a line.

“Deck log,” he said. “She keeps a fair hand, does the mate. Course altered west, at master’s order, no consul. Ah, you’ve had that. But here’s the night before. Pilot declined at the seaward mark. Owner’s agent advised the master by radio to proceed without pilotage and the master noted the advice and her answer under it.” He put his finger on the answer.

Juno read it. She read it twice. It was four words and a signature: I will not.

“She’s a strange one,” Teo said.

“Get your jar,” Juno said. “Gloves on. Write the time on the lid.”

The tide was turning. It turned over the reef where the Dawn’s bow was hung up, and the freighter told her so. The plates moved under her soles with a heavy underwater groan. A long rifle-shot crack ran out along the starboard rail and she felt it in her teeth. When the swells came in, white water ran over the reef’s shoulder and broke against the freighter’s flank, and the water on her bottom drove in and drove out, and the whole ship lifted a few inches and sat back down.

“Every turn moves her an inch or two,” Teo said, capping his jar. “She’s not holed but she will be. The bottom’s pinned on the reef’s shoulder and the swell’s working her on it like a file on a nail. Give her another turn, maybe two, and the bow’ll start to open.”

“How long?”

“Before the surge, four hours. Before she starts hurting herself, maybe less.” He looked fore and aft along the deck. “Hull’s sound enough to tow. I’ve sounded her twice. Her bottom’s whole, her bulkheads are whole, and if the water was open and her stern was clear I could take the strain and she’d come off. But she’s pinned on that shoulder. We pull her bow off first and then swing her stern into the deep water. Two lines and a deal of patience.” He wiped his face with his wrist. “And it wants her master telling me what the hull did when it grounded. There’s things about this ship I’ll find out with a sounder and a week. Her master knows them this morning.”

“Her master,” Juno said, “will not come down out of the fog.”

“She spoke to you.”

“She gave me four hours of fog and a warning about her bulkhead and a thing about the town that didn’t make sense unless she meant it.” Juno put her hands on the rail and looked at the white water. “Teo. When the surge comes in over that reef, the salt goes up the estuary and into the lens, and it stays in the lens after the water goes down. That’s the thing that doesn’t wash out. Pell says the line could be at the mouth by tonight.”

“I know it.”

“Then she knew it too. She said, the people up the estuary who never asked for any of it.” Juno turned from the rail. “There’s a woman aboard this ship who steered for that reef, and I want to know why, and I’ll not have it said afterward that I stood in the fog and asked her once and gave up.”

She found the chain locker open and the ladder down, and she went down it because there was nothing else to do with a choice once you had it. The space was low and smelled of tar and old rope and the sea. The fog had followed her down the hatch in a gray thread and hung in the air above her like something unwilling to settle.

“I’m not armed,” Juno said into the dark. “I’m not the law, either, in case that matters to you. I’m the woman with the line and the tide.”

A silence. Then, from the far end, past the drum of the windlass and the coiled hawsers, the same low voice. “You came down without a light.”

“I’ve got my torch in my pocket and I’ve not used it because I’d rather you saw me plain when we talk.”

“That’s a courtesy,” the woman said. “I’ll remember it. Now hear me. I’ll not come aft and stand on my own quarterdeck and answer questions while Rusk’s winch is turning over out there. A thing said in the open gets heard by the wrong ears and a thing done in a court gets done slowly. My crew are frightened men. My deck’s got a wire on it before nightfall either way. So you’ll get my name when the ship’s off this rock or she opens and the sea answers for me.”

“Your name’s on the harbor log already,” Juno said. “Rusk will have it by supper.”

There was a small sound in the dark that might have been a laugh, and might have been the windlass shifting. “Then Rusk can have it. He wants a ship towed out past the shelf and scuttled where no court and no camera can see her, and he wants it done before anyone can walk down her holds with a jar. He’ll not get that from you. I know that much, and it’s the only thing I know today that does me any good.”

“There’s drums in your lower hold and one of them’s leaking.”

“I know it.”

“Did you put them there?”

“I brought this ship to this reef,” the woman said, and the fog hung, and she said no more.

“Why this reef? Why a channel with a town on it?”

“Because I have that much conscience,” the woman said, and Juno heard her stand up in the dark, and heard the small creak of a ship’s apron, and then the voice came from nearer and higher, from the ladder. “You’ll want to make your line fast fore and aft and take the strain slow, or you’ll tear her shoulder open on the rock and put what’s in her into the strait, and then the estuary, and then the wells. That’s your answer and it’s all the answer I’ve got in me. Now let me by.”

Juno stood aside in the dark. The woman went up the ladder, quick and sure, and was gone out of the hatch into the fog, and by the time Juno came up onto the deck there was no one at the bow but the empty windlass and the white water beyond it.

She stood at the bow a while alone. The reef worked. The fog thinned off toward the west and the strait lay open and flat and gray, and aft of her Rusk’s yellow tug sat steady with her winch turning over, turning over, patient as the sea.

The tide had begun to move against the freighter’s flank, and the sound of it under the bow was the sound of a clock being wound.
