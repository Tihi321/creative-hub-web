Voss's Confession

The wire came over the rail and slapped the deck. A monkey's fist at the end of half an inch of good braid, thrown hard by a man who had been told to throw it hard.

Juno caught it.

Her hands knew what they wanted. The axe sat in the rack by the wheelhouse door. One stroke would part the braid, and Rusk's man would stand at his rail with a wet end in his fist and nothing to pull on. Then Rusk would send another, and she would cut that one too, and they would spend the afternoon at the wrong end of a rope fight while the reef ground the Dawn's bottom plates to foil.

She threw the braid to Teo, who had come up the ladder with his gloves on.

"Make it fast to the bow bollard," she said. "Lead it back to my winch."

He turned the monkey's fist over in his hand. "You're taking it."

"I'm taking it and I'll give it back to him point first. Two lines beat one if I use his wire as a spring and a wild card at once. He wants to pull. I'll have him pull where I point."

"That's either the cleverest thing you've said all day or the last." He looped the line over his shoulder and went forward. "Bow bollard. Winch. Aye."

Juno went aft, and Voss went with her, the master's coat snapping at her knees.

"You mean to have his tow on your bow," Voss said.

"I mean to get this ship off this rock."

"He'll see what you're about before you're a cable into it."

"Then I'll have a cable on him."

At the wheelhouse door Juno stopped with her hand on the latch. The bridge lay long and dim behind salt-grayed glass: the chart table under its green cloth, the wheel with its brass spokes, the master's chair bolted down with the leather worn silver at the arms. Beyond the forward glass the reef ran white and the bar lay low and gray, and past the bar the flats where she meant to put this ship. A good bridge. A bridge a person could love. Voss stood in the doorway as if it belonged to someone else.

"Come in," Juno said. "Shut the door."

Voss came in and shut it. The reef hummed up through the deck plates. She stood at the glass with her hand flat against it, and she would not sit and she would not turn.

"Say your piece," Voss said. "You'll not have long."

"I'll have what I take." Juno pulled off her gloves and laid them on the chart table. She stayed on her feet too. "You told me the drums are not the ship and not the cargo and not salvage. You told me to leave them. Now say what you meant, and say it plain, before that wire is over my bow and I've no more time to ask."

Voss's hand stayed on the glass. The reef took the swell under them and the plates rang, and when she spoke her voice had lost its edges, gone plain and worn the way a rope goes at the last of its life.

"I ran her on the reef."

Juno had known it since the torn logbook. The half-entry in her coat had told her, and Fen had told her, and hearing it said in the master's own voice on the master's own bridge in the flat gray morning was another thing. She put her hand on the chart table and said nothing.

"On purpose." Voss's breath went out of her. "I brought her west at one in the morning with a north lamp behind me and no one to fetch it, and I put her on the shoulder of that reef the way a woman puts a wedge in a door she means to keep shut. She struck at ten minutes past the hour and she held. I'd chosen the shoulder. I'd had my boat out to sound the rock the week after I made up my mind, on the flood tide, and I knew how much water lay over it and how hard she'd take it."

"What week?"

"Three weeks before we sailed, in the autumn, after the last run was put to me. Not before. I want you to have that clean." Voss's jaw worked. "I'm a master mariner with a pilotage exemption for the north terminal and forty years off the land, captain. I don't need a pilot to find a rock in a marked channel. I took my own boat and I sounded it in daylight where no man could see me do it, and then I brought the ship down on it in the dark. There's no fog in this. There's me."

"And your mate?"

"In it with me. He's ashore now with the rest. He'll not speak." The nails of her hand went white on the glass. "The owners didn't know. The owners still don't. They think the reef took me in the fog. That's what makes it a salvage instead of a piracy."

"Why."

Voss turned from the glass then. Her eyes went to the open hatch and came back, and something in her face had come loose at the seam.

"Halyard Shipping." She said it the way a person says a name they've been carrying in the mouth too long. "Thirteen years I've had the north run and never lost a deckhand and never missed a tide. In the spring they put drums aboard for the lower run. Agricultural waste, they said. Licensed, manifested, above board. Then in the summer a man came to the ship with a rubber apron on him and put his hand on a drum like a man on a sick beast. He said keep them off the heat. He said don't let the seams go wet. He said if one weeps, put it over the side in deep water and don't log it." Her voice held level, neither rising nor falling. "There's no licensed waste a man says that about."

"So you looked."

"I took a sample the way you took one. My brother-in-law runs a bench in a lab in the city, and I put the jar in with my shirts and took it home and he ran it." She said the code plainly, as it was stencilled on the drums. "SX-9. A degreaser. The kind of thing a plant makes and can't get rid of at home, so it goes in a ship and the ship opens her sea-cocks out past the shelf and the deep has it and the manifest says agricultural lime and everybody sleeps. Two years of it, my brother-in-law said, going by the drum counts. I'd made four of the runs and not known."

"And you reported it."

"I did. I wrote it out and gave it to the coast office and it went to a man who gave it to a man, and in three weeks my brother-in-law was out of his job and had a letter from a lawyer, and Halyard's man came aboard and put his hand on my shoulder and said the company was pleased with me." Voss's mouth bent. "So I stopped reporting it. I'm not proud of the stopping. Then they told me the Dawn was for sale to a shell company in the islands once the last run was made, and would I take her out one more time before she went, and I knew what the last run was. Sixty drums in the lower hold and a manifest that said lime, and the plan was the shelf and the sea-cocks and no ship to come home at all. Or a ship come home clean. Either way, no drums."

"And you came west."

"I came west." The tremor was in her hand now and she let it show. "If the ship went aground with the drums in her, the drums would be found, and a drum found on a shore has to be dealt with where there are courts and cameras and no deep water to hide it. I wasn't sure I'd live to be hanged for it. I made my peace with that off the Skerry. I'm not sure yet that I have."

Juno took the half-entry out of her coat and held it up. Course altered west. At master's order, no consul.

"You wrote this."

"I wrote it and tore out the page before it, so one day someone would find it and know it wasn't the fog that did it. I meant it to be found by a tow with a nose for a lie." Voss's eyes came round to Juno's. "And it was."

"Say the rest. Why this coast? Why my estuary?"

"Because a narrow strait makes the news and a port wall doesn't. Because I knew the rock would hold her and a quay wouldn't, and because I'd have the summer soundings in my head, and because I'd never see my sister again and I'd made up my mind to that." Voss's voice went, for a moment, to almost nothing, and came back. "I chose your town for what it would cost other people. I'll not dress it. I knew that when I did it and I know it now, standing here."

Juno folded the paper and put it away. The Gannet's winch turned somewhere out beyond the glass, and the wire would be on the bow inside ten minutes, and she stood in the wheelhouse and made herself feel all of it: the drums weeping in the dark, the green film on the bilge, the sea carrying it up the estuary into the lens the town drank from. The prize she had come for and would not now get. She put that last away where she put things she could not afford yet.

"The drums don't go to sea," she said. "Whatever a court does to me, they don't go into the deep and they don't go up my estuary. I'm taking this ship south onto the west flats, where the mud will hold her and a court can come and take the drums off her in daylight. You'll take your wheel, or I'll take her off this rock with you tied to the rail. I'd sooner have your hands than your word."

"You'd do it."

"I'd do it."

"Then I'll take the wheel." Voss's hand came away from the glass. "And you'll have my word the bulkhead holds to the bar. There's no lying about a bulkhead. It holds or it doesn't and it doesn't care what I say to you."

"That's the first thing you've said I've trusted all morning."

The door opened and Rusk's man came in with his harness still on him and his hat in his hand, red in the face.

"Captain Rusk's compliments," he said. "He's aboard and at the bow, and he'll speak with the salvor. Now, he says."

"Then he'll speak with me."

Rusk stood forward with two of his crew and his hands behind his back, studying the reef the way a builder studies a bad foundation. The Gannet's braid lay on the bow where Teo had made it fast, and a Gannet man was bent over the monkey's fist with a spike in his hand. Rusk watched him and held his tongue until Juno came up the deck. Voss stayed at the break of the foredeck, and Rusk's eyes went to her and stayed a moment before they came back.

"You've got my line on your bow," he said, "and a freighter under you groaning like an old dog, and you've not moved her an inch. I've been patient."

"You've been ten minutes."

"I'll do what I came for. The company's man in the city's been on the phone since six. They want her off this reef and out past the shelf by morning, and I'm the man with the horses." Rusk put his arm out at the water beyond the bar. "It's the right thing and you know it. That ship's got something bad in her and bad wants deep water. We put her over on her side out past the shelf and she goes down in three hundred fathoms and whatever's in her stops being anybody's problem. That's how it's done. That's how it's always been done."

"No," Juno said.

Rusk turned his head. "No?"

"No. There'll be no tow on this ship out past the shelf. She doesn't go to the deep with the drums in her so the company can wash two years of dumping into a trench no court will send a diver into." Juno kept her voice flat. "I've got her papers under seal, a sample in a jar, and a master who'll say what's in the drums and how they came aboard and who wanted them gone. I'll not be the one who sails that evidence out to where the sea can hold it instead of a court."

Rusk laughed. It was a good laugh, warm and short, and it did not last. "A master. You've a crew of three won't say what day it is, and a master's not opened her mouth since you came over the rail. What court hears a broke tug with a bank letter in her drawer?"

"The sample doesn't miss payments," Juno said. "And the master will be at her own wheel inside the hour. You can hear it from her on the deck of her own ship if you like, and the harbormaster will hear it after you."

Rusk's face went through something and came out the far side set. He looked at Voss, and Voss looked back at him, steady, her eyes on his and staying there. Then he looked at the braid on the bow, and at the spike in his man's hand, and something crossed his face that was not anger. Reckoning.

"Then we've a difficulty," he said. "I've a contract with Halyard and a claim filed and I'm the man with the horses. You cut that line off my bow or I'll have my man cut it, and then I'll take her stern and tow her out from under you, and you'll be a line in my logbook."

"It's your line," Juno said. "There it lies. But if your man puts a spike to it, every soul on this deck sees him do it, and the harbormaster hears it from me before your winch turns. You want to cut my tow, cut it with your own hands, and you'll be the man who did it."

Rusk opened his mouth, and the bullhorn spoke across the water before he found the words.

"Ahoy the Dawn. Ahoy all."

Every head on the foredeck came round. The pilot boat was out of the Bellmouth channel with white at her stem and half the town's small boats strung out behind her, and Pell stood in her bow with the bullhorn at his mouth and his boots already wet.

"This is the harbormaster of Kestrel Reach. I'm coming aboard. I'll see the claim and the ship's papers and the lot of you on this deck together. There'll be no tow put on this vessel and no line cut on her until I've had my say. I'll be aboard in ten minutes."

Rusk stood at the bow with his hands at his sides and his jaw working. He looked at the town's boats coming on behind the pilot boat, and at the braid on the bow, and at Juno.

"Ten minutes," Juno said, and went to meet the pilot boat, and put her back to him.
