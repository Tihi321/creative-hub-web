Kestrel Reach

The next town meeting fell on the first Thursday in November. Pell tacked the notice to the door himself, and when anyone asked him what it was for he said it was a meeting, and that was all.

Juno read it on her way down to the wharf. Town meeting. Item before the council. All welcome. The wind had got under one corner and the paper flapped against the wood. She stood there until the cold worked into her fingers, then went aboard and set to work on the Ninth Hour's number one pump, which had seized in the night. She could not afford to fix it. She could not leave it be, either.

At six Teo came down the pier with two cups of coffee and stood in the engine-room door watching her, her arms buried to the elbow in the bilge.

"A spanner and a bad mood won't turn her," he said.

"I know."

"Then why are you down in there?"

"Because she's my boat," Juno said.

He set a cup on the rail for her and drank the other himself and let the rest go.

The meeting house held more people than it had for the charges. They stood along the walls and filled the doorway, and the cold came in behind them. The children were at the back again, and this time they made noise, and no one told them not to.

Juno took a seat in the third row, since nothing further back was free, and because Fen had saved it for her.

Fen had come up from Bellmouth on the morning bus. Her work coat lay across her knees, her gray brows were drawn down, her hands were folded, and she carried the courthouse smell that Juno knew of old: dust and paper and long waiting.

"You didn't have to come," Juno said.

"I wanted to see what a town does." Fen's mouth was flat. "All my life I've watched towns look the other way. So I came up on the bus."

Pell called the meeting to order. He stood at the front, opened his book, and looked past it at the benches, the way he always did.

"Two items," he said. "I'll take the small one first. It's mine, and I'll say it quick, because I've been saying it slow to myself for a month and I've got no better at it." He shut the book. "I'm giving up the harbormaster's post at the end of the month. My grandfather dug this town's first well. My father ran the launch. I've stood behind that desk thirty-one years, and I mean to go home and dig in my garden and watch somebody else do it."

A murmur ran along the benches. Not surprise, not quite. Softer than surprise. A town hearing a thing it had known was coming and had not wanted said aloud.

"Now," Pell said. "The other item."

He turned the page.

"A bank in Bellmouth holds a mortgage on the salvage tug Ninth Hour. Four payments are out, there's a date in December, and the bank has the right to seize her and sell her, and it will. The court's ruled, the prize is gone, and there's no man in this room who can pay eleven thousand four hundred dollars out of a fish catch." He raised his head. "But this is not a man in this room. This is a town."

He let it lie there.

"Your council has been in touch with the bank, and the bank has agreed a price. A bank will always agree a price when the price is cash and quick and the alternative is a court date and a boat nobody bids on. The council won't tell you how the price was struck. It won't tell you what the fish house and the boatyard and the man from the well have put in already, because every one of them asked me to keep quiet, and I'm a harbormaster and I keep a confidence." His hand lay flat on the book. "What the council asks is that the town make up the rest. Not as a gift to a captain. As a purchase, for the town. A harbor without a tug is a harbor that watches and waits, and this town has watched and waited long enough."

The boatyard man was on his feet before Pell had sat down. He said what he had said at the last meeting, in fewer words, and then he said the rest: that he had known Juno's father, that he had let a quarrel stand nine years past the point where a man should have let it stand, and that he was sorry for it, in front of the whole town. Then he sat down.

After him came the fish house women, one after another, reading out the numbers they kept in the tin. The schoolteacher brought the children's collection: eleven dollars and forty cents in a coffee can with the lid cut open. The man who ran the fuel dock offered to carry the Ninth Hour's diesel at cost for a year. A woman near the door, whose face Juno did not know, stood up and said she had filled a sandbag in the dark and never learned whose idea it had been until the paper printed it, and that she had three hundred dollars and a boy in her yard who could splice.

Then Gorse stood up.

He kept the chandlery at the head of the wharf, and he had the ledgers of half the boats in the harbor in his back room, and he was not a man to speak at meetings. He stood with his hat in his hands and did not look at Juno.

"I'll be the one," he said. "Somebody has to be." He turned the hat over. "I've got no quarrel with the captain. I'll say that first, and it's true. But I've got a mortgage of my own and a note at the same bank in Bellmouth, and a bank that forgives a debt for a town is a bank that knows a town will pay what a boat won't. I'm not saying no. I'm saying what it costs." He looked down the benches. "If we buy her, I want it in the books where the money came from and where it went. I want it read out at the meeting after. And I want the boat worked, not kept. A town tug that sits at the pier is a monument, and I can't sell chandlery to a monument."

A woman behind him said, "That's fair."

"It's fair," the boatyard man said, "and it's a hard word on a good night, but it's fair."

Pell wrote it in his book. "Read out at the meeting after," he said. "And the boat worked. Both in the minutes."

Gorse put his hat on. "Then I'm for it."

The vote was not close.

Pell wrote the count in his book and said the council would call for the money Monday and pay the bank Tuesday, and that the boat would belong to the town before the week was out. Then he looked straight down the benches at Juno. "Come to the office Thursday of next week and sign for her. We'd have you sign in front of everybody, only you'd hate it, and I've known you twenty years and I won't do that to you."

Juno was on her feet before she knew it.

"I'll sign," she said. Her voice came out rough, and she let it. "I don't know what to say, so here's the plain thing. I came into the Bellmouth that morning with a letter in my coat that said I was four payments from nothing. I put the letter away and did the work. The work was the work. I'd have done it knowing it cost me the boat and the prize and my name at the bank, because the drums don't care about any of that and neither does the water." She looked round at the wet coats, the children, the coffee can, at Teo at the back with his arms folded and one corner of his mouth turned up. "A good many of you stood on a wall in the dark and filled bags beside me and never asked what for. That's the thing. That's all of it. And Gorse is right. She'll work. I won't have her kept." She sat.

Fen's hand rested on her arm a moment and came away. The meeting moved on to other business, and for the rest of the evening nobody looked at her again. The town could not have done her a kinder turn.

Thursday she signed at the harbormaster's desk, Pell looking on, Teo by the door with his cap in his hands.

The bank's man had come up from Bellmouth, polite and quick, and never once mentioned the court ruling. When the last paper was blotted he shook her hand and said the bank wished her well, which Juno took to mean the bank had got what it wanted and would now like to forget her.

Pell put the papers in the safe, shut it, and rested his hand on the door a moment.

"There," he said. "She's a town boat now. A town boat wants an engineer, and I hear there's a good one going spare and asking for a thousand dollars of parts."

Teo crossed the room. He looked at the papers, then at Juno, and the wryness had left his face and something younger stood behind it, less guarded.

"I'll stay," he said. "On the boat. Where else would I go? A man can't walk off a thing he's held together this long. It'd fall down the minute his back was turned." He put his cap on. "But I'll want the slip for a fortnight and the parts, and it's understood I fix the engine and not the politics."

"Understood," Pell said.

"And the boat's name stays on her."

Juno answered that. First words out of her since she signed.

"Her name stays," she said. "The town can own her. Her name stays."

Teo nodded once and went out. The door swung behind him, and the bell above it rang and settled.

Voss came up from Bellmouth on the Friday, and Fen rode down to meet her at the bus and walked her along the wharf, because Fen had said on the telephone that a woman coming to Kestrel Reach to give away a bell ought not to come alone.

Ashore Voss looked smaller than she had on her own deck. A gray coat, and not a ship's coat. Boots, and not sea boots. Her hands were in her pockets, and she stood at the edge of the pier looking out at the flats where her ship lay, the wall of sandbags holding the channel behind her.

"They want me back at the courthouse Monday," she said. "The company's lawyers have found a way to ask the same question twice and call it new. It will take a while." She was quiet. "I'm told there's a chance I'll do time. My lawyer says everyone says that and it's usually a mood. This time I think maybe not."

"You ran a ship onto a rock on purpose," Juno said. "There's a law for that."

"There is."

The tide came in over the flats, lipped at the sandbags, and drew back. Out past the wall the Dawn lay down in the mud with her bow high, the way Juno had left her, and gulls walked her decks like they owned them.

"She's holding," Voss said, looking at her. "I went out with the surveyor Tuesday. The back's not broken. She's set in the mud to her bilges and she'll lie there till they cut her up. The lower hold's dry. He opened it and put his hand on the drums and they were dry as a church, and the wall behind her has not moved an inch." She said it like a report, flat, and then her voice changed. "The deep tanks held. They're what saved her. The weight fore and aft, the way you had me flood them. The surveyor says a ship that floods even and settles in soft mud will lie a long while. He's seen it before, on the north coast. She'll come to pieces slow."

"And the slick?" Juno said. "In the bilge, when we opened her. There was oil in the coaming and a drum weeping green."

"The boom's still round her, and it's stayed empty. Whatever wept stayed in the steel. The estuary's had nothing off her but rust." Voss's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "That's the whole of my account, captain. I've given it to the court and I'll give it again Monday. The logbook pages I tore out myself the night we came in. They had the rendezvous on them, the reef and the hour and the barge's name, and I tore them because I meant the finding to happen in daylight and in front of witnesses, not in some office where a company man could lose them. A stupid way to do a right thing. The court's said so. I've said so."

"And the barge?" Juno said.

"The Lysander. Her master's sworn at Bellmouth that he came alongside the Dawn in the dark off the reef and took nothing off her, and that he was paid to be there and paid to keep quiet, and he named the man who paid him and that man's named the next one up." Voss looked at the water. "Fen's given a statement too. She says it was the mate who dogged the engine-room door from inside the night of the grounding, so no one would go down and find the tanks being flooded early, and Barrow who hosed out number three because the drums had been staged in it and he wanted the deck clean. The ship unloaded nothing. There was nothing to unload. The lime manifest was paper and the drums were the whole of her cargo, and now the whole of her cargo is evidence in a shed at Bellmouth with a guard on it."

"I'm glad of that," Juno said. "I've wanted to know."

"I've a thing for you," Voss said. "Not a gift. I'm in no position to give gifts. It's a thing I'd like you to keep, because I don't want to see it go to a scrapyard, and because you're the one who knows what it was for."

She lifted it out of a canvas bag at her feet. The Dawn's bell, brass gone green with salt, the ship's name cut into it in a band of raised letters, a short piece of the lanyard still knotted at the crown.

"I took it off before they impounded her," Voss said. "They won't miss it. It's not evidence and it's worth nothing. It's a bell that rang on a ship."

Juno took it. Heavier than it looked. She turned it over, read the name upside down, then right way up.

"Why me?" she said. "I beached your ship. I flooded her. She'll never float again."

"You kept her drums out of the water," Voss said, "and you kept the town's wells fresh. And the bell was the thing I heard when they hauled her off the rock and I thought she'd go under us, and she didn't." Her eyes stayed steady on Juno's. "That's what was saved, Halloway. Not the ship. The bell is what's left of the morning, and I want it somewhere it will be rung. Ring it when a ship comes in safe. I'd like to think of that."

Juno held the bell a while, then tucked it under her arm.

"I'll ring it," she said. "It'll want a bracket."

"It's got a crown," Voss said. "You'll find a bracket."

She went back up the pier with Fen to catch the bus. Juno watched her go, a small gray figure threading between the fish crates and the bollards, then carried the bell along the wharf to the harbormaster's office and set it on Pell's desk.

Pell gave up the post at the end of November, and he did not go quietly. A man who has stood behind a desk thirty-one years does not go quietly. He goes with a list.

He handed Juno the list on her first morning: four pages in his own hand, telling her where the tide board hung, which well was the deep one, which gauge to read first after a surge, and who in the town could be got out of bed at three in the morning and who could not. On the last page, in different ink: The pilot boat wants her injector cleaned. Don't let the mechanic tell you it's the pump.

"That's it?" Juno said.

"That's the job," Pell said. "The rest you'll learn. You've been reading the channel forty years without being paid for it. Now you'll be paid, and they'll expect you to be right."

She took the post. She signed the book and hung her father's watch in the office where the clock had been, the clock having stopped the year before with nobody bothering about it, and she sat down behind the desk and looked at the water through the window. All her life she had wanted to be out on it. Now she would spend what was left of that life bringing other people in.

Then the pilot boat's engine failed to start, and she went down and cleaned the injector herself.

Her first morning with the launch came in the first week of December. Fog lay on the Bellmouth, the air was cold enough to make her eyes water, and a coaster called the Heron was bound in for the fish pier with a load of salt marsh hay and a master she had never met.

She went out in the launch and climbed aboard and stood in his wheelhouse with her hands in her coat. He gave her a look over his shoulder that said plainly what he thought of a harbor pilot sent out in a boat with cracked paint and a woman at the wheel of it.

Then she began to give him the channel, turn by turn, buoy by buoy, in the flat voice she kept for tugs and weather. He stopped looking at her. He started looking at the water, and by the narrows he was doing exactly what she said.

Past the Bellmouth reef there's a set to the current no chart marks true. A pilot learns it in his arms and in the sound the swell makes against the reef's shoulder. Juno had learned it years back, in a boat that now belonged to the town. She took the Heron down the channel with the reef a line of white in the fog off her starboard bow and the bar flats showing gray where the tide had left them, past the wall, past the beached Dawn with her bow high and her bell gone, into the estuary, and set her course for the fish pier.

Behind her the flood tide climbed the estuary mouth. She could see it in the water under the Heron's stern: the line where salt met fresh and fresh turned it back. Thin, and moving, and going out. The surge had broken on the Dawn a month ago, and the river had taken the salt and carried it down and away, and the line that had lain across the mouth all autumn was thinner every day and further out.

The wells were fresh. The school tested them Fridays and wrote it in the book, and the book sat on the shelf in the office beside the well gauges, and Pell had written the first page himself.

"Pilot," the Heron's master said. "Fish pier or south wharf?"

"South wharf," Juno said. "The fish house women are working this morning. You'll foul their lines on the pier."

He grinned at that, the first grin of the day, and eased her over. Juno put her hand on the rail of a ship she did not own and looked back up the channel at the salt line going out, and heard, faint across the flats, where somebody had found a bracket and hung it, the bell of the Meridian Dawn being rung for a ship come in safe.
