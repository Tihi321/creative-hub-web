The Drums

The lower hold was a steel box with no light in it but Teo's torch and the red eye of the emergency lamp, and the drums filled it wall to wall.

Juno counted from the door. Four high in the middle stack, three at the edges, each drum low and wide and heavy, the blue paint gone black and bubbled where the seams had sweated. The nearest stencil read SX-9, and under that a smaller code in a row of letters and numbers she couldn't make anything of. The deck plates under the drums were wet. Torchlight found a color in the wet, green in the yellow, and it ran in thin fingers down toward the bilge.

Teo had wire in his hand. He'd cut the last bolt two minutes back, the door had swung, and the two of them stood and looked in and let the smell come out at them. His torch beam shook. He brought it down and held it still.

"Give me the light on the middle one," Juno said.

She climbed the drum row and put her face close and read the code without touching it. The drum's mouth was wet. Not dripping. Weeping, slow, the way an old wound weeps. She put her ear near it: the sea working the hull, nothing else. She put her ear back and listened past the sea, and the drum was breathing, a faint ticking gasp at the seam, and what came out of it reached her nose. Sweet, the way a dead thing is sweet before it is anything else. Sharp, the way a cleaner is sharp. Wrong. Her eyes watered, the back of her throat closed on itself, and she came off the drums fast with her wrist over her mouth.

"Down," Teo said. "Down and back." He had the door half closed and his shoulder against it. "Get her shut."

"Leave it."

"Juno—"

"Leave it. That door's going to be open in an hour whether we want it or not, and I'd rather know what we're about." She got her breath. The hold was bigger than she'd thought, drums going back into the dark in row on row, and the smell had layers now that she stood in it, each layer taking her further from lime and further from a merchant's mess. She held the torch out at arm's length and walked the beam across the tiers. Some drums had been stowed with care, squared and chocked. Some had been flung in at the end by men in a hurry who didn't care where they landed. The care and the hurry in the same hold: that was the thing that stayed with her. "How many?"

He had his torch up and his collar over his mouth, and he counted low, moving the light across the tiers. "Forty. Forty-two. There's more behind the stack. I hear them when the ship moves. Call it sixty." He lowered the light. "And the one I tapped's not the only one weeping. Three I can see from here have the paint gone soft at the seam. The bilge is a film, and the film is going off the plates with the water. Her pumps lift the bilge, Juno. Ours and hers both. You know where a bilge pump puts it."

"Over the side."

"Over the side."

She climbed down and stood in the wet with him. Film on the water, drums in their tiers, the dead engine room door beyond, dogged from the inside of the frame. The film moved when the ship moved, a slow bright crawl that went out and came back and never thinned.

"Get me a sample off that near drum," she said. "Sealed. Not a drop on you."

"Aye." He was already reaching for his crate. Then he stopped. "Juno. Before I do. You want to know what it is before you take a sample, or take the sample and let a lab tell you?"

"I want to know what it is in time to do something about it. Take the sample."

He did. Long gloves, the jar, a glass tube held to the weeping seam. The drum gave up a thin brown stream and he had it capped and taped in his crate inside a minute and a half. When he stood, the gray was on him around the mouth. He wrote on the tape with a carpenter's pencil, the date and the drum number and the hold, and his hand wasn't steady, and he read what he'd written before he put the lid down.

"It's not going in the estuary," Juno said. "Not while I have her."

"It's already in the estuary if her bilges lift before we do." He wiped his hands on the waste rag, as if the rag could take off what he'd seen. "She opens at the shoulder when the swell comes and the whole hold's in the water. I can't pump that hold dry, Juno. I could pump it down if the breach was patched and the patch held and I had more hours than I have and a pump that isn't old and one more man who knows the trade. I've got none of those."

"Then we don't let the hold go in the water. We get her off the rock and beach her and keep her in one piece on the mud."

"With the drums in her."

"With the drums in her."

Teo looked at the drums. The hull groaned, the plates rang under their boots, and one drum deep in the stack answered with a faint tipping sound, as though it had shifted.

"Right," he said. "Then we need her master to want it, and I don't care what she's hiding. We're out of time for it."

Juno looked back once at the open panel before she took the ladder. The torch beam found the blue drums, the green film, the weeping seams, and the whole of it looked like something that had waited years to be found and was not glad of it. Then she climbed, and she kept her eyes on the ladder.

Voss came out of the bow with the groaning. She stood at the break of the foredeck, coat buttoned to the throat, hands at her sides, and she watched Juno come up the ladder and she waited, which was more than she'd done all morning. The wind had come round over the bow, and it carried a little of the smell up out of the open hatch and laid it along the deck. Voss's nose moved at it. Her face didn't.

Juno didn't stop at the ladder. She crossed the deck and stopped a pace off and kept her voice level.

"Sixty drums," she said. "Half of them weeping. The stencil says SX-9. Your lower hold is a film of it and your bilge is lifting it and putting it over the rail, and I've got a sample in a jar and a mind to put it in the hands of a court. Now you'll tell me what I've got in my hold, or I'll take it off you in front of your crew and a harbormaster and whoever else is listening."

"It's not your hold," Voss said.

"It's my salvage."

"Then salvage the ship and leave what's not the ship alone."

"I can't leave it alone. It's in her and it's wet and it's going up-channel if she tears open, and you knew that when I found the panel and you knew it when you told me not to open it." Juno put her hands on her belt. "I've put up with a lot since dawn. I've been offered money to sink my own claim, and given until nightfall by a man in the biggest tug on this coast, and told three lies by three of your people, and I'll take all of that because it's the trade. But I'll not take a master who stands at her own rail and watches her ship poison a town's water and won't say why. So say it. What are the drums? Where were they going? Who put them in this ship?"

Voss's eyes went to the open hatch. It was the first thing all morning she had looked at directly, and she looked at it the way a person looks at a wound they have been told not to touch. Her mouth opened. Juno saw the shape of what was coming, and it was not a refusal. Voss's jaw set as if against a pain, and she said, "They're not lime, they're—" and then the words stopped in her, and something behind her face closed like a fist, and she shook her head once and looked away.

"They're not lime," Juno said quietly. "Go on from there."

"I'll not say." Voss's voice was thin now underneath the flat. "I'll not say it to you and not to the court and not to that man behind us. I'll not say it and be the one who said it."

"You'll not say," Juno said, and the anger went out of her voice and left the cold.

"I'll tell you this and nothing more." Voss turned her head. "The drums are not the ship and they're not the cargo and they're not salvage, and if you take my ship off this reef you take the drums with her and you'll answer for that to people who'll not ask your name first. So leave them. When the sea takes her, it takes what's in her, and that's a better end than yours would be."

"Better for who?"

Voss gave her nothing.

"For who, captain?"

The Iron Gannet's winch started up across the water. The wire hung slack off the Dawn's stern, the big tug moved in the current, and her stack blew black smoke across the gray. Then the radio in the wheelhouse cracked and Doran Rusk's voice came out of it, flat and cheerful and loud, and the deckhands on the Gannet's foredeck began to run.

"Salvage tug Ninth Hour, this is the Iron Gannet. I've filed my claim with the court at Bellmouth and my line's going on this wreck's stern in ten minutes. You'll want to have your gear clear and your people clear, over."

Juno walked to the wheelhouse and picked up the handset. Her gloves were wet from the deck rail; she wiped them once on her thigh before she keyed it.

"Iron Gannet, Ninth Hour. You'll keep your line off this ship. I'm her salvor of record and I've been aboard since dawn and you know it."

"You were her salvor of record and you're welcome to it in a courtroom in a year," Rusk said. "In a courtroom in a year it'll not matter which of us had the line on her when the water came. What'll matter is where she was when the water came. I'm putting her out to sea where the bad in her can sink deep and quiet and no one gets hurt but the fish." The winch began to turn again. "You've got a town up that estuary, captain. I heard about your four payments and I heard about the surge. Now I'm offering you the courtesy I'd want myself. Ten minutes. Get your people clear. Iron Gannet out."

The set went dead. Juno stood with it in her hand and looked out the wheelhouse glass at the Dawn's stern, where the Gannet's men were a scatter of small figures with a messenger line running out from her rail to the water, and the wire coming down. Every one of them knew his job and was doing it without hurry, and that was worse than if they'd run. A man in a hurry makes mistakes. Rusk's men did not look like men who made mistakes.

"Teo," she said, into the companionway. "Cat."

"Here." He came up the ladder with the sample crate under his arm. "I heard him."

"He's going to put that wire on her stern and haul her off while I'm still aboard her and her master won't say one word. I can cut his line, or I can be quicker than he is, and I've not got the horses to out-pull the Gannet." She put the handset down. "So I'm going to do neither. I'm going to put my line on her bow and take her south, into the estuary, and beach her on the flats by the west bar where there's mud to hold her and shallow enough the sea can't get over her. He can tow her stern off to sea all he likes once she's on the mud."

Teo looked at her. The plates rang under them, the reef groaned, the water ran white past the glass.

"The south channel's narrow," he said. "The bar's shallow. If she touches going over she'll go sideways and the drums go in."

"Then we don't touch."

"And if he puts his wire on her before you've got her head round? Two lines on one wreck and neither of us gets her where we want."

"Then I'll have a word with him." Juno went to the glass and looked out. The Gannet's messenger line was across the water and a man in a harness was going down the Gannet's side toward the stern of the Dawn. "Teo. Seal the lower hold. Not with the dogs. With the board in the partition, and set the portable pump with the discharge led back into her own deep tanks and not over the side. If she takes water on the way round, I'd rather it go in her than in the estuary."

The partition was a steel bulkhead plate hung on two hinges in the lower hold, and it was not a fit. Teo had seen that when he took the sample. The plate had been cut down from a hatch cover years ago and hung in a hurry, and where the tank line ran through there was a gap the width of two fingers, and the gap went all the way through.

"It's barred across the front fast enough," he said. "But the partition's got a gap where the tank line goes through. Two fingers. Water'll find it before a cat finds a saucer."

"Then block the gap."

"With what?"

"With whatever holds water and isn't us." She pulled on her gloves. "Move, engineer."

He moved. Juno went back out on deck and crossed to where Barrow stood at the foredeck rail with Fen and the boy behind him and the sea running white on the reef beyond. The bosun had watched the whole of it, Juno saw, the leaning in at the rail and the refusal and the radio, and his hands hung at his sides now and there was no calm left in him.

"You heard what's coming," she said.

"I heard."

"We're taking her south into the mud before the Gannet puts a line on her, and I'll not do it with three idle hands on this deck. Your engineer says she'll take water at the shoulder going over the bar and we've a hold full of drums that'll go up-channel if she founders. So you'll do what you were hired for, bosun. You'll take your two and get the collision mat ready to go over that shoulder and you'll work it as she moves. I'm not asking you to lie for your master or tell me hers. I'm asking you to keep your ship off the bottom."

Barrow looked at the Gannet's wire coming down and then at the drums' hatch and then at Juno. He was afraid, and she had known that since the forepeak, and now his fear had a use in it.

"Aye," he said. "Fen, the mat's in the forepeak locker with the lashing. Boy, you'll carry and you'll not speak." He stopped at the ladder and turned. "Captain. If she goes over quick, the mat's no good. It wants time."

"Then I'll give you time. Hold her head to the swell and take it slow."

He went below. Juno went back into the wheelhouse and stood at the glass and looked at the reef, and she called up her own boat on the radio, one deck below her and made fast fore and aft, and Teo answered from her.

"Cast off the stern line and keep the bow on," she said. "I'm taking her off myself and I'll want you where you can put your shoulder to her if she sticks."

"You'll take the strain on one line and the two of us no better than we are?"

"I'll take it slow. Ready when you are."

The horn answered. She felt the line come taut and the old diesel muttering under her feet, and the Dawn under her lifted on the swell and settled and lifted, and the reef showed white the length of her and hid again.

Then Voss came up the ladder and stood in the wheelhouse door.

She did not ask leave. She crossed to the wheel and put her hands on the brass spokes where they had worn silver, and she stood with her back to Juno and her eyes on the white water forward.

"I'll take her," Voss said. "It's my ship and I know her bottom. You don't."

"You'll not turn her on me."

"I'll take her into the estuary and over the west bar and I'll give you every foot of her, and when she's on the mud you'll have the drums and the court and the lot of it." Voss's hands closed on the spokes. "I'll give you my word the bulkhead holds to the bar. There's no lying about a bulkhead. It holds or it doesn't, and it doesn't care what I say to you."

Juno looked at the back of her, at the salt on the coat and the hands on the wheel and the one thing she had said all morning that had the truth in it. The crack had come and closed again, but she knew now where the seam was.

"Then take her," Juno said. "Slow. Hold her head to the swell and let the bosun work."

The Dawn came off the reef the way an old animal comes up out of a wallow, in no hurry and with a long complaint of her plates. The line drew and the old diesel took the weight and the white water went out from her bow in a slow ring, and she floated, and she answered her wheel.

Juno took the handset and keyed it. "Iron Gannet, Ninth Hour. She's off the rock and she's under my tow and she's going into the estuary to be beached on the west flats. You'll keep your wire off her."

There was a pause. The winch kept turning. Then Rusk's voice, without the cheer: "You've not heard the last of this, captain."

"Neither have you," Juno said, and put the set down, and watched the estuary mouth open ahead of them, gray water and the long low bar and the town's roofs beyond it, and the Dawn's bow come round to the channel slow as a held breath.
