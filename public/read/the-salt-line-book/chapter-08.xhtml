The Tow

Pell went back down the pilot boat's ladder, and the small boats followed him out, and by eight the estuary mouth had nothing left in it but the two ships and the gulls.

Juno did not sleep. She stood at the wheel and watched the tide make over the bar and climb the flats until it lay against the Dawn's side and began, in its patient way, to lift. By noon the freighter was leaning. The flood had taken her off her belly and rolled her a few degrees to starboard and ground her bilge into the mud, and she hung there half afloat and half not. The seam at the starboard bow stood clear of the water still. It would not stay clear. The next flood would take it.

Teo came over the gap with his crate of canvas and chains and laid it out on the Ninth Hour's deck. He had two hundred feet of good line, and he put one end on the Dawn's port rail and walked the rest round the bow and down the starboard side, and Barrow hauled it under the forepeak from the water, ducking his head under the spring of the hull, and payed it up until the line came up out of the sea from under her keel and lay dripping across the rail.

"That's the messenger," Teo said. "We bend the mat to it and walk her round. The softner goes on first, against the iron. I've seen a mat chafe through in ten minutes on a rough plate and then you've a rag and no mat."

"Walk me through it."

"Two lines bent to the mat, one from the stern, one from the bow. The messenger goes under her and we haul it in on the starboard bow until the mat comes down over the seam and lies flat. Then the chains. The mat's weighted along the bottom edge, and the chain's what holds it down and keeps the water from getting under it and peeling it off. If the chain goes slack she billows and goes." He knelt and showed her where the canvas was sewn. "She'll complain when the water first presses, but she'll hold. She was built to hold. That's the whole of her."

"And if she wants a man in the water?"

"She doesn't. Not if the chain's rigged right." Teo looked at the water coming across the bar. "She wants the ship still. The sea's got to leave her alone for the space of a minute while I draw it down."

"Then we'll make her still."

Barrow came over the rail with Fen behind him, and Rusk's boy came after, because Rusk had sent him with a bag of shackles as a kind of apology and a kind of watching. Juno put them to work. Barrow took the bow line, Fen the stern, and the boy hauled the messenger in and payed it out by turns while Teo worked the mat's corners and the chains and swore at nobody. It took an hour. When it was rigged the mat hung slack along the Dawn's starboard side under the waterline, the chains running along the bottom edge like the hem of a curtain, and Teo straightened with his hands on his back and looked at what he had made.

"Now we put her down over the seam," he said. "The ebb's in two hours and there's the last of it on the bar and the flats. Take her down while the water's on the flats and touch her and hold her there and I'll draw the mat down. She'll not roll if she's on her keel in the mud."

"And if she opens on the way?"

"She won't open on the way. She's tight as a drum at the seam until the sea stands up on her, and the portable pump'll do for the water that's in her. This is the window. The window's now."

Juno went aboard the Dawn and stood in the wheelhouse with Voss over the chart.

"Here," Voss said, and put her finger on a place where the flats ran out in a long finger of sand with the deep water close alongside. "The west bar. There's water enough over it at the top of the tide to float her in and no more, and the mud's soft and it's never been dug. She'll go in up to her bilge and lie quiet. I've come over that flat in the Dawn's boat and I know the bottom."

"You'll take her yourself."

"I'll take her myself. There's no hiding in it now." Voss's hands went to the wheel. "And you'd best take the tug on her bow and hold her head into the channel. I'll not get her round the bar on my own engine."

So they took her off the reef.

The Dawn came off the rock at two in the afternoon with her own screw turning slow and the Ninth Hour's line on her bow. Juno stood at the tug's wheel with her hand on the throttle and felt the freighter come up behind her like a thing that had been waiting in a ditch and was at last let go, and the two ships went down the Bellmouth channel with the flats on the western side and the bar ahead. Voss held her head and Juno held her pace, and the Dawn came down to the west bar, and the flats ran out to meet her, gray and green and soft, and the freighter touched.

She touched and went on and lay down in the flats very slowly, the way a person lies down at the end of a long day. The mud took her to her bilge and held her, and her bow was up and her stern was down and the water in her came forward and lay in the forepeak and stayed there.

Teo went over her side with the mat and the messenger and the chains and drew it down over the seam in the still water while the mud held her. It went down and lay flat along the length of her and the chains held at the bottom edge and the canvas took the pressure of what water was left and bellied a little against the iron and settled, and Teo put his hand flat on it and held it there a moment and said nothing.

The tide did not come back that day. When it did, they stood ready.

The night was the long part, and it went by in the sound of water.

Teo had the pump on the Dawn's foredeck with its suction in the forepeak and its discharge led aft to the deep tanks, and the tanks filled through the small hours and the water in the forepeak went down, and the ship sat down in the mud by her stern to the tune of it. Juno held the Ninth Hour on her quarter with springs out fore and aft and watched the gauges and the tide and the sky, and Barrow walked the rail with a lantern and Davy watched the lower hold and Fen sat with Voss in the wheelhouse with the radio on.

The surge came up the estuary mouth at four, ahead of the light. Not a wave. The whole sea coming up, a gray lifting of water over the bar and up the channel, slow and enormous, and it took the Dawn's side and covered her foredeck and stood against the mat and stopped.

The mat held. It held under the seam and along the chains, and the softner took the iron, and the canvas did not part. The forepeak took water to the lower step and no higher, and the bulkhead behind it took the head of it and groaned and held, and Teo's pump went on lifting the water and dropping it into the deep tanks, and the ship kept her bow up like a horse that had been frightened and had come back.

At half past four the deep tanks were full.

Teo came to the Ninth Hour's rail and told her, flat, that the tanks would take no more and the discharge had to go somewhere and the forepeak was still taking water.

"Over the side," Juno said.

"That's estuary water out of the forepeak, not out of the hold," Teo said. "I want that plain. It goes into the channel behind us and it's the same water that came in on the flood, and the drums are above the bilge and the bilge stays in the bilge. There'll be nothing of the drums pumped out of her tonight. I've had her sumps dry all night and I'll have them dry at morning."

"Over the side. And you'll have it in your log what it was."

He shifted the discharge over the rail and the forepeak water went out into the channel in a gray arc, and the weight went out of her bow with it. The Dawn settled a little deeper and her bow came up and the water at her seam lay on the flattened canvas and found no way in.

The Gannet was not there. Rusk had gone out on the ebb the night before and had not come back, and his wire had been cut off the Dawn's foredeck and coiled and sent back with the town's boats, and there was a place on the rail where his grapnel had bitten the iron.

The dawn came up gray and the surge went out with it.

By full light the water was down the channel and the flats stood out of the sea, and the Dawn lay in the mud with her flooded tanks and her bow up and the mat on her side like a bandage. The lower hold was dry and the drums stood in it above a bilge that had not risen, and Teo came up out of the engine space and sat down on the hatch coaming with his hands black to the elbow and let his head hang.

Juno walked the flats alone.

She walked out along the north lip of the estuary mouth where the water went over from the deep into the flats and down through the sand into the lens, and the flats were wet and gray and the gulls stood on them, and there was a mark on the sand like a lip where the salt had come up to and stopped. It ran in a long slow curve from the channel to the north well and it was low and brown, and it did not go past the well.

She had the crew draw samples. The man from the town well came out in the flat-bottomed launch with his gauges and took three more from the north well and the south well and the one by the school, and he read them on the Dawn's deck with the wind trying to take his papers, and he was quiet a while.

"Fresh," he said. "All three. Not a grain of salt that wasn't in them before the surge." He shook his head. "I've twenty years on that water and I'd have bet the other way. She held."

"She held in a line," Juno said. "There's a mark out there. It comes to the well and stops."

He wrote it in his book, the time and the mark and the wind and the tide and every gauge, and gave her the carbon to keep.

Rusk came back at nine.

He brought the Gannet in on the flood and came aboard the Ninth Hour in his good coat with his papers under his arm and asked for the harbormaster. Juno sent Barrow for Pell, and the three of them stood in the wheelhouse out of the wind. Rusk's boat lay off in the flats with his men in it and no wire in the water.

"I'm here to put a thing in the log," he said. "I didn't sleep and I've thought about it and it wants saying in front of the harbormaster with the time on it."

Pell opened his book on the chart table and set his pen at the top of the page. "Go on."

"I, Doran Rusk, master of the Iron Gannet, put my grapnel in the Meridian Dawn's foredeck at the estuary mouth yesterday morning after the harbormaster's instruction against a tow and after the salvor had a lawful line on her bow." He said it steadily, the way a man reads out a thing he has written and learned. "The line was within my contract and outside my orders. It was put on because the company wanted the ship out past the shelf and because I wanted the company's money. If the mat had parted and the drums had gone into the estuary I would have been the man who did it. I'm putting it down so that no one has to prove it."

Pell wrote. "Anything more?"

Rusk looked at the flats and the mark down at the well. "I stopped doing what the company wanted when the drums got to be a thing a court could read. I'll not pretend it was for the water. The water being fresh means the town's off my back and the file can be shut and the company's lawyers have nothing to hang on me." He said it without shame and without pride. "The one thing is true and the other is true and I'll have them both in the book."

Pell wrote it. "And the drums?"

"The company will hear from the court about them. I'll say what I saw on that deck and what was in that hold and who wanted it in deep water, and not a word past what I saw. I'm not a witness for your side. I'm a witness for the court."

"That's what a witness is," Pell said. "Whatever a man likes to call it." He closed the book. "You'll be served with the salvor's claim and the court'll hear from you, and you'll not leave this coast without my leave. That's all."

Rusk put out his hand and Pell took it. Short, and neither of them leaned on it. Then Rusk went down to his boat, and the Gannet's stack went black and she went out the estuary mouth on the ebb and away north with her claim withdrawn and her complaint withdrawn and her master's statement in the log.

"Don't trust it," Juno said.

"I don't," Pell said. "But it's in the book in his hand and the time on it, and the court will read it in his hand and not mine." He stood at the rail and looked at the flats and the mark on them and the Dawn in the mud. "Now there's the matter of the ship. The court'll want her and the drums'll have to be lifted off her in the light of day by men in paper suits, and there's not a thing of that I can do but say so."

"And me?"

"You beached a ship you saved off a reef to keep her cargo out of my water. I know what the law calls that and I'll not dress it up. The court'll say no salvage is due off a ship her salvor put on the mud. There'll be lawyers and it'll cost you the fuel and the men's wages besides." He looked at her. "You'll be at the town meeting on Thursday."

"Will it change what the law says?"

"No. But the town's got a thing to say and it's not the court, and it wants saying at the same time. That's a different ledger and it gets kept in a different book." He turned to go. "Get your engineer to look at your pumps. And remember. You beached that ship in front of the whole town and the whole town saw you do it. Whatever the lawyers do to you, that's the thing that gets remembered."

Juno went back to her own deck.

The Ninth Hour lay on the Dawn's quarter with her fenders crushed and her paint scored and the cold hammering of Teo's tools coming up out of the engine-room hatch. She had a list to port where the Dawn had come against her, and she was the only thing Juno had in the world besides her father's name and a bank letter.

She went down the ladder and looked at the pump apart on the deck plates and Teo's hands black to the elbow.

"How bad?"

"Cracked head. Burnt winding on the big pump. Bent coupling. She'll want a slip and a fortnight and a thousand dollars of parts and she'll never be the boat she was." He kept his eyes on the pump. "And the court's not going to pay us."

"No."

"Do you know it in your bones?" He set the impeller down. "Because the sum's the same sum and it doesn't care about the water at any well. What the bank wants is what the bank wanted. We're further behind than we were on Monday and the boat's in worse shape and there's no award in it. That's not a hard sum, Juno."

"Then we'll do it the way we do everything else."

"Badly and last."

"Badly and last." She put her hand on the rail. "Tell me what it'll take to have her turning."

"Four days if the parts come up from Bellmouth and the coupling can be straightened instead of made. A thousand if I'm lucky. Two if I'm not."

"Then order the parts and I'll find the thousand."

Teo was quiet. Then he laughed, and the laugh had no joy in it and some warmth, and he shook his head and went back to the pump. "Get out of my engine room. Go and be the salvor for a while. Go and write it down for Pell."

Juno went up. The wind had gone round to the northeast and the tide was making and the flats were coming back under the water, gray and slow, and the Dawn lay in the middle of them with the mat on her side and the drums dry in her and the town's mark down at the well.

She got out her log and her pencil and sat on the hatch cover in the cold and wrote it down from the beginning: the mayday, the reef, the empty holds, the panel and the drums, the mat and the tow and the grapnel at the mouth and the going onto the flats, the flooding, the mark on the sand. It took her four pages and the afternoon. Barrow brought her a cup of tea and stood with her while she finished and said nothing.

At dusk the salt line lay at the mouth of Kestrel Reach, low and brown and stopped, the way a wave stops on a beach and goes back, and the water beyond it was gray and rising and clean, and the town's wells stood behind it. Juno sat with her log in her lap and her father's name on the boat under her and eleven thousand four hundred odd dollars sitting in the bank's drawer, and let it sit. It was one thing among the others now, in a day that had a mark on the flats in it and a man's own words with a date on them, and it did not loom.

Teo came up on deck at last with a spanner and a smear of oil across his forehead and stood at the rail and looked at the wreck in the flats.

"We'll want hands," he said. "In the morning. To take the drums off her in the light."

"Pell's got the court for that and the court's got the men in the paper suits." Juno got up and put the log under her arm. "We'll want hands to pump her and watch her and keep the mat down until they come. And we'll want the town. Put it on the radio at first light and I'll ask Pell."

"That's a thing you're good at," Teo said. "Asking the town."

"It's a thing that's been done to me," Juno said. "There's a difference. I'm learning it."

She went below and lay down in her bunk in her wet coat, and the Ninth Hour rocked under her with the tide coming in over the flats, and the pump hammered and the water went on going out of the Dawn's forepeak and over the rail, and she slept.
