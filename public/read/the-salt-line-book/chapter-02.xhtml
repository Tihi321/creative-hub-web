The Reef

The reef showed itself first as a change in the water.

Juno had run the Bellmouth her whole life. She knew that reef the way a blind man knows his own stairs, but the fog lay thick and the tide ran hard and the channel gave her nothing to see. Then the water ahead went wrong. The long gray swells that came through the strait flattened and broke against something that was not there, a line of white teeth showing and hiding, and she eased the throttle and took the Ninth Hour down to bare steerage and crept up on it, one hand on the wheel, one on the engine-room bell, listening to the old diesel turn over and the water break and the gulls complain somewhere above the fog.

The Meridian Dawn lay across the reef like something dropped.

Not bow-on, the way a ship grounds when weather drives her. The freighter had come in sidelong. Her bow twisted hard to port and lifted, the whole forward third of her hull hung up on the reef's shoulder, her stern swinging free in the deep water. She was a long flat-hulled thing, as Teo had said, paint flaking off her bows in pale scabs, rust weeping down her flank in long red tears. Her deck canted to starboard. Not much. Enough.

"She's not holed bad," Teo said, beside her at the rail. "Look at the waterline. No slick, no bubbles. She's sitting on it like a drunk on a curb."

"Drunks roll off curbs."

"This one might." He spit over the side and watched it go, his eyes narrow against the fog. "She's not breaking up yet. The surge comes in and works her, though, and it'll open her like a can. I want to get aboard and sound her before the tide turns."

"Find her people first." Juno brought the tug in along the freighter's starboard side, wide of the reef, and Teo took the fenders off the rack himself, swinging them over by their lines and letting them knock down the flank of the tug one by one. The two hulls came together with a soft heavy knock. The fenders ground and squealed, and the Ninth Hour nosed up against the freighter's flank like a small dog against a horse. The old tug shuddered all along her length. Juno felt it up through the wheel and into her wrists.

"Meridian Dawn," she called up. "Ninth Hour alongside. Put a ladder over."

The freighter's deck rail stood three meters above them, and no face showed over it. The fog moved across her superstructure and her derricks. Somewhere aft, a line slapped a mast. The wind gave a low note through the stays and then gave nothing.

"Say again," Juno shouted. "Freighter Meridian Dawn, this is the tug alongside. Show me a face."

The fog swallowed her voice. The two ships moved against the fenders. Teo stood looking up, his head tipped back, the rag still hanging from his hand.

"Juno," he said. "The bridge windows are dark."

She saw it. The bridge was a squat white box forward of amidships, and its windows held no light, and the accommodation block behind it held none either, and the lifeboat davits overhead stood out empty against the gray, swung out over the water, their falls hanging loose and turning in the wind.

"Boats are swung out and empty," she said. "Both of them."

"Deck's bare. Bridge is dark." Teo picked up the boathook and jammed it over the freighter's rail, testing. The hook rang on the steel and held. "Could be they got hurt and they're below. Could be they're down there in the dark with a radio that's died. I don't know what it could be, and I don't like that I don't know."

"Then we'll go and find out." Juno hauled herself up the boathook line, found the rail with her boot, and came over onto the freighter's deck. The steel under her feet was wet and cold and it moved under her in a way a hull should not move, a slow grind of iron against rock that came up through the soles and into her teeth. The deck canted to starboard and her body wanted to lean with it and her sea-legs argued. She crouched, and waited, and listened.

Then she heard it.

Not machinery. Not water. A small sound, muffled, from forward, from the bow, where the hull was lifted against the reef: a board moved, and a hush, and the hush that follows when people who should not be heard have heard you.

Her heart went hard against her ribs once and then settled. She had heard that hush before. There was a woman on the floor of a flooded galley in a hurricane year who had made the same sound when Juno came through the door. The fear came off her, and under the fear the shame of hiding.

Juno straightened and walked forward along the rail, keeping her hand on it. The bow was hung high on the reef. As she came forward the deck sloped uphill under her, and the fog thinned a little, and she could see the foredeck now, the anchor windlass, the big hatch of the forepeak, the winch drums shining with old grease. Behind the windlass was a small steel door set into the deckhouse there, and the door was not quite shut. A black line of gap down its edge. The width of a breath.

She looked at the gap and let the silence run out, and did not hurry to fill it.

"Meridian Dawn," she called, close now and quiet, without a shout in it. "Tug alongside. Don't open that door onto a scared man. I've got a Teo with me and he doesn't like guns and I haven't got one anyway. Come out where I can see you."

The door stayed shut. Then it opened, just enough, and three faces looked out of it in a stack, one above the other, as if they were shelves in a cupboard.

The first was a woman, dark-skinned, gray in her brows, wearing a heavy canvas work coat with the collar turned up and a ship's apron folded at her waist. The second was a young man with a split lip and a swelling under one eye. The third was an older man, wiry, bald, with a face like a weathered post, and he came out first and stood in front of the other two, planting himself on the sloped deck like a man setting down a crate.

"Who are you?" he said.

"Juno Halloway. Salvage tug Ninth Hour. I heard your mayday. I've come to help, if you'll let me." She tipped her chin at the woman. "Are you hurt? Anyone below?"

"Hurt?" The woman looked past her, at the sloped deck, at the fog, at the long gray water running in between the headlands. "No. Not hurt."

"Then come out of there." Juno stepped back to give them the deck. "What's your name?"

"Barrow," the bald man said. "Bosun. Or what's left of one."

"Bosun," Juno said. "Where's your crew?"

"Gone," Barrow said, and the woman beside him said "Ashore" at the same moment, and the young man with the split lip said nothing at all, and the two words hung in the air like two birds flown the wrong way past each other.

Juno let the silence sit. The fog moved.

"Gone," she said. "Ashore. Which is it?"

"It was dark," Barrow said.

"Dark or not, you know your own crew." She looked at him steadily. "Where's the master?"

The three of them looked at one another. Nobody in that huddle was weighing what was true. They were weighing what to say.

"Can't say," Barrow said.

"You can't say where your master is."

"That's right."

"Her name's Voss. I heard her on the radio yesterday evening." Juno said it plainly and watched them. "I've heard her voice. She was aboard then. She asked for help. Is she aboard now?"

"We can't say," Barrow said again, and the wiry frame of him went up and down with his breathing and nothing else did. The young man had gone carefully blank, the way a man does when he is afraid of his own face. The woman in the work coat stared at the deck planks. When she looked at the young man he turned a little, as if to hide the split lip and the bruise under the eye, and she looked away again, too fast.

"You can't say," Juno said. "You were asleep when the ship struck. You don't know where your people went. You don't know where the master is. You were hiding in the forepeak when a ship that had asked for help heard nothing back." She looked at each of the faces, one at a time, in the way her father had taught her, not a man who asks or tells but a man who looks and knows. "There's a tide coming, and a surge behind it, and your freighter's on a reef. I've got a tug alongside and an engineer who can keep her afloat if you'll tell me the truth. If a lie's what you've got, I'll take it, but I won't like it, and I'll remember it when the harbor master writes down who said what."

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Barrow said, "We were asleep," in a voice that had decided to be calm and hadn't gotten all the way there. "There was a noise. The ship went over. We came up. Everything was dark and there was no one about, so we went in the forepeak to wait. That's all."

"That's all," Juno said.

"That's all," Barrow said.

The young man with the split lip looked at the deck.

Teo came over the rail with the pump gear on his shoulder and a length of hose coiled over the other. He set his load down on the sloped deck and looked at the three in the forepeak door and at Juno's face.

"Nobody's telling you anything," he said.

"They were asleep."

"Everyone's always asleep." He crouched by the hose and started running it out, his hands moving quick and sure. "Give me an hour. I'll sound this hull from the outside and tell you what the reef has done to her. I'll find the bilge and get a suction going. If she's not holed I'd like to know it before the tide turns, because after the tide turns she'll either float or she'll open. There's no third thing."

"Do it. I'll take her log."

Teo looped the hose over a bollard and stopped, looking up at her. "Juno. You heard her on the radio last night."

"I told you I did."

"A master doesn't leave her ship aground and go quiet. Not one with a radio working and a tug coming. She said hold on and wait. Those were her words, near enough. And now she's not here and these three are." He wiped his hands on his thighs. "You take her log. I'll take the hull. Halfway through this I'm expecting something to stop making sense, and I'd like it to be the hull and not the people."

"Hull first," Juno said. "People second."

"Hull first." He went aft with the hose dragging behind him.

The freighter's bridge was up the ladder and forward, dark, and Juno went up with her hand on the rails. The ladder treads were wet. The door was not locked. Inside, the wheelhouse smelled of cold coffee and burned wiring and something else, a faint smell beneath those, not quite chemical and not quite anything she could name. The windows were fogged. A radio console glowed dim on emergency batteries. An empty mug had rolled across the chart table and left a brown ring on the chart, which was the Bellmouth and the reef and a penciled course laid across it that did not go through the channel.

She stood over that course and studied it. A pencil line, hard and straight, running from the channel into the reef's north shoulder like a rule drawn across a page. A ship that had lost her way would have wandered. This line had been drawn by a hand that knew where it was going.

She left the chart where it lay and opened the logbook.

It lay on its rack, open, and its last pages had been torn out. Not one page. A wad of them, close to the binding, leaving ragged tongues of paper where the book had been parted from its own recent history. They had not torn clean. Nobody tears ten pages clean with bare hands.

Juno stood in the empty wheelhouse and looked at the torn edge. The paper tongues were still young. No dust on them, no weighing-down of age. The torn white showed raw against the old cream pages.

Then she read the last page that was left.

The last two days of entries were ordinary. Course, weather, watches, a note about a fuel transfer at the east dock, a note about the peeling paint. Then a half-line, and the page after it gone.

"Course altered west," the half-line said. "At master's order, no consul—"

She turned the book over. The torn stubs hung down from the binding. Nine or ten pages, maybe more. Whoever tore them out had done it carefully, in a hurry, and then had not thought to throw the book over the side. Or had not had time.

At master's order. Juno looked at her father's name on her own transom in her mind's eye, the steel letters cut by a man who had never once lied to a maritime court. There were orders a master gave that the company did not want written down, and there were companies that would have pages torn out for them by a frightened bosun, and Juno had been too long on this water to believe in coincidence.

She put the logbook under her arm and went to the wheelhouse door and found Teo coming up the ladder with his head down, his face the color of old paper under the soot.

"Hull's sound," he said. "No breach wide enough to matter. Her bow's sitting on the rock like a man leaning on a fence but the water's not coming in. I've got a suction in the bilge and I'll keep it there."

"Then that's a salvage."

"That's a salvage." He came up the rest of the way and stood bracing himself against the canted door frame. "Juno, there's a thing."

"What thing?"

"There's no cargo. I opened the number two hatch on my way up. A freighter this size, bound for the east dock with a load of lime, and her holds are empty as a church on Monday."

"Empty."

"Swept clean. Boot marks in the dust where a load's come out. Whatever she was carrying, it came off her in a hurry, and not at the east dock, because the east dock's not on the Bellmouth reef." He looked at her. "So she unloaded something before she grounded, or she grounded to unload something, and then the floor was scrubbed and the book was torn, and the bosun's sleeping through all of it."

Juno looked at the torn edge of the logbook in her hand.

"Can't say where the master is," she said. "Everything went dark. Asleep."

"Sleeping people don't hide in the forepeak."

"No." She turned back to the dark radio console and the dim glow of it. "They don't."

She brought the mic up and set it to the harbor channel, and the radio found Kestrel Reach, far off through the fog, and the voice that answered was Pell's.

"Reach Harbor."

"Reach Harbor, Ninth Hour. I'm aboard the grounded freighter, Meridian Dawn. I want it logged."

"Logged how?"

"Salvage. I'm the first salvor on scene. I came alongside believing she was abandoned. Her deck was bare when I hailed her and nobody answered. Her bridge is dark. Her boats are swung out and empty. Nothing aboard her was making a sound but the water. I put my hook over her rail on that belief, Pell, and I want that belief in the log, because it's the truth of how I came aboard her."

Pell did not answer at once. When he did, his voice had gone careful, the way a man goes when he's setting a thing down in a ledger and knows it will be read again.

"Fog on the strait, deck empty, boats swung out, no answer to hail. Written. And then?"

"Then I found three of her crew in the forepeak. Since then there's the bosun and two hands aboard, and not one of the three will name her master. They've given me a wall and they're standing behind it. Now. I'm not logging them as crew of an abandoned ship. I'm logging how I came aboard, and you can log that a court will want to hear the rest."

"And a court will," Pell said. "So hear me now, and hear me once. A salvor who boards a crewed ship needs the master's say. It's the master's ship to give or to keep, and a mate's word won't stand for it and a bosun's won't. Every dollar of your claim will lean on that boarding. If it comes out in the court that you knew men were aboard before your hook touched her rail, you'll be arguing from the mud."

"I didn't know it. I know it now."

"Then keep the two facts apart, girl. Put in the log what you saw from your wheelhouse, and put the crew in behind it, and let a lawyer make what he likes of it. I'll log the time you hailed and the fog and the boats. And I'll log the hour you came alongside, so the court can do its own arithmetic." A scribble of pen. "Juno. You said you heard her master on the radio yesterday."

"I did."

"And she's not there now."

"She's not."

"You'll want to write everything down yourself, then. The time you came alongside, the time you opened that log, the names of the three you've got. Maritime court will read it. And, Juno, the surge is still running. My tide board's showing it arriving at the flood tonight and staying through to morning. Call it forty hours of bad water before it eases. Forty hours, and it wants to push up the estuary on the first of them."

"Tonight."

"Tonight. So if you mean to save that freighter, you'd best do it before the salt line comes in. Because once it's in, the only thing that freighter's worth is what the court says she's worth, and the court won't care about Kestrel Reach." A pause. "It won't care about your four payments either. It'll care about what the sea did and what you did while it did it."

The radio clicked and the channel fell quiet.

Juno stood in the dark wheelhouse with the logbook under her arm. Through the fogged window she could see Teo on the foredeck with his hose, and the three sailors standing together by the rail like people who were sorry to be there, and beyond them the rocks showing white where the incoming tide was working the swells, and the fog, and the gray rising water.

She put the logbook inside her coat, against the bank letter, and went down to the deck.

"Teo," she said. "How long to get a tow line on her stern and pull her off that reef?"
