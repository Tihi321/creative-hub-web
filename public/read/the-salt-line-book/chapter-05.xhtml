Contradictions

Juno took Barrow first.

She put him in the freighter’s officers’ mess: a low room, a bolted table, a dead radio, a porthole with gray water sliding past it. She sat on one side. He stood on the other until she told him to sit, and then he sat the way a man sits when he has decided to be patient about something.

Teo had gone below with his jars and his torch. The Ninth Hour lay alongside, made fast fore and aft, her old diesel muttering to itself. Every so often the deck under them moved, a long slow swell under the hull, and the plates in the mess ceiling answered with a sound like a door closing in another room.

“The night she struck,” Juno said. “Say it from the top.”

Barrow folded his hands on the table. His hands were old and clean and did not fit the rest of him. “We were making the last of the ebb. The engine came off her. She lost way and went where the sea took her, and the sea took her onto the reef. We came up when she struck. That’s all there is.”

“The engine came off her.”

“Failed. Aye.”

“Then why is her engine room shut and dogged?”

He did not answer that one. She had walked the machinery spaces herself an hour before, and the engine-room door had been shut and dogged from the inside of the frame, the lugs down. There was no bar across it anywhere. She had stood in the dark with her torch up and heard the sea move in the bilges beyond it, and thought nothing of it then. Now she watched Barrow not answer, and knew the dogs had been put down by somebody who did not want her in it.

“You told me on the foredeck you were all asleep when she struck,” she said. “Fen tells me that’s not so.”

Barrow looked at her, and his weathered face did not change. “Fen’s frightened. She’ll say anything she thinks will make you easy.”

“Would you rather I have Fen in here?”

“No,” he said, quickly. Too quickly. “She’s no help to you. She’s a deckhand and she was in her bunk mostly, and she don’t know the running of the ship.”

“Then tell me why your master altered course west.”

His hands came apart on the table. For half a second something went across his face, and then it was gone and the weathered calm was back.

“I’m the bosun,” he said. “The master keeps her counsel. I don’t ask her course.”

“You knew of that panel. You knew the paint on it and the bolts on it and whose job it was to keep it painted. And you told me a while ago you’d never seen a drum in this hull.”

“I said I didn’t know of a drum in this hull.”

“Let’s have the truth, bosun. You’re not a clever liar and I’ve got hours, not days.” She leaned forward. “When I take the strain, does she come off whole?”

Barrow sat a moment with his eyes on the table. When he looked up, his face had gone a shade and he said, “She hit the reef. That’s all.”

“Get out,” Juno said. “Send me the woman.”

Fen sat where Barrow had sat and would not put her hands on the table. She kept them in her lap, and she looked at Juno’s face a long while, and then at the porthole, and then back.

“You want the truth,” she said. “It’ll not help you and it’ll cost me.”

“I’ve a court to write to and a rock to pull your ship off and a tide coming in. Tell me what it costs you and I’ll tell you what it’s worth to me.”

Fen breathed out. “We wasn’t asleep. We was up. All of us up and the master on the bridge since before dark. We was running the engine near flat out for an hour, and the master gave the order and we came west into the strait. No pilot, no boat. And the north mark’s lamp was out where there’s always lamps. That’s what I know.”

“How came she onto the reef?”

“The master brought her onto it.”

“She ordered it done.”

“She did it. There’s a difference.”

Juno set her hands flat on the table. “Why?”

“That’s not mine to say.” Fen’s gray brows came down. “I’ll tell the court when the court asks. Before then I’ll not say one word more, because there’s a man out there”—she nodded at the nearest wall, and Juno understood she meant Barrow beyond it, not somebody outside the ship—“who’ll say I’m a liar, and there’s worse than that beyond the bar of a courtroom if I open my mouth wrong.” She looked at Juno steadily. “You’ve had a look at what’s in her. You know what they’ve done. Then you know why she had to come ashore somewhere it could be found and not go to the bottom quiet. If she’d gone to the east dock they’d have had the drums off her in the dark and washed her clean and she’d have sailed, and this water’d be poison in a hundred years and no one the wiser. So we came west, and she put her on the reef, and now here we are.”

The hull moved under them. The mess ceiling answered. Somewhere below, Teo’s torch sang a note against steel.

“Who are you protecting, Fen?”

The woman’s jaw worked. “The one who did what was right,” she said. “And one other I’ll not name.” She stood up. “I’m going back to the forepeak now. Send the boy, and go easy on him. He’s had a bad week of it.”

Davy came in with his rag in his fist and the bruise under his eye gone yellow at the edge. He did not sit until she told him twice, and then he perched on the edge of the chair and looked at the table.

“They’ve told you their stories,” he said. “Barrow and Fen.”

“They’ve told me different stories. I want yours.”

“Barrow said the engine failed.” Davy laughed, quick and short, not because it was funny.

“Did it?”

“No. She was running sweet, sir. I had the watch on her and she was running true and then the master—” He stopped.

“Go on.”

“She gave the order and we came west, and I said to Fen, I said, this ain’t the course, and Fen said, do as you’re told, so I done as I was told.” He wound the rag until it was tight around his knuckles. “And then the north lamp was out and we couldn’t see the reef in the fog, and we went over it, and Barrow come down and he had hold of me before the plates had stopped ringing and he hit me and said I’d run her aground and he’d see me hang for it.”

Juno looked at the yellow bruise and the split in his lip and said nothing. The fog had thinned enough now that a bar of light lay across the table.

“Tell me the truth now,” she said quietly. “You’ve given me a course change and a lamp out and a bosun with a temper. What I want to know is whether she hit that reef by want of eyes on her, or whether the reef was showed to her on purpose.”

Davy’s face went carefully blank, in the way of a young man who has been told what to say and does not believe it, and then all at once his blankness came apart and he put his face in his rag and his shoulders went.

“I think she was showed,” he said, muffled. “I think the master had it planned out before we ever came west.” His head came up. “But I’ll tell you what I do know. Barrow came up from the deep tanks the day before and his boots was wet and his sleeves was wet, and he was sweating like a man who’d seen the sea where the sea shouldn’t be. He said the tanks was taking it and I said, what tanks, and he said, the deep tanks, and he’d no business down the deep tanks at all, that’s the chief’s work, and he told me to hold my tongue and he put his hand on the rail nigh the hatch like he’d shut it in my face. I don’t say the water was there before the reef. I say Barrow knew there was water before the reef, and he’s not a man to get wet for nothing.” He wiped his face. “I’m done. Send me back. I’ve said enough.”

The officers’ mess had a wire mesh over its one small window and the window looked out on the Dawn’s own foredeck. Juno stood at it after Davy had gone and watched the fog come off the water, and she counted what she had.

One said the engine failed. One said the master took her west and put her on the rock. One said Barrow knew the deep tanks were wet before ever she touched it. Three people on one ship, each looking at the same night through a different fear. Barrow afraid of what the court would make of it. Fen afraid of a man behind a wall. The boy afraid of a rope.

She turned it over the way she turned a tow line over in her hands. They were not each protecting themselves. Each was protecting something, and the somethings did not match, and a set of lies that did not match was a strange kind of lie. A crew that feared for their jobs would tell one story. A crew that feared for their necks would tell one story. Three stories meant three separate fears, and that took money, or it took a kind of loyalty she could not see the shape of.

Teo came in with his hands wrapped in waste rag and his crate in the crook of his elbow, and he set it on the table and pulled out a folded paper so oiled and handled she would not have known it for a manifest.

“Read it,” he said.

She read it. Cargo: agricultural lime, bagged, two hundred and twelve tonnes, consigned to the east dock, dated six days before the grounding. She turned it over and looked at the back and it was blank.

“Lime doesn’t leak out of a drum.”

“Lime doesn’t come in drums at all.” Teo sat down heavily. “That’s a manifest for what a dock clerk signs and a pilot reads and a customs man glances at and never opens. Two hundred and twelve tonnes of lime is a tidy ship and a tidy pay, and nobody walks down into the lower hold to count the bags.” He tapped the paper. “I found the mate’s stow note. Says the lime was loaded at the number-three shed, up the inner harbor, for the east dock, and here’s the thing. It was loaded, and it never arrived, because there’s not a bag of it aboard. And there’s a barge note pinned to the back, and the barge note says unload at the reef by night. But it gives no barge number and no cargo, and there’s no sign it was ever done.”

“Because what they unloaded at the reef wasn’t lime either.”

“Because what they unloaded was the drums going ashore somewhere and the crew going ashore somewhere, and this paper was meant to be the tidy story.” Teo folded it away. “Juno. There’s another thing. The reef’s groaning. The swell’s picked up and she’s working on that shoulder and I don’t like the sound of the plates.” He put his rag down. “Whatever that panel holds, if she tears on that rock it goes into the water and up the estuary and into the wells. So we’ve got to decide what we’re doing in the next hour, not the next four.”

Juno went up on deck. The fog had gone off to the west and the Bellmouth lay open in a flat gray light, the water black and working, the reef a line of white that came and went. The Dawn lay across it, moving. She could feel it now through her soles, the long slow lift and the settle, and on the settle there was a sound under everything like a knuckle rapped on a door, again and again, patient and wrong.

Barrow had come out of the forepeak with Fen and the boy behind him. He stood at the rail with his arms folded and watched the reef.

“You wanted words,” Juno said. “I’ve had them. All three.”

“I know what they said.”

“You don’t. But here’s mine.” She stood beside him and looked out over the water. “One of you says the engine failed, which a court can check, because the engine’s still there and it’ll tell its own story. One says the master took her west on purpose. One says you were down the deep tanks a day early with your boots wet. Now the tide’s turning and this ship is groaning under my boots, and I’ve a town up the estuary that drinks what runs out of the Bellmouth. I’m done asking who’s lying. I’m asking you one thing, and if you’re a bosun worth your brass you’ll answer it straight. When I take the strain, does she come off whole?”

Barrow looked at the reef a long while. His jaw worked, and he looked at Fen, and Fen looked away, and he looked at the boy and the boy looked at the deck.

“I don’t know,” Barrow said, and for the first time his voice had nothing in it but the truth. “I know she’s pinned on her shoulder and I know she took water fore of the collision bulkhead, and I know the master’s the only one aboard knows where it went.” He said it flatly, a man reading off a chart he had never wanted to draw, and Juno understood that the wet boots on the stairs were a thing he would not speak of and a thing he could not now deny. “You want her straight answer, captain, you’ll have to make her want to give it.”

“I thought you’d never say it,” Juno said, and left him standing at the rail.

She found Voss at the bow, at the windlass, in her heavy gray coat. The master had her back to the deck and her eyes on the water, and the fog thinned and lifted, and the great yellow shape of the Iron Gannet lay off the stern with her winch turning over.

“I’ve had a story each from your bosun and your two,” Juno said. “None of them agree. I don’t care who’s lying. What I care is the ship.” She came and stood beside her at the rail. “Look at the reef. It’s moving her. In an hour I’ll have my line on her and I’ll take the strain and I’ll either bring her off or I’ll open her on the rock. Now. You’re her master. Is she whole under the water, or is she not?”

Voss looked at the white water showing and hiding, showing and hiding. The reef groaned. The freighter’s plates answered it.

“She’s whole at the forefoot,” Voss said slowly, “and she’ll not stay whole long. Tell your engineer this. The water she’s taken is forward of her collision bulkhead, and the bulkhead holds her still, and the bulkhead will fail before the bottom does.”

“Then take her off the rock.”

“Not yet.”

“You’ll tell me why.”

“I’ll not.”

“Then I’ll tell you this,” Juno said. “When the surge comes, the salt goes up the estuary and into the wells of Kestrel Reach, and the town drinks it for a generation. And the sea’s already coming into your forward hold, and what’s in the lower hold’s already leaking. So if you won’t take her off with me, you’ll stand there and watch me do it alone, and if she tears on that shoulder, whatever your drums are, they’ll be in the water, and you can tell the court that was the price of your silence.”

Voss turned her head and looked at Juno a long while, and her face was made of fear held absolutely still, and then she looked away to the reef and said nothing at all.
