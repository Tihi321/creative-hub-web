The Salt Line

The night came down hard, and the surge came with it.

It rose up the estuary mouth, a slow gray lifting of the whole sea, no wave to it, and the wind swung round into the northeast and shoved the water ahead of itself. The Dawn lay on the west flats with her bow up and her stern down and her flooded tanks holding her, and the sea climbed her side until it licked at the rail. Juno had the Ninth Hour alongside the freighter's quarter with two springs out and every fender she owned jammed between the hulls. She stood in her wheelhouse and watched the water climb and did not sleep.

Teo came up at midnight with his hands black to the wrist. "Number one pump's gone. Winding's hot and the impeller's chewed to hell. I've got the portable running off the Dawn's own emergency generator and the big one on the tug still turning, but the big one's losing her prime every twenty minutes."

"Keep her at it as long as she'll go."

"She's a towboat. She's doing a thing she was never built to do, with a cracked head and wire where the gasket goes." He wiped his hands on his trousers and looked at them and wiped them again. "She'll fail. The only question's what she does before she fails."

"Then we'll do the same," Juno said. "Fail last."

The breach widened at two in the morning.

Barrow came up the ladder fast and put his head into the wheelhouse where Voss sat and Juno stood. "She's making water faster. The seam's opened and the mat's worked loose at the bottom chain. The pump's holding it level the way a man holds a door against a flood."

Teo went forward with him and came back with his face set. "The mat's torn away at the lower edge. I can't get it back on her in this sea. I can't send a man over the side in the dark. That's a man drowned."

"Then we don't send him," Juno said. "We hold what we've got and wait for the light."

At three the portable pump died. Teo had it apart on the deck with a torch in his teeth and came to the wheelhouse with the sheared impeller in his hand, a twist of bright metal no longer any use to anyone. He put it on the chart table and stood looking at it, and the big pump hammered below them and the whole tug shivered with it.

"If the big one goes, I can't promise her past dawn," he said at last.

Juno went out on deck. The wind came over the rail and the spray came with it, and the lanterns on the flats bobbed where the town's boats lay, waiting on a word from her that she had not yet found how to give.

She called Reach Harbor. It took three tries; the air was full of weather.

"Reach Harbor, this is Ninth Hour. Pell, are you there?"

"I'm here." His voice came thin out of the static with wind in it. "It's half past three in the morning. What's happening down there?"

"The Dawn's taking water forward of the bulkhead and my pumps are failing one by one. If the forepeak fills and the bulkhead goes, the drums go in the estuary and up the estuary to you. I need hands. I need sandbags in the channel mouth, and I need them now, before the tide turns."

A long pause. "How long have I got?"

"Till the tide turns. Four hours, maybe."

"Then I'll have them there." The radio crackled. "You keep that ship on the flats, Juno Halloway. I'll bring you the whole town."

They came at four, out of the dark: the pilot boat first, then a fishing boat and another, a scallop dredge, a flat-bottomed launch that had no business in a sea like that, and behind those the small boats, a dozen and then more.

Pell swung himself over the rail with empty sandbags over his shoulder, and behind him his son and the boy who pumped gas at the harbor station and two women in oilskins from the fish house and the man who ran the boatyard and had never once spoken to her, and more crowding over the rail until the deck was thick with people.

"Where do you want us?" Pell said.

Juno looked out at the channel mouth. The surge was at its height and the water stood high on the flats. "The mouth. Not across the channel. You'd never hold it with bags. Along the north lip, where the water goes over into the flats and down into the lens. Sandbag the flats and leave the channel running. The Dawn's sitting on the mud and the mud's holding her; build me a wall behind her and the sea's got nowhere to go but out the channel."

"And the drums?"

"The drums stay in her and the hold stays dry. That's my job. Yours is the wall."

Pell wiped his hands on his coat, slow, and looked out at the water and the wind and the lie of the flats. Then he turned to the crowd. "You heard her. North lip of the channel mouth, behind the wreck. Fill them and lay them and keep laying them. The tide turns at seven and we've got no more than that."

They went. The boats broke up and ran up the channel with a lantern in the flat-bottomed launch's bow, and the sandbags went up the flats in a chain of hands, and the wall began.

At half past four the big pump lost its prime and did not come back.

Teo had it open before the smell of hot metal had cleared. Juno stood over him with the torch while the wind worried at her coat, and the big pump lay in pieces on the deck plates, the coupling bent and the winding black.

"That's the last of her," he said. "The bilge pump's all I've got and it's a toy in a sea like this. Maybe an hour before the forepeak takes the bulkhead."

Barrow came up at a run and said the water in the forepeak had taken the lower step. Voss came out onto the deck with her face gray and set.

"How long?" she said.

"An hour," Teo said. "Maybe. The bulkhead's a good bulkhead and it was built to be beat. But the water'll find its way aft through a seam and it'll come up in the lower hold from below, and then the drums go where the water goes."

"They won't," Juno said.

She went across with Teo and looked down into the lower hold with a torch. The drums stood there, sixty of them, stencilled SX-9, weeping their green film into a bilge that was rising. It came up the frames a finger's breadth at a time and lay black and quiet and patient.

She went back on the Ninth Hour's deck and looked at the lanterns bobbing in a line on the flats, and at the water she could not pump, and at the hour she had.

"Teo. The deep tanks. Voss's flooding's holding the bow up. What if we flood her more?"

He was quiet. The wind came over the rail and lifted the end of a loose line and dropped it.

"More weight aft, more bow rise," he said slowly. "The forepeak takes less water over the seam and the bulkhead's got less head on it. But she'll sit down in the mud and strain her bottom, and if she can't float she'll break her back. That's a ship that won't come off these flats in one piece."

"And the drums?"

"The more her bow comes up, the higher the drums sit. They'd be dry." He stopped. "You're not asking me to flood the ship so I can save the pumps."

"I'm asking you to flood the ship so we don't need them. We've been trying to hold the sea out of her and we're losing because we haven't got enough pump. So we put the sea in her where we want it and lift the hold above it."

Teo rubbed his face with the flat of his hand. "It's insane. It might work." He looked at the shell of the big pump and the burnt winding and the bent coupling on the plates. "It'll lose you the ship."

"She's not my ship."

"She might have been. If you'd saved her off the reef instead of beaching her, she'd have been your ship and the award with her." He looked at her. "You've been thinking it since the flats."

"I've been thinking it since the reef. Get Voss. Tell her to flood it all, fore and aft."

Voss flooded the Dawn all the way, and the freighter sat down in the mud as the dawn came up gray behind the channel.

It went slowly, the way an animal lies down, and it took a long while to watch. The tanks swallowed the water and swallowed it and swallowed it, and the Dawn settled by the stern until her well deck was at the waterline and the mud held her bottom like a hand. Her bow came up and up until the sprung seam stood clear of the sea, and the forepeak was a bucket with a hole that no longer reached the water. The water in the forepeak came down over the stumps of the pumps and lay there and did not rise. The bulkhead took what was left of it and groaned and did not fail.

Teo stood at the rail with a torch on the lower hold, and the light went down into it and found the drums standing dry above a bilge that had stopped rising. "She's dry," he said at last. "She'll stay dry. The drums are high and the ship's down and the mud's holding her. She'll not float off this tide or the next, but she's not going to break."

The tide turned at seven and did not come back.

By full daylight the surge was going out and the morning was gray and cold and the wall was there.

Juno walked it with Pell, wet to the knees, from the channel lip to the flats behind the wreck. It ran low along the north flats, sandbag on sandbag, the whole length of them. The people who had built it lay along it asleep in their oilskins, and the boatyard man sat up against a sandbag with his hands raw and his eyes shut.

"Test it," Pell said.

The man from the town well was there with his gauges and a woman from the council with a clipboard. They drew a sample from the north well and the south well and the well by the school, and ran the tests there on the grass with the wind trying to take the paper. Juno stood with her hands at her sides and waited.

Pell read the gauges and was quiet a while, and then he looked up. "Fresh," he said. "All three. Not a salt grain in one of them that wasn't there before the surge. The lens held and the flats held."

He put his hand out and Juno took it. The man from the well put his hand out too, and the woman from the council, and the people along the wall began to wake and look at each other, and someone said the word and it went along the wall, fresh, the water's fresh, and someone began to laugh.

The Gannet came back at eight.

She hove to off the channel and put a boat over, and Doran Rusk came up the flats with his man and his papers under his arm. He came to where Pell stood on the wall and Juno stood beside him, and he did not look at Juno at all.

"I'm here to file a statement," he said. "For the log."

Pell got out his book and his pen and laid them on the seawall. "Go on."

"I want it recorded that the Iron Gannet stood by through the night at the harbormaster's direction and rendered all assistance asked of her and no more." Rusk's voice was flat. "And that I take no part in the beaching of the Meridian Dawn, and that in my judgment she was put aground in a manner inconsistent with the law of salvage, and any claim arising from it is not mine."

"Noted," Pell said, and wrote it. "Anything else?"

Rusk looked at the Dawn lying down in the flats, and at the wall, and at the people along it, and at Juno. Whatever he was calculating now, he could not make the numbers come out.

"The town's water's fresh," he said. "I'm glad of that." The words seemed to cost him something. "I'll take my complaint about the interference off the file. There's no prize in this for anyone. I'd only be spending my own money on lawyers to prove it." He tucked his papers under his arm. "The company will hear from the court about the drums. I'll tell them what I saw. I didn't know what was in that hold and I'd not have towed it to sea if I had."

"You'd have towed it to sea," Juno said, "because it paid."

Rusk looked at her a moment. "Yes," he said. "I would. And you'd have done the same, once. I've watched you too long to think different."

"Maybe," Juno said. "But I'd have known what I was doing."

Rusk turned and went back down the wall to his boat, and the boat went out to the Gannet, and the Gannet's stack went black and she went out the estuary mouth into the fog and did not come back.

Pell closed his book and stood with his hand flat on the cover. "I'll want your statement too. Both of you. What was in the hold and what was done with the ship and every hour of it. The court'll want it and the company'll want it, and I'll have it written down plain so no man can make it anything else."

"It'll be in the log," Juno said.

"A harbormaster's log and a captain's statement are two different things. You beached that ship to keep the drums out of the sea and the salt out of my wells. That's a thing the court should know from you and not from Rusk."

"They'll say I saved her off the reef and then put her on the flats and lost her, and a ship run ashore by the salvor who took her in tow is not a ship saved from the sea. That much I know. There's no award in it. There might not be a claim." She said it steadily and found it did not shake her to say it. "There's a sum in a bank drawer with forty days on it that says I should have towed her out and let Rusk scuttle her. That sum's going to say that a long time. It doesn't change what was in the hold."

Pell was quiet. "I know what the law says. I knew it when I let you do it. I could have ordered you off her and let Rusk tow her out to deep water and I did not, and I'd not do it different. But the law's the law, and the court'll read it the way it reads it." He looked at her. "You knew it when you did it. You knew it on the reef and you knew it when you flooded her."

"I knew it."

"Then I'll not insult you by being sorry for it." He put his book in his coat. "Get your engineer to look at your pumps. And be at the town meeting on Thursday. There's a thing or two the town wants to say to you and it won't wait for the lawyers."

Juno went back to the Ninth Hour and climbed her own rail and looked at her.

The old tug lay alongside the Dawn's quarter with her fenders crushed and her paint scored and her stack black, and Teo was in the engine room with the pump apart on the deck plates and his hands black to the elbow. She was listing to port where she had taken a knock on the quarter, and she was the only thing Juno had in the world besides her father's name and the note at the bank.

"Talk to me," she said through the pipe.

"She's a mess," Teo said. "Cracked head and a burnt winding and a bent shaft coupling and she'll want a slip and a fortnight and a thousand dollars of parts. I've got her bilge pump and the hand pump and that's all that turns." He paused. "You know the court's not going to pay us for this."

"I know it."

"Do you?" His voice came up the pipe warm and flat and a little sad. "Because I've been doing the sums while I had the pump apart. There's a fourth payment due Monday and a bank that wants what the bank wants, and we've got no prize and no award and a tug that won't make a tow till she's fixed and no money to fix her. That's not a hard sum, Juno."

"Then we'll do it the way we do everything," Juno said. "Badly, and last."

Teo laughed down the pipe, a real laugh this time. "Go and get some sleep. There's nothing more the old girl can do today and nothing more you can do either. The town's water's fresh. Whatever happens Thursday, that's true and nobody can take it off you."

Juno stood at the rail a while longer and looked at the Dawn lying down in the flats with her drums dry in her flooded hold and the wall behind her and the water coming back up the estuary mouth clean and gray and fresh. Then she went below and lay down in her bunk in her wet coat and slept, and the Ninth Hour rocked under her with the tide, and the salt line went out with the ebb and did not come back.
