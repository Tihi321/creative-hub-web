Empty Holds

Fog had got into number two hold and would not leave. It came down through the open hatch in a gray column, landed on the floor plates, and lay there flat. Juno stood at the bottom of the ladder and turned a slow circle with her torch held low.

The plates were clean. Not new-clean. Scrubbed-clean, with a mop's memory in the corners and a watery shine where the last of it had dried. Up the walls the paint carried long horizontal scuffs at knee height and at shoulder height, where a crowd of crates had been dragged past and scraped it off in passing.

"Teo," she said. "Look at that paint."

He came down behind her, boots ringing, and crouched at the base of the wall and ran his thumb along the scuff.

"Dragged," he said. "All the way out. A load that sat and settled doesn't leave marks like that. This is a load that got pulled, one thing after another, and to one end." He jerked his thumb aft. "The stern ramp's down. I saw it from the top. Lowered and left down, and if it's down in the water, that's how a thing came off her in a hurry."

Juno walked aft along the hold. Her boots echoed, and the echoes came back off the far end so that she sounded like a woman walking toward herself. The plates were dry, and that surprised her. A ship aground on a reef and her holds dry as a barn floor.

The stern ramp was a wide steel gate set into the after bulkhead, and it stood open. The sea came in through it, flat and gray and quiet, filling the end of the hold to just below the floor plates. It lay there without a ripple, calm as water in a cellar.

"That water's not coming in through the ramp," Teo said. He crouched and put his hand in it, brought it up, smelled it, then licked the back of his knuckle and made a face. "Fresh. Spray, or the hold's own washing-water. The tide's not in her."

"The tide's not in her," Juno said, "and her holds are empty and her ramp is down and her crew were asleep. That's four things in one morning."

"There's a fifth." He pointed. "Boot marks. In the dust. The dust's the only honest thing on this ship."

She looked. The steel wore a fine gray film, and in the film were traces, many of them. Boot prints circled the ramp, going out and coming back in, and by the number of them the going out had been done many times and the coming back in more quickly, and the prints overlapped and scuffed each other out. Crate-corner marks, square and deep, dragged in straight lines that fell away off the ramp into the water. And by the after bulkhead a pair of small shoes had stood still for a while beside a pair of large boots.

"A woman's been down here," Juno said. "And she stood still and talked to a big one, and then they went."

"You're reading dust like a tea leaf."

"I'm reading it the way a salvage court will read it. What time have we got?"

Teo looked at his watch, then at the open ramp and the fog above it.

"Tide's slackening toward the ebb," he said. "Two hours to turn. The surge is behind it. When the flood comes in over the reef she'll start to work. If we're going to pull her off, it's now or it's after, and after is a different ship than this one." He hauled his hose past her and started paying it out over the plates. "I'll get the suction down here. You go and talk to your three sleeping beauties."

They stood where she had left them, by the forepeak rail, bunched tight. Fen had her collar up and her eyes on the reef, and the white water showed and hid, showed and hid. Barrow stood a little apart with his arms folded, bald head bare to the fog, and he watched Juno come the way a dock boss watches a customs officer. Davy had found a rag and was winding it around a winch drum, which is the work a body does when he wants to be seen doing work.

"Bosun," Juno said. "I've been down your holds. Number two's empty, swept, and her stern ramp is down in the water. There's dragged paint on the walls and a wet drag-mark in the dust that says a load came off her and went over the side in a hurry. And the water lying in the after end of that hold is fresh."

Fen turned her head very slightly. Davy wound his rag, and wound it, and had run out of winch to wind.

"You were asleep when the ship struck," Juno said. "That's what you told me and I wrote it down. But that load didn't come off this hold while you were asleep in the forepeak, because someone would have heard it and felt it and woken up. Where was the Dawn bound?"

"East dock," Barrow said.

"Her holds are empty. Her manifest will say lime and it will be a lie, and I'll have written down that you told me lime." She let it sit. "Where was she bound?"

"East dock." He did not blink.

"When did the cargo come off?"

"Don't know."

"Did you help unload her?"

"Don't know."

Fen made a small sound, a breath through her nose, the kind of sound a person makes when something has gone past what they can stand.

"Barrow," she said. "For God's sake."

"Fen," Barrow said, without turning his head. "Don't."

Fen. Juno filed the name and let her eyes rest on the woman a moment. "Fen," she said. "You said ashore, yesterday. When he said gone. Which of your people went ashore?"

"I said it," Fen said, and her jaw worked. "It's still true."

"Who went ashore?"

"Ask me something I can answer."

"Can you say where your master is?"

Davy wound the rag one more turn and said, without looking up, "She's aboard."

The silence went all the way around the foredeck and back. Barrow turned his head, and Davy kept his eyes on the winch, and the rag went still.

"You'll be quiet now, Davy," Barrow said, and it was not the voice he had been using.

"She's aboard," Davy said again, to the winch, as if the winch were the only thing he trusted. "She's been aboard the whole—"

Barrow moved. Not far, only a step, only the shift of a big lined body between the young man and Juno, but Davy stopped talking and the foredeck went quiet.

"Master's aboard, then," Juno said, into the quiet. "That's a start. Where aboard?"

Barrow's jaw set. He said nothing at all, and the nothing was a wall and he was behind it, and he looked at her over the top of it and waited to be asked again.

"All right," Juno said. "Then I'll ask the thing I came to ask. I can put a line on your stern and drag you off this rock, and I want to know if she comes off whole or if I tear her open and drown you. So." She looked at Barrow, and at Fen. "If I pull, does she hold?"

Fen's hands came out of her pockets and gripped the rail, and the knuckles went white. Barrow looked at the reef with his arms folded and said, "That's a question for the master."

"Where is she?"

No answer. He stood with his face like a weather post, and the fog moved, and Juno understood that this silence was not the same as the last one. The bosun did not know where his master was standing, or did not want to know, and he would not be the one to put her in front of a salvor's questions.

"Then I'll find her myself," Juno said, and walked away.

The lower hold sat under number two, reached by a ladder in a shaft so narrow her shoulders touched both sides. The smell met her at the top of it. Not bilge. Not fuel. Not the cold dead smell of a hold shut up a long time.

"Teo," she called down. "What's that smell?"

"Don't know," his voice came up. "Sweet, isn't it. Sweet and sharp both. You coming down?"

She came down. The lower hold was low-ceilinged and dark, and her torch ran along steel bulkheads and over pipes bundled tight to the walls, and Teo crouched by the after bulkhead with his beam up on a plate of steel set flush into the wall. A square of it, wider than her two arms and taller than the hatch on number two. It had hinges. It had a hasp. It had a row of bolt heads all around its edge, and on the bolt heads the paint was not the same paint as the wall beside it.

"That's not a bulkhead," Teo said. "That's a door somebody put in front of something and painted over so it wouldn't look like a door."

"Provision room?"

"Provision room's aft of that. I checked. Provision rooms don't get hasped and bolted and painted." He put his hand flat on the panel and leaned close. "And this is where the smell's coming from. Round the edge of it. Same smell as the bilge in number two. I pumped that bilge and the smell came up with the water and I thought it was just a modern ship. It's this."

Juno put her hand where his had been. The steel was cold. She put her nose near the seam and breathed in. Sweet and sharp and wrong. A smell that would get into a man's coat and stay there, and there was one proper home for a smell like that on a working ship, and that home was labeled and sealed and logged, and it did not live behind a bolted painted door in a lower hold.

"Can you get it open?"

"I can burn those bolts with the torch. Half an hour."

Juno straightened and looked at the bolted door and the thread of wrong smell coming round its edge. She thought of the wet drag-mark in the dust and the boot prints by the ramp and the young man with the split lip, and the thing in her chest that had been a salvage claim went cold and heavy and settled into a harder shape, a thing with edges. She had come aboard to take a ship off a rock for money. She was standing in the dark over something that was not about money at all.

"Hold off on the torch," she said.

She found Davy at the winch, alone. Barrow and Fen had gone forward and the fog had folded over them and left the young man with his rag and his bruise, and he wound and unwound the rag and watched her come and did not run.

"Your lip," Juno said. She stayed a long stride off, hands at her sides. "The reef do that?"

Davy looked at the winch. "Fell."

"On what?"

"On the deck."

"Deck's flat as a plate. You'd bruise your chin, not your mouth." She let the quiet work on him. "You said she's aboard. The master. That's a brave thing to say in front of a big man. Is he the one who shut you up?"

He wound the rag. His hands were shaking, and he was trying to wind the shake out of them.

"I'm not the law and I won't take you to anyone," Juno said. "But Fen won't say and Barrow won't say and I've got a tide coming and a bolted door in your lower hold, and I've got to know whether the master's the one holding that door shut or the one who wants it open. You can tell me which, and you can tell me nothing more, and I'll be content."

Davy stopped winding. He looked up the deck at the fog, and at the forepeak, and his young face did a thing Juno had seen before on men older than him, which is to say he made a decision and then was afraid of it.

"We were not asleep," he said, low. "The bosun went down to the deep tanks with a hose the day before the grounding, and he came back wet to the shoulders. There was a lamp gone from the north mark and he said I took it. And then the master brought her west, and the bosun had us in the forepeak before she struck, and he said—" He stopped.

"Go on."

"He said we were asleep," Davy said. "He said it before she struck. He said it in the forepeak while the ship was still running."

Juno stood very still.

"Where's the master now?"

"I don't know." He looked at her straight for the first time, and there was no lie in it. "She's aboard. That's all I know. She come out of her cabin after and she went forward and she's not come back, and the bosun don't want her found. That's all I know, and I've said more than I meant to, and I'd be glad if you'd not tell him it was me."

"I won't tell him," Juno said. "Now put that rag down and go and find my engineer in the lower hold. Tell him I said to hold the torch until I'm there."

Teo came up the ladder at a run and found her at the rail with her hand on the cold steel, looking at the water.

"Juno. Before we do anything else. There's a fifth thing I owe you."

"What is it?"

"I got a look at number three hold. Starboard side, behind the engine casing. Empty too, and swept, but not swept the same as the others. That one's been hosed. Scrubbed and left wet, and the plates are still wet, and it's not rain. Somebody washed her out on purpose and washed her out late, hours ago, not days." He wiped at his face with his wrist and left a smear of rust and soot on his cheek. "I've been on salvage ships thirty years and I've seen a hold scrubbed for a few reasons. Weevils. Fever. A cargo that leaked and they want it forgot." He looked at her. "These people aren't merchants and this isn't a merchant's mess. If I drag her off and she opens on the reef with whatever's behind that panel, it's not a salvage. It's a spill. And a spill in this strait runs up the estuary, and the town drinks from the lens, and it won't be the court that pays for it."

"I know it."

"The master's the one knows where the water went," Teo said. "Davy says she's forward. Go and find her and put it to her straight. I'll hold the torch. I'll not open that door on my own judgment."

Juno looked down the barrel of the deck. The fog moved through the strait and the white water worked the reef, showing and hiding, and somewhere forward in that gray was a woman who had gone quiet in the dark rather than answer a radio.

"Keep the suction in number two," Juno said. "And get a sample jar and your gloves and wait for me. If it's the thing I think it is, I want it seen and sealed before this ship comes off that rock and the sea gets a vote in what happens next."

She found Voss on the bow, at the windlass, in a heavy gray coat with a ship's apron over it and a cap pulled low. She had not seen her come out. She stood square to the white water with her hands at her sides, and she did not turn when Juno's boots came up the sloped deck, and she did not turn when Juno stopped three strides behind her.

"Captain Voss," Juno said. "My engineer says your hull's sound. He says with two lines and knowing what your bottom did when it struck, he could haul you off before the surge comes in tonight. But there's a bolted door in your lower hold with a chemical smell coming round it, and I'm not pulling this ship off this reef until I know what's behind it. So I'll ask you plain. Will you help me save your ship, and will you tell me what I'm standing over?"

The fog went through the both of them. The tide clucked at the freighter's flank.

"Not yet," Voss said.

"Not yet is not a no."

"It's not a no." Voss's voice was low and steady and it cost her something to keep it so. "You've got a claim and you've got a court and you've got a town up the estuary. You'll get your answer when I've thought it through."

"The tide won't wait for your thinking."

"No," Voss said. "It won't."

She stood with her cap down and her eyes on the reef, and she gave Juno nothing else, and the white water showed and hid, and at last Juno spat over the side and left her standing.

The panel came open and the smell came out of it all at once.

Teo had the last bolt and he turned it and pulled, and the door swung wide on its hinges, and Juno put her sleeve to her mouth and took the torch from him and held it in.

Drums. Row on row of them, low and wide, stacked tight in a steel compartment no bigger than a larder. Every one painted the same dull blue and stenciled with a black code, and on the drum nearest her the paint had gone black and bubbled and crept down its side. A slick lay in the coaming beneath them, thin and bright, threaded with green and yellow. One drum, near the middle, had a wet mouth at its seam.

"Count them," Juno said.

Teo counted. He counted twice. "Forty. Maybe a few more behind. Three tiers."

"Get your jar. Gloves on. A sample, sealed, and write the time on the lid." She held the torch steady on the leaking seam and the slow bright bead coming down it. "And Teo. Not a word of this to the three of them, not yet. We know now what they've been afraid of, and a frightened man does a fool thing."

"What are they afraid of?"

"Of being caught with it," Juno said. "Or of what the owner does to the man who lets it be caught. I don't know which, and it wants knowing before I put a line on this ship." She looked at the wet seam and the green in the slick and thought of the town up the estuary, drawing from a lens it had never once had to think about. "So here's what we do. We get the sample, and we get the ship's papers out of her desk, and we keep this panel shut and in the log as sealed and in her master's keeping until I've had my say with her. The finding-out is the salvage now, whether the court rules it or not."

Teo got his jar and his gloves and set to work over the leaking drum, and Juno held the torch and counted the hours she had left. The tide was turning. She could feel it in the plates under her boots, a slow grind of iron against rock, and out past the bow the white water was beginning to move.

There was a woman at the windlass who would not say yet. There was a bosun who would not say at all. There was a young man with a split lip who had told her when the lie began. There were drums in a bolted room, and a town up the estuary that did not know any of it, and a bank letter in her coat that said forty days and four payments and eleven thousand four hundred dollars, and none of it mattered as much as the tide.

"Teo," she said. "How long to get a tow line on her stern?"

He sealed the jar and wrote on the lid and looked at the water on the reef and did not answer at once.

"Two hours," he said. "If the master tells us where the water went."
