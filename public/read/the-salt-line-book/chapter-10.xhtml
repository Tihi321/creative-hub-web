Charges

The notice came by email to the harbormaster's office, and Pell printed it out and walked it down to the wharf. A thing like that, he said, ought to be handed over. Not read off a screen in a room by yourself.

Juno read it on the fish house steps with the wind picking at the paper.

Halyard Shipping had been charged. Illegal dumping of listed hazardous waste, sixty drums of it, carried under a false manifest across two state lines and bound for the deep water past the shelf. Conspiracy to conceal. Falsification of shipping records. Four names sat below the company's, and she knew two of them.

"Voss?" she said.

"Fully cooperated," Pell said. "Her statement's the spine of the whole thing, they tell me. She's been in the courthouse at Bellmouth since Tuesday, giving it over and over to whoever asks, and she does not flinch from it. Her mate's done the same. The company's lawyers are saying they didn't know and the lawyers' lawyers are saying they did."

"The cook? Fen? Davy?"

"Witnesses. Barrow's lawyered up and gone quiet, which is his right. Fen's told them everything she knows twice. The boy Davy's told them about the beating." Pell folded his arms against the cold. "The drums are the case. Your man Teo took a sample out of that hold sealed and witnessed, and the court's chemist has it, and it's SX-9 to the last decimal and no lime in it anywhere. They've got a barge that came alongside the Dawn in the dark off the reef. They've got the note in the manifest naming the reef and not naming the barge. They've got the barge itself now, and the barge has a master who would like very much to be a witness instead of a defendant. It's going to hold."

"Good," Juno said. The wind took the edge of the paper and she held it flat with her thumb. She looked at the four names again. Names, that was all. Small print on a county notice. She found she could not feel much about them one way or another, which surprised her. What she felt was for the drums, and for the crew who had put them where they could be found.

"It's going to hold," Pell said. "And it's going to take you with it into that courtroom as a witness, because you're the one that found it and opened it and beached it, and they'll want you to say so in your own words."

The maritime court sat in a low brick building at Bellmouth with a green ceiling and a radiator that knocked all through the afternoon.

Juno had been in it once before, when her father was alive and there was a dispute over a mooring chain. She remembered the smell of it, dust and wet wool and paper, and the way the light came down from the tall windows and lay on the floor in shapes that did not change.

She wore her good coat and her father's watch. In the witness chair she put her hands flat on her knees so they would not fidget. The chair was hard and too tall for her and she had to keep her feet flat to reach the floor, and she decided not to think about that.

The examiner for the court was a narrow woman with silver hair who took her through it slowly, hour by hour, and Juno gave it plain.

She told them how the mayday had come in the fog and how she had dropped the barge and logged a claim. How she had come alongside and found the Dawn lying on the reef with her boats gone and her decks empty. How the three crew had come out of the forepeak with their stories that did not agree, and the logbook with its last pages torn out and the half-entry in her own coat. She gave them the half-entry word for word, because she had read it so many times she could not forget it: Course altered west. At master's order, no consul.

She told them about the panel, bolted and hasped and painted over, and the smell that was not lime. About the drums and the green film on the bilge and the sample under seal. The examiner asked her to describe the film. It was the color of a frog's back, she said, thin as oil, and it moved on the water in the bilge when the ship worked, and there was a smell to it that sat in the back of the throat and stayed there.

"Captain Halloway," the examiner said, "when you understood what was in that hold, what did you do?"

"I put my line on her bow and took her into the estuary."

"You did not tow her out to sea."

"No."

"And what did you understand would follow from that choice?"

Juno looked at the woman's narrow face and the silver hair and the pen held over the page. "That I'd lose the claim."

"Say what you mean."

"That a ship beached by the hand that claimed her is not a ship saved from the sea. That if I took her onto the flats I'd have nothing coming, and I'd have nothing coming because I chose the flats. I knew it on the reef. I knew she'd not come off the mud in one piece and I knew the law would call that a ruin I made myself."

"Objection to the characterisation of the witness's conduct as arising from her own judgment," said the company's lawyer, a heavy man who had been objecting all afternoon to the shape of the air.

"Overruled," the judge said, without looking up.

The examiner set down her page. "You say you knew the law would look at you that way. Why, then?"

"Because the drums don't float when the sea takes them," Juno said. "They sink, and what runs out of them goes up the estuary with the flood tide and into the lens, and the lens is the only water this town has got. I had an hour and a decision. Whichever I made, I'd have to live in it after. I'd rather live in it with the wells fresh."

"Would you do it again?"

"Yes."

The word came out before she had thought whether she had the right to say it. The examiner held her eyes a moment, then wrote something. Juno listened to the small dry sound of the pen and thought about her father, who had sat in this same chair and come out of it with his mooring chain and his name intact, and she wondered what he would have said.

She thought she knew. The ruling was the ruling and the water was the water, and then he would have gone back to the boat and got on with it.

The company's lawyer stood up to cross-examine her. Three questions: the timing of her claim, whether she had known the ship was not abandoned when she boarded her, whether her own tug had been in arrears at the time. Pell, in the back row, started to get up and Juno gave her head the smallest shake and he sat down again.

"I was behind on my payments," Juno said. "The bank had forty days on me. I'd have claimed a rusty bucket if it had paid the note, and that's the truth of it, and the truth of it doesn't change what was in that hold or what I did with it."

The lawyer looked at his papers and asked nothing more.

The ruling came down on a Friday, in writing, in language that Juno read three times on the fish house steps and then folded and put in her coat.

The court found that the Meridian Dawn grounded by her master's deliberate act, and that a vessel run ashore by the deliberate act of the salvor who had boarded her was not a vessel saved from the perils of the sea within the meaning of the articles. It found that the services rendered by the Ninth Hour and her master were voluntary and of great merit, and that they averted a grave danger to life and property and to the public water supply of Kestrel Reach, and it said so in words that Pell later had copied out and pinned up in the harbormaster's office so that anyone who came in out of the weather could read them.

But on the law, no salvage award was due, because there was no property saved that the law recognised, and the service had not preserved the thing salved but had altered it beyond the law's reach.

No award.

Not one dollar of it.

The court noted the public service in a paragraph, and the paragraph was worth what a paragraph is worth at the bank.

She stood on the fish house steps in the cold and read it a fourth time. It had not changed. She folded it, put it away, and went down to the wharf without a word to anyone on the way.

She had kept the bank's first letter in the drawer of the chart table since the morning Teo found it under the coffee pot, and she took it out now and laid it flat and read it again as though it might have learned something since September. Four payments in arrears. Eleven thousand four hundred and eleven dollars. She read the figure once, aloud, the way she had read it that first morning, and then she stopped saying it. Saying it did not make it smaller.

Teo had the number one pump out on the deck plates in four pieces and was not looking at it. He had his back against the engine-room doorframe and his arms folded and his eyes on her.

"The boat's not the bank's yet," he said.

"Don't." She held up the letter without turning. "Not that. Not today. We've said it seven years and it was true every year but this one, and this year it's a thing to say because it sounds like hope and it isn't."

He was quiet. The water slapped at the pilings under the wharf and the light in the cabin came gray through the salt on the window. He shifted his weight off the frame and came and looked down at the letter, and then at the figures she had pencilled on the back of an old tide table, and he put one black finger on the column she had added twice.

"There's a fifth payment now," he said. "They've added a fee for the second notice. Twenty-two dollars and a half. It's there on the second page and it's the smallest thing in the whole business and I've been staring at it all morning like it was the one that sunk us."

"Twenty-two dollars and a half of fee," Juno said, "and the whole rest of it still owed. It doesn't matter. It's a stone in a landslide."

"We've not got the rest," Teo said. "We've nine hundred and forty in the world and a cracked head and a burnt winding and a court ruling that says what you did wasn't salvage. I've done it every way there is to do it. There's nothing in it."

"I know there's nothing in it." She folded the letter along the crease it already had, and then, carefully, she tore it in two, and in four, and put the pieces in the stove, which had no fire in it. "But not today."

"Tomorrow, then."

"Tomorrow we'll light the stove," Juno said.

The complaint came in by post on the Monday.

Doran Rusk had filed it before he stood on the wall, and a court clerk in Bellmouth had written across the top of it in pencil the one word that closes a file: withdrawn. Pell held it up and read the word aloud and laid the paper on the desk.

"He's gone," Pell said. "The Gannet's out the estuary and he's gone with her, and the clerk says he paid his fees and took his papers and told them he was done with the Bellmouth. A man doesn't withdraw and sail off unless he means it." He sat back in his chair. "But it'll be in the county register all the same, and it'll be read by somebody in an office who never saw the wall, and they'll ask why a man withdrew it and there'll be no good answer. That's a grudge that don't have to be carried by the man that made it. It'll just sit there in the file and wait for someone to ask."

"Let it wait," Juno said. "It's not the worst thing in that file. The worst thing in that file is a company that put drums on the reef, and they've got lawyers for that."

"They have." Pell turned the paper over and over in his hands. "He said a true thing to you on the wall. That he'd have towed the drums to sea because it paid. He didn't have to say it."

"He said it because it was true and because he wanted it in my head the way it was in his." Juno looked out the window at the harbor water laying its gray light against the pilings. "He was right about what he'd do. He was wrong about what I'd do. But I don't need to hear him say so to be done with him."

Pell put the complaint in the drawer with the other papers that had come to nothing and closed it. "There's a town meeting Thursday," he said. "The notice is on the door. It says nothing about salvage or Rusk or your tug, because I wrote the notice myself and I know what to put in a notice and what to leave out." He looked at her. "You'll be there."

"I'll be there," Juno said.

Thursday the meeting house was full to the back wall with people in wet coats.

Juno sat near the door with the cold coming in behind her and her hands in her lap. Nobody had asked her to speak and she did not intend to.

Pell stood up at the front with his book in his hand and opened it and did not read from it.

"I'll not go over the ruling," he said. "You all know the shape of it. The court says she wasn't a salvor and the law says a salvor's what you have to be to get paid, and there's men in Bellmouth who'll tell you that adds up to her doing wrong. It doesn't add up to that and I'll say what I saw." He put the book down. "I saw that woman take a ship onto the flats in a rising sea to put her drums above the water and her wells above the salt. I saw her lose her prize and say no word about it while she was doing it. I watched her flood the ship she had come to save, and she did it because it was the only way under heaven to keep the drums dry and the lens fresh. I stood on that wall and read the gauges and I tell you the lens held. It held because of the decisions she made in the dark with no sleep and nothing in it for her." He straightened. "This harbor has been telling the world for a hundred years that a town is a thing you keep. That's what she did. Kestrel Reach keeps her. I move the council say so."

The council said so, by a vote that was not close.

Then the boatyard man, who had not spoken to her in nine years, stood up and said he had sandbagged beside her all night and had seen her at the wall before the light, and that the boatyard would stand her a haul-out and a slip at cost whenever she wanted it. He sat down again before anyone could thank him.

The woman from the fish house said the fish house would take a subscription, and it went along the benches, name and sum in a tin. The man from the town well said he would have the school test its water every Friday from now on and write it in a book, so no one could ever be in doubt again.

Juno sat in the back and did not stand up and did not speak, and found her hands were shaking a little, which had not happened to her in a long time. She put them under her coat where no one could see.

Afterward she walked down to the wharf with Pell. The street was wet and the lights lay in the puddles and the cold came up through her boots.

"A subscription's a fine thing on a Thursday night," Pell said. "It's the Tuesday morning I'll be watching."

"It'll be what it is." Juno stepped around a crack in the pavement. "That's not on you. Whatever comes Tuesday, you did a thing tonight that wasn't owed to me."

"It was owed," Pell said. "I'm a harbormaster. I've watched salvage come and go forty years and I've seen men paid for less than you did and seen better men paid nothing, and the law's never once come out where the water was. So a town said its say tonight and wrote it down, and something came of it, or something didn't. That's how it goes in a harbor."

They came to the end of the wharf and stood and looked at the Ninth Hour lying alongside the fish pier with her paint scored and her stack black and her list to port, and Teo on her deck coiling a line.

The lamp over the fish pier was lit and it laid a yellow road on the water. The road broke and made itself again. Out past the flats the Dawn lay down in the mud with the wall behind her.

"There's a thing I've been meaning to ask you," Pell said, "and I'll ask it plain because we're standing in the cold and neither of us has got the patience for the long way. If the council were to ask the bank what it would take in cash to make them whole and let the boat go—" He stopped. "I've said more than I meant to. There's an item coming up at the next meeting and it's not for me to tell you what's in it. Be there."

Juno did not look away from the water. She had learned a long time ago not to take a man's half-sentence and build a house on it.

"I'll be there," she said. "I've been at every one."

Teo finished his coil and hung it on the bitt. He came to the rail and looked at the two of them standing on the wharf and lifted his chin.

"She'll want a slip before she's good for anything," he called. "Whatever else you're about, harbor master, put that in your book."

"It's in the book," Pell called back. "It's been in the book since the flats."

Teo went below, and the light in the engine room came on and showed yellow through the portholes, and the old tug sat on the tide with her heart lit inside her. Juno stood and watched it and let the cold work at her, and after a while Pell went up the street to his supper, and she stayed until the lamp on the fish pier went out.
