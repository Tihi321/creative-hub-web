Two Hundred Clocks at Nine

Roux & Fils, Horlogerie, the Marais. Monday 23 March, half past nine in the morning.

A narrow shop lined floor to ceiling with clocks, in grey street light. The brass bell over the door swings as the door opens and makes no sound at all.

Someone has wedged its clapper with a twist of paper.

On screen: Dec (brown leather jacket, satchel, cheerful)

DEC (happy) Monsieur Roux? C'est moi. L'Irlandais. For the gears.

Nothing answers him. Not one tick.

Two hundred clocks cover the walls, and all of them are silent. A row of cat clocks has its eyes fixed, every one looking left.

DEC (worried) That's very quiet for a clock shop.

On screen: Dec (uneasy), Roux (upright on a stool behind the counter, hands flat, staring at the wall)

ROUX (neutral) It's Sunday.

DEC (thinking) It's Monday. I said Monday, didn't I? For the escapement wheels.

ROUX (surprised) Who are you?

DEC (neutral) Dec. Declan Hartley. I was here Thursday. You called me a thief because I offered you forty euro for a mainspring.

ROUX (neutral) You are a thief.

Roux says it with relief, as if he has found one thing in the room where he left it.

ROUX (sad) I closed the shop on Saturday night. I went to bed. Now it is Sunday, and I come down to wind them, and they are all...

He lifts one hand and lets it fall.

ROUX (sad) They are all at nine.

Dec looks properly. The regulators, the carriage clocks, the ship's clock, the cat clocks, the cheap plastic one over the workshop door. Every face says nine exactly.

On screen: The clocks: two hundred faces, every hand pointing to nine

DEC (scared) Right. Okay. That's a lot of nine.

ROUX (worried) Is it Sunday?

This time it is a question from a very old man, and his voice cracks on it.

Dec comes round the counter. Roux smells of machine oil and pipe tobacco, and of something sweet and dusty.

DEC (worried) It's Monday the twenty-third of March. Have you eaten anything? Had a drink of water?

ROUX (angry) I had my coffee. Everything tastes of cloves. Cloves and slate. Like licking a church roof.

He sticks his tongue out like a child at bad medicine. It is the wrong colour: a dull smeared grey, as if he has been sucking a pencil.

On screen: Roux (tongue out, grey; eyes wet and pink at the rims), Dec (staring)

DEC (thinking) Grey tongue. Cloves. Slate.

Dec files it away, the way his father taught him to keep the screws from a lock: in the order they came out.

ROUX (angry) Somebody has been in here. They have been touching my clocks.

DEC (neutral) Did anyone come to the door last night?

ROUX (angry) Nobody comes to the door. I lock the front, I lock the back, I go upstairs, I sleep, I come down. And now it is Sunday.

DEC (neutral) Monday.

ROUX (angry) Don't correct me, thief.

DEC (happy) That's more like it.

The same shop, beside the longcase clock.

A tall plain oak clock stands by the counter, the kind a farmer hangs his hat on. Behind its little glass window, the pendulum hangs dead.

On screen: Dec (crouched at the open trunk door, phone torch on)

The pendulum hangs at a slant. Dec shines his torch up into the case.

DEC (thinking) Well, hello.

A sliver of brass, no bigger than a matchstick, has been pushed between the crutch and the pendulum rod.

On screen: The pendulum: a thin brass sliver wedged in, holding it off to one side

DEC (thinking) Somebody wound you, set you to nine, and then jammed you so you couldn't go. On purpose.

Lower down, on the bare oak inside the trunk, there is chalk.

On screen: The chalk drawing: a neat circle like a clock face, twelve small marks round the rim, and at the top, where twelve should be, the numeral IX

It is small and careful. Whoever drew it had all the time in the world.

DEC (worried) Monsieur Roux? Did you draw something in your clock?

ROUX (angry) I don't draw. I mend.

DEC (neutral) Grand. Just checking.

He takes a photograph without touching the chalk, and closes the little door the way you close a door on a sleeping baby. Or a bomb.

Roux's back workshop.

Trays of springs and wheels lie like jewels under a strip light. At the far end is the back door to the courtyard.

On screen: Dec (trying the back door, frowning)

The door is locked. Dec looks at it with a professional's interest, and then with a different kind.

DEC (thinking) Ah, now. That's not right at all.

ROUX (angry) What? What's not right?

DEC (neutral) When did you put a new lock on your back door?

ROUX (angry) I didn't! That door has the old lock. My father's lock.

DEC (neutral) Well, it hasn't now. It has a thing out of a blister pack.

On screen: The back door lock: a cheap shiny brass cylinder, fresh bright scratches on the screw heads, and fine scratches round the keyhole like the tracks of tiny skis

DEC (thinking) See those lines round the keyway? That's a pick. Somebody picked this badly, dragging a rake in and out like he was raking leaves.

DEC (neutral) A professional leaves you nothing. You'd never know I'd been there.

He is explaining a lock to a man who has just lost a day of his life, and he can't stop. It is what his mouth does when he is frightened.

DEC (thinking) But here's the thing. There's a thumb-turn on this side, and it's locked from the inside.

DEC (thinking) So whoever picked it from the yard never went out this way. It's a stage set.

ROUX (worried) Then how did they come in?

DEC (worried) The front. With a key. Or you let them in.

Dec unrolls his canvas roll on his knee: twelve picks and two tension wrenches, each in its own pocket. He calls them the cutlery.

DEC (thinking) If I pick it from this side, the pins will tell me...

He has the wrench in the keyway and the hook going in after it, tongue between his teeth.

Out front, the bell over the door rings, loud and clear. Somebody has unjammed it.

PICHON Do not move, monsieur. And please do not finish whatever it is you are doing to that door.

The same workshop, a moment later.

Two young officers fill the doorway. Between them stands a round, tired man.

On screen: Dec (kneeling, hook in the lock, frozen), Pichon (rumpled beige raincoat, grey moustache, reading glasses on a cord, a white napkin tucked in his collar with a small gravy stain)

PICHON (neutral) Commissaire Pichon. Police judiciaire. And you are?

DEC (scared) Dec. Declan Hartley. Look, this isn't what it looks like.

Pichon looks at it: the man on his knees, the hook in the keyhole, eleven more picks laid out like surgical instruments.

ROUX (angry) That is the thief! He wanted my mainspring for forty euros!

PICHON (neutral) Monsieur Hartley.

He says the name the way a doctor says the name of a rash.

PICHON (neutral) At eleven minutes past nine a neighbour reports a break-in. At nine thirty-one I find you inside, at the back door, with burglary tools.

PICHON (sad) I was at breakfast. I would like to go back to breakfast. Please make it easy for me.

DEC (worried) I'm a locksmith. I build escape rooms. You lock people in a room, they find the clues, they pay you.

DEC (worried) I'm building one for the Fondation Vaulmont. A medieval clockmaker's cell. I was buying gears! Monsieur Roux, tell him!

Roux considers this.

ROUX (thinking) He is a thief. But I don't think he stopped my clocks. He can't even get the price of a mainspring right.

DEC (happy) Thank you.

PICHON (neutral) Stand up, please. Slowly. Leave the little knives on the floor.

DEC (angry) They're picks, not knives. They wouldn't cut butter.

Dec stands and leaves the cutlery on the floor. An officer takes his arm, not roughly, and turns him to face the wall.

On screen: Dec (face to the wall, talking fast), Pichon (behind him, unimpressed)

DEC (worried) Can I say one thing? The back door's locked from the inside. Somebody picked it from the yard to fake a break-in, but they never left that way.

DEC (worried) And the clocks were stopped on purpose. There's brass jammed in the pendulum, and a drawing in chalk inside his clock. A clock with nine at the top.

DEC (worried) And his tongue's gone grey. He says everything tastes of cloves.

There is a pause behind him.

PICHON (surprised) His tongue.

Another pause, longer. When Pichon speaks again, his voice has sat up.

PICHON (thinking) A clock. With nine at the top.

DEC (neutral) In the longcase. Inside the door.

Footsteps cross the shop. The little door of the longcase opens. There is only the silence of two hundred stopped clocks and a delivery van beeping somewhere in the street.

The little door closes again, very softly.

On screen: Pichon (close behind Dec, grave, folding his napkin into four)

PICHON (neutral) Monsieur Hartley. You are under arrest.

DEC (surprised) For what? I've just told you...

PICHON (thinking) Exactly. You have told me a great deal more than you should know.

He tucks the folded napkin into his raincoat pocket and nods to the door, where the street and the police car wait.

PICHON (neutral) Come. We will talk about it over lunch.

Dec looks back once at the longcase clock, at Roux on his stool, at the cutlery on the floor.

He has never in his life managed to keep his mouth shut, and this doesn't feel like the morning to start.

What do you choose?
