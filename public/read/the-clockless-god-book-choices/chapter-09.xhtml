The Seventh of the Nine

Atelier Morel, rue Vieille-du-Temple. Sunday, ten past eleven.

Morel has a rule about Sundays. But the skylight is lit, and the Allegory of the Sundial stands on the easel under Inès's UV lamp.

On screen: Morel (on Inès's stool, in her apron, scalpel in hand, nose almost on the paint), Inès (in the doorway, a bag of books, no sleep)

He is working on the gap in the Latin line. Too deep, too fast, too much solvent, right where the painter's ghost ticks lie in the ground.

INÈS (angry) Jean-Paul. Stop. You're taking off the ground.

The scalpel goes skittering across the floor. His face is grey and wet.

MOREL (scared) Inès. You said a week. Your family.

INÈS (neutral) I lied. So did you.

She has had a list ready since Basel, beginning with the flyleaf. She doesn't need it. He puts his face in his hands and cries, the way a tap drips.

She switches off the UV lamp, pulls up the other stool and waits.

MOREL (sad) The gallery, in two thousand and nineteen. My partner went to Brazil, then came the virus. Three banks. My wife still thinks the flat in Vincennes is ours.

MOREL (sad) Then Lassalle came. My notary for years. He had a client, a patron of the arts, who would buy my debts. For a few small favours.

INÈS (neutral) Vaulmont.

Morel flinches as if she has hit him.

MOREL (worried) A valuation. A painting lent for a party. Then last autumn they asked if I still remembered my radio.

MOREL (sad) As a boy I built long-wave transmitters in my father's shed in Brittany. I told Lassalle once, at dinner, as a joke.

MOREL (sad) They gave me figures to check. Power, frequency, range. A son et lumière, they said. I wrote my name in the front of the notebook like a schoolboy.

MOREL (angry) I didn't ask what it was for. That's what I was paid for. Not to ask.

INÈS (neutral) And the painting?

MOREL (worried) They need a word under the varnish for their ceremony on the first. I'm a businessman with a diploma, Inès. I can't see what you see. So I gave it to you.

MOREL (sad) You'd find the word, I'd take it to them, and you'd never know. Nobody would be hurt.

INÈS (angry) Gérard Lassalle is in the river. Anselme Roux lost a day.

MOREL (scared) I know! On Friday a young man telephoned. Very polite. He said Lassalle was afraid at the end, and fear is a debt like any other.

MOREL (scared) He hoped I wasn't afraid. They expect the word by Sunday night.

INÈS (thinking) Did you find it?

MOREL (sad) No. I never could.

She believes him. She tells him nothing about the tower, or REMIT, or the R and the E in her notebook.

Four floors down, the street bell rings. A long, polite, unhurried ring. Morel goes the colour of old paper.

Atelier Morel. A minute later.

Even footsteps climb all four flights, and the man who reaches the top has lost no breath. He looks round the room like someone who has paid for it.

On screen: Vaulmont (sixty, silver hair swept back, grey three-piece suit, a silver chain across the waistcoat), Inès (hands in her apron pockets), Morel (shrinking on his stool)

VAULMONT (happy) Madame Varga. Forgive me for coming on a Sunday. Auguste de Vaulmont.

The voice is warm oil, poured slowly. She last heard it under the Barrière d'Enfer, saying Lassalle has been remitted.

INÈS (neutral) Monsieur de Vaulmont.

Her own voice comes out quite normal. She will be proud of that later.

VAULMONT (neutral) I've come for the Allegory. It's needed for the vigil on Wednesday. My people are downstairs with a van.

He stands before the painting, hands behind his back, and looks at the square of blue sky she uncovered.

VAULMONT (happy) You have very good hands. I've owned this picture thirty years and never knew the sky was that colour.

INÈS (surprised) You're the anonymous buyer.

VAULMONT (thinking) The owner. Poor Gérard loved his little fictions. I asked him for the best hands in Paris, and he found them.

VAULMONT (happy) You were in the mountains, I think. The air on the plateau is very clear in March. One can hear bells for miles.

In her apron pockets, her hands close into fists.

VAULMONT (sad) I knew your father a little. Zoltán Varga. He drove the twenty-eight in Lyon, past the Hôtel-Dieu, and sang in Hungarian at red lights. A fine baritone.

VAULMONT (sad) To lose one's days like that, one by one. The cruellest debt is the one we pay without knowing we owe it.

Inès stands quite still. He hasn't said a word a stranger could call a threat. He has simply told her what he holds.

VAULMONT (happy) Your Monsieur Hartley is building us a charming room. I do hope nothing interrupts his contract.

VAULMONT (neutral) And Jean-Paul's affairs are so complicated. This atelier is mortgaged to a bank on whose board I sit.

VAULMONT (happy) We all hold a little of each other's time, Madame Varga. We're all in debt to someone.

He opens his silver watch, the way any man checks the time, and looks into it.

On screen: Vaulmont's watch, open: a white enamel face with no hands

VAULMONT (neutral) Did you find the word?

INÈS (neutral) No.

VAULMONT (thinking) No. I don't suppose you did.

The street door of the atelier. A quarter to twelve.

Two men in grey overalls carry the painting down in blankets. A van waits with its back doors open.

On screen: Vaulmont (buttoning a black overcoat), Inès (in the doorway), the bald man (by the van, huge and silent, a cuckoo tattoo on his neck)

VAULMONT (happy) Brother Kessler will take it from here. He's very good with fragile things.

Kessler lifts the painting into the van as gently as if it were asleep. Vaulmont gets into a long dark car and is gone.

Inès has forty-one photographs, in the cloud and on a memory stick in her coat. She has nine ticks, an R, an E, and REMIT. She doesn't have the painting.

MOREL (scared) What do I do, Inès?

INÈS (neutral) Go home. Lock your door. Don't drink anything anyone gives you.

A flat in Vincennes, third floor. Sunday, near midnight.

She rang him at ten, at half past and at eleven. Then she rang Dec, who came on a hired bicycle in nineteen minutes. The door wasn't locked.

On screen: Morel (in his armchair, hands flat on its arms, staring at the wall), Inès (kneeling by the chair), Dec (by the mantelpiece)

On the low table sit a cup and saucer, with a finger's depth of cold tea.

On screen: Every clock in the room at nine: the carriage clock, the kitchen clock, the oven, the cable box frozen at 09:00

INÈS (scared) Jean-Paul. Can you hear me?

His mouth moves.

MOREL (neutral) Sunday.

His tongue is grey, much darker than Roux's.

Dec is already on the phone to the emergency services, spelling the address twice in his terrible French.

INÈS (sad) They aren't angry with him. It's housekeeping. A debt that stopped being useful, written off.

DEC (worried) And now they've one restorer left.

INÈS (scared) Me.

Dec rings off. The next number in his phone says Commissaire Pichon.

DEC (thinking) Pichon would want to know about this.

INÈS (worried) Pichon answers to the prefect. And the prefect is on Fabien's list, Dec. He's drinking the wine on Wednesday.

DEC (thinking) Pichon also fed me a cassoulet and let me go.

In the armchair, Morel stares at the wall. On the mantelpiece the carriage clock says nine and stays there.

What do you choose?

  • Take everything to Pichon, prefect or no prefect
  • Trust no one in uniform: stop the vigil yourselves
