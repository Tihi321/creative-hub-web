Frozen Hours

The chamber of the Debt Clock. Friday night, eight o'clock.

Black iron cage, black beams. Snow sifts through the slits and settles without melting.

On screen: Dec (against the wall, knees up, lips going blue), Inès (beside him, coat buttoned to the chin, frost on her glasses)

Dec spent half an hour flat on the boards with his thinnest pick, then two picks tied with a bootlace. The padlock sits on the far side of the trap.

DEC (sad) Give me a keyhole and I'll open anything. They didn't give me a keyhole. They gave me a floor.

He kills his phone torch to save the battery. Hers died at five past four.

Inès mutters something long and vicious in Hungarian.

DEC (happy) Say the one with the horse again.

INÈS (neutral) It isn't about a horse. It's about your mother and a horse. I'm sorry. I don't know your mother.

DEC (happy) She'd love you. She'd be terrified of you.

The chamber, later. Pitch dark.

On screen: Inès (eyes closing, head drooping), Dec (turning toward her voice)

Minus twenty, Fabien said. Her fingers won't bend. She feels warm and heavy and sleepy, and some old part of her knows that is the bad sign.

INÈS (scared) Talk to me. You always talk. Don't let me sleep.

DEC (worried) Right. Right. I've a story about a door in Salthill.

Mrs Kilbride, a stuck mortice, a cat called Pádraig and a sandwich. He tells it in the dark, with both arms.

DEC (happy) ...and Pádraig had the sandwich the whole time. Your turn. Tell me something true.

She is too cold to decide not to.

INÈS (sad) My father lost days. Zoltán. He drove a bus in Lyon for thirty years and knew every street.

INÈS (sad) He taught me to swear in Hungarian. And to look at things. Ne csak nézd, lásd. Don't just look. See.

INÈS (sad) At the end he'd wake up in nineteen ninety and dress for work. Every morning I told him, and every morning he was frightened.

INÈS (angry) These people do it on purpose. Like taking a coin out of a fountain. And they call it a religion.

INÈS (neutral) That's why I came.

Dec blows into his hands, twice.

DEC (sad) I'm sorry. Mine dropped dead in the van, outside a chipper on Shop Street. I was in Dublin, wearing a lanyard, because the shop was a bit small for me.

DEC (sad) I wasn't there. So I ran his shop into the ground, and then I started locking people in rooms. Because I couldn't get out of one.

INÈS (neutral) You're not that. I watched you open that lock without the word. You're the most stubborn person I've ever met, and I've met my mother.

He leans against her shoulder, and the shivering stops.

DEC (thinking) We'll not mention any of this. After.

INÈS (neutral) Never.

DEC (happy) Grand.

The chamber. Half past ten.

On screen: Inès (suddenly sitting up), Dec (fumbling for the phone)

INÈS (surprised) Dec. The torch. Point it up.

Through a square hole in the ceiling the beam finds the bell, huge and green-black, the iron hammer poised beside it. Letters run round its lip.

On screen: The rim of the bell: HORAM PVLSAT QVI TRAHIT

Inès laughs, short and cracked.

INÈS (happy) The hour is struck by whoever winds. Trahere, to draw up the weight. Whoever holds the clock holds the time.

INÈS (thinking) Dec. Can you make this go?

The great stone weight hangs on its chain into the dark. Beside the frame, a square iron shaft, and on the floor a crank some clock-keeper left four hundred years ago.

DEC (happy) Ah, now. Ah, now.

The chamber. Winding.

On screen: Dec (hands over hers on the crank), Inès (glasses steamed, hair damp)

Every turn takes everything they have. After fifty she isn't cold. After a hundred she's sweating. In the cage a ratchet goes clack, clack, like a huge slow heart.

DEC (thinking) This is the striking weight. When it's up, I pull this lever and she strikes. Hold the lever and she keeps striking.

INÈS (angry) Then don't let go.

He reaches into the cage up to the shoulder. She nods. He pulls.

BONG.

The note hits them in the teeth and the ribs, and snow drops off the beams in a cloud. Inès claps her hands over her ears and laughs.

Seven. Eight. Nine. Out through the slits, down the valley, over the sleeping farms.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

Thirteen.

Dec lets go. The silence rings.

The chamber. After.

On screen: Inès (sitting against the cage, shaking), Dec (holding the light)

Her hand touches paper, wedged under the bottom bar of the frame. The winding has shaken it out an inch. It is a leaf of parchment with a charred edge, a page the sisters never managed to burn.

On screen: The ledger page: nine little seals numbered I to IX: a key, a wheel, a closed eye, scales, a bell, a ladder, an open hand, a mouth, a crown. A Latin title beside each.

INÈS (thinking) I. Custos Horae Ultimae. The Keeper of the Last Hour.

On screen: Beside the open hand: VII. Exactor

INÈS (scared) The Collector.

DEC (worried) The Seventh will collect.

INÈS (scared) They aren't people. They're offices, like a government. Somebody holds each one. Today.

Far down the valley a high, angry engine is coming. And sirens.

The chamber of the Debt Clock. Twenty past eleven.

Bolt-cutters bite through the padlock. Up comes the volunteer fire brigade of Les Bréviaires in yellow helmets, all talking at once. The bell hasn't rung since sixteen ten.

On screen: Clothilde (snowmobile jacket over the red cardigan, a thermos in hand), Inès and Dec (wrapped in foil like sandwiches)

CLOTHILDE (happy) Well. You've woken the dead. I suppose that's something.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) Drink. Don't argue.

Coffee and plum brandy, half and half. It goes down like a lit match.

A fire brigade Land Rover on the valley road. Near midnight.

On screen: Dec (phone in hand), Inès (half asleep against the window)

Dec's phone, warm from his skin, has six per cent. He scrolls back to the very first photograph, enlarges it and holds it out to her.

On screen: The inside cover of the notebook, and in the top corner in small slanted blue biro: J.-P. Morel

Inès knows the hand. Four years of yellow notes on her bench. Inès, client called again, voilà, could you possibly...

INÈS (sad) Our restorer is close to the word.

INÈS (sad) Not me. Him.

She watches the snow all the way down.

Room four at Le Balancier. Saturday, four in the afternoon.

A white quilt, red geraniums on the sill, a travel clock by the bed and a cuckoo clock by the door. Roux's drawings are spread over the bed.

On screen: Dec (lying on the quilt, turning nine wheels in his head)

Inès is up at the museum with the brown page and a Latin dictionary the size of a breeze block. At breakfast they talked about the weather for twenty minutes, very carefully.

A knock. The corridor is empty. On the floor sits a tray: a white teapot, a cup, two biscuits and a card.

On screen: The card: Avec les compliments de la maison, pour vous réchauffer.

DEC (happy) Ah, Madame Jobin. You're a dote.

The tea is hot and spiced. He drinks half the cup in one go.

Then he stops with the cup halfway to the saucer.

On screen: Dec (very still, cup in hand)

Something faint lies under the spice, sweet and dusty. Behind it sits something flat and mineral, like cold wet stone, at the back of his tongue. His tongue feels a size too big.

DEC (thinking) Madame Jobin never leaves a tray. She stays and asks about the honeymoon. Nobody stayed.

DEC (thinking) Ah, stop. It's the house blend. The cold's got into your head.

The pot is still nearly full, and he is still cold to the bone.

What do you choose?

  • Cloves and slate, like Roux: spit it out and play dead
  • It's only the house's spiced tea: finish the cup
