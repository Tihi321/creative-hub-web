The Keeper's Son

The dinghy came in under the lee of Little Farrow, where the rock shelved down to a slip cut into the stone, and Mara shipped the oars and let the swell carry her the last few feet. The island rose in black tiers, gullied and wet. Above the slip a path of loose scoria climbed toward the weather station. Beyond it, on the higher ground of the next island over, the lighthouse stood white and patient with its lamp dark.

She dragged the dinghy above the tide line and made the painter fast to a ring bolt set in the rock. Old iron, red with rust, worn bright at the eye where a hundred painters had been made fast before hers. That small brightness bothered her. Somebody had been coming and going here long after the station was supposed to be dead.

By the time she reached the top she was warm under her coat and breathing hard. The station was a low complex of concrete and timber built to be useful and never to be liked: a main hut, a store, a radio room, and at the seaward end the beacon tower with its glass lantern and its steel leg running down into a concrete footing. The turbine on its mast turned slowly, and would turn whether or not anyone was watching. The windows were dark. But in the lee of the main hut a door stood propped open with a brick, and from inside came a thin yellow light and the crackle and warble of a radio set doing something it had no business doing in a place like this.

She knocked on the door frame with two knuckles and stepped in out of the wind.

The room was warm and close and smelled of paraffin and hot dust and solder. Shelves lined the walls, and the shelves were lined with radios, some of them older than she was, their cases scratched and their dials worn smooth. A workbench ran the length of one wall under a hanging lamp. On it a man sat with his back to her, hunched over something small and bright, a soldering iron in his hand. Valve gear stood around him in stacks, humming their low warm hum. A kettle ticked on a paraffin stove, and a cat slept on a folded coat as though the whole world had been arranged for its convenience.

"You're the engineer," the man said, without turning. "From the tug."

"I'm Mara Kell." She stayed by the door. "You've got my frequency."

"I've got everybody's frequency." He set the iron down in its rest and turned. A man of about her own age, broad weathered face, hair that needed cutting, and eyes with the look of someone who spent a great deal of time looking at small things. "Idris Vane. I'd shake your hand but I've got solder on it." He held up the hand in question, thumb and forefinger bright with it, and grinned in a way that was almost apologetic, then let the grin go, too soon, the way a man lets go of a thing he is not sure he is allowed to hold.

"You keep the station?" Mara asked.

"I keep the radios. Station's been decommissioned since before the flood. But the sets in here, some of them, they'll still pull in anything that's on the air. It's a hobby. A vice, my father would have said. Kettle's on."

She should have said no. She had a job to do and a clause hanging over her, and a man who kept radios in a dead station was the first interesting thing she had found on the island. She said yes.

He made the tea strong and did not ask her how she took it, and she did not correct him, because he had it right. They drank it standing, because there was only one stool and neither of them sat in it. The wind worked at the roof and the radios hummed.

"You'll be here about the beacon," Idris said at last.

"I'm here to certify it. It's been throwing a fault. The insurers won't open the lane while it does."

"Ah." He turned his cup a quarter turn on the bench. "The lane."

"You know the lane?"

"Everybody here knows the lane. It's been shut three years. The big ships go round the long way now and the small ones don't come at all." He said it lightly, and then he said nothing for a while, and the nothing went on a beat too long. "So they've sent you to fix the beacon."

"To silence it. It's a navigation hazard, apparently."

"Is it." He said the two words to his tea rather than to her, and something moved behind his face and was put away again.

Mara let the silence sit. Her brother Dan had taught her that, in a caravan in Donegal with the rain on the roof while he tapped his knuckles on the table and waited for her to get the letters right. People and radios are the same, he had told her. You learn more from the gaps than the tones. So she drank her tea and let the gap Idris had left do its work.

"Will you show me the beacon?" she asked at last. "You're here. You know the gear."

He stood up so quickly that the cat lifted its head. "I'll take you. Bring your lamp."

Mara walked around the beacon twice before she touched it, the way she walked around every machine twice, looking at it before she asked it anything. The wind was cold enough now to make her eyes water. Idris stood with his hands in his pockets and watched her, and there was a tension in his watching that she could not place.

The access panel came open in a minute. The inside was neat, too neat; someone had been here and been careful to leave no trace of having been. The turbine turned, the bank of batteries under the footing was charged and its terminals clean and greased. All the power that could be relied on out here, where the cold Labrador Current met the warm Gulf Stream and made its fog, came from that wind and that sun and those batteries, and all of it was in better order than a decommissioned station had any right to be.

"The panel's clean and the lamp's clean," she said, half to herself. "But there's a draw on this circuit that doesn't match the lamp. Something's pulling power that isn't the beacon. How much does the lamp want?"

"Two hundred watts, when it ran."

"And it's pulling nearer four." She traced the trunking down the tower leg with her fingers and found the second cable, a thin one, older and more careful than the rest, spliced in with a neatness that spoke of somebody who knew what they were doing. It ran down the footing and into the rock. "Where does this go?"

"The vault," Idris said. "The old cable vault, underneath. It's where the station's lines came in from the landing."

"It's flooded, I was told."

"It is." He said it shortly, and then, because he was a man who could not help filling a silence, he added, "The pump failed a year ago and nobody was sent to fix it. Or nobody came."

"A year ago." Mara looked at the cable. Not just spliced in. Live. "Somebody's been feeding this."

"The station looks after itself," Idris said. "The wind and the sun. It was built to." He shifted his feet on the rock. "You'll want to see the fault record. It's in the office. I'll get it."

He was already turning away, and she let him go, because she had learned something in those last few seconds, though she could not yet have told you what. Only that the thin cable mattered to him, and that he had not wanted her to look at it, and that he was a bad liar the way a bad bearing is bad: not silently, but with noise that anyone who knew the sound would hear.

The office was one room with two desks and a wall of grey filing cabinets and a chart of the Farrow Islands, yellow at the edges, pinned up over the radiator. Idris was in the cabinet with his sleeve pushed up and his arm in to the elbow, and he was in it longer than a man looking for a fault record should be.

"Here." He laid a buff folder on the desk and stood back from it as though it might bite. "Station log and fault reports. Last year's."

The fault report was thin. A page of it, dated a year back, in blue ink in a careful hand. Beacon showing intermittent fault on test at 22:00. Signal audible on band at 4.8 MHz. Reported to contractor by telephone. No reply. Mara read it twice. "Reported to which contractor?"

"Whoever holds the maintenance contract. The office in St. John's." He said it carefully and flatly, like a man walking on ice he is not sure of.

She turned the page. The next entry was dated the same day, later, and it was in the same careful hand and a different pen. Pulse confirmed. Running to its old schedule. Will hold. And a third entry, three days after, in pencil now, the letters unsteady: Still holding. Somebody has to.

"You wrote these," Mara said.

"I keep the station's records. It's my job."

"Your job is the radios."

"It was my father's job to keep the station, and then it wasn't a station, and then it was only mine." He took the folder from her, not roughly, but as though the paper were hot in her hand, and shut it and put it away, and when he came back he stood with one hand flat on the desk. "The pulse has been transmitting for years. Longer than the fault record. Longer than the lane's been shut."

The wind leaned on the window and let go. "How long is years?" Mara asked.

But Idris was already shaking his head, and his evasiveness had in it now something that was not evasion at all but fear. "I'm not the person to ask," he said. "I'm only the radios."

"You're the only person here." She kept her voice level. "You knew the pulse was on that band before I told you. You knew it was running to a schedule. That's not a radio hobby. That's knowing."

He gave no answer to that. He turned to the window and stood looking out at the dark, and when he spoke again his voice had gone down and gone careful, as though he were reading from a page he had written a long time ago and never meant to read aloud. "My father kept this station for twelve years after the lane shut. He'd been keeper here before that, and when the weather service pulled out he stayed on the beacon contract, and he was glad of it, because he'd nowhere else to be. Then one winter he went up to the light on the hill to check the lamp, and he fell on the stair." He said it evenly, and the evenness was worse than grief. "The inquest said he slipped. He'd gone up and down that stair twice a day in weather that would take the roof off, and he slipped."

"Idris."

"He's on the hill. The lighthouse." Idris nodded at the dark of the window. "Twelve years. And there's no weather station any more and no keeper, but I keep his radios warm, because that's what he asked me." He turned from the window. His eyes were dry and his hands were not quite steady. "So when you ask me how long the pulse has run, I'll tell you I don't know, and what I mean is that I know what I can prove and nothing more, and I've had years to learn that proving a thing and surviving the proving are two different jobs."

Mara stood with the fault record between them. She had come to the island with a clasp knife and a torch and no expectation of finding anything but a bad oscillator, and she found instead a dead keeper, a thin live cable and a man who had been carrying all of it alone. "The vault," she said. "That's where the cables come in. If the beacon's got a draw that doesn't belong, the vault's where I'll find the reason."

"It is." He looked at her, and his eyes were steady and unhappy, with nothing left in them of the joking man who had apologized for the solder. "But not tonight. It's a flooded space and the tide's wrong for it and I won't take you down there in the dark. Go back to your boat. Come back at the right water."

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow." He paused. "And Ms. Kell. When you talk to your contractor. The contract. Don't put too much in it. Some things, you write them down, and somebody reads them, and then things happen to the people who wrote them. I'd rather you didn't write down his name where anyone can read it."

She went out into the wind. Halfway down the path she remembered her torch and went back, and through the window she saw him at the bench with the fault folder open under the lamp, not reading it, only sitting with his hand flat on the cover.

The crossing back was hard work. She rowed into the wind with her teeth set and the salt running into the corners of her mouth, and she did not think about Idris Vane or his father or the careful blue handwriting until she had the painter made fast and the deck under her boots.

On the Steadfast she put the kettle on and the radio at her elbow, because that was the shape of her days, and she was drying her hands on a towel when the set crackled.

"Steadfast, this is Rourke." Salt-flat and unhurried, folded small and put in an envelope. "You went ashore."

Mara put the towel down. "I did. The fault's on the supply side. I'll have a diagnosis for you by tomorrow."

"That's not what I'm asking." A pause. "Did you speak to anyone on the island?"

Mara looked at the log open on the chart table with its last entry in her square hand, and she heard herself say, "There's a man who keeps the station's old radios. Idris Vane. He showed me the beacon."

There was a quiet on the line that went on one second, two, and it was a different quiet from any Sella had used before. "Vane," she said. "I see." And then, in the same even voice, "I'll remind you again of the secure-the-site clause. Anything you find, anything you're told, I hear first, in writing, before anyone else in the world. There are people in this business who ask too many questions and I've had to let them go." She said it the way she said everything, as a fact already decided and only now being told to you. "Report to me in the morning. Rourke out."

The line clicked. The static came back. Mara stood with her hand on the cold rim of the set.

She had told Sella about the beacon and nothing else. Not the thin live cable spliced into the trunking. Not the fault record and the careful blue ink. Not the boy who had apologized for the solder and asked her not to write down a dead man's name. And Sella had heard the name Vane and gone quiet, and the quiet had not been the quiet of a woman hearing news for the first time. It had been the quiet of a woman checking that her arrangements were still where she had left them.

Mara poured her tea. It was too strong, and she drank it anyway, at the wheelhouse glass, with the deck lifting under her and the island dark and patient to the east and the lighthouse burning white on the hill.

At 20:41, the tide close to its last of the flood, the pulse came again. The four tones, as machine-cut as before, the small needle jumping, the tide's shadow written on the air.

Mara wrote it down in her log, and she wrote nothing in the report.
