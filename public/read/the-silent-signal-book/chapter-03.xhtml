The Flooded Vault

The right water came at 06:12, and Idris was waiting at the slip with two lamps, a coil of line, and a face that had not slept.

"You're early," Mara said, shipping the oars.

"You're earlier." He made the painter fast without being asked. His hands were steady. That told her he had decided something in the night.

They climbed the path in a grey light with no sun in it. Below the station the sea was pulling back, uncovering the shelf and the black teeth of the old cable run that had been drowned at high water.

The vault entrance was a concrete mouth in the rock, half hidden by storm weed. A steel door had been dogged shut and then, later, undogged. The dogs were bright with use. Idris worked the wheel with both hands and the door came open with a groan that went down into the rock and came back changed.

The smell was cold and mineral and old. Wet concrete, rust, and under that the sweetness of cable insulation gone soft. Mara put her lamp inside and the beam found a stair going down into black water.

"How deep?" she said.

"Deep enough that you don't want to be down there when the tide turns. Twelve metres by eight metres, cable heads on the far wall, a metre of water over the floor at low water. There's a ledge. The old switchgear stands on it. That's where the pulse comes from."

"You've been down."

"Once. To see what my father was doing. I didn't stay."

They went down together. The water was over Mara's boots and then over her knees, and the cold closed around her like a hand. The chamber opened out. Her beam found the cable heads in rusted ranks, and the ledge above the waterline, and on the ledge a grey steel cabinet with its door open and its guts showing.

"Don't touch the busbars," Idris said. "There's still a charge in the batteries, and the trunking back to the beacon's live."

"I know it's live." Mara waded to the ledge and set her lamp down. What was feeding it was a transmitter. Not a big one. A marine band set, older than the ones in the radio room, bolted to a timber plinth and wired into the battery bank through a charge controller that had been hand-built and hand-labelled. The wiring was loomed and tied, every joint soldered and sleeved, and on the plinth's face a strip of tape carried three words in careful blue ink.

For the tide.

She crouched over the plinth and followed the wiring with her lamp. "This isn't a fault. This is a beacon of its own. Your father built a second transmitter down here and ran it off the station's batteries, and it keys itself off the tide. There's a float switch in the sump. The tide rises, the float lifts, the switch makes, and the set fires." She looked up. "That's why the gaps between the tones aren't even. The water's not just switching it on and off. The water is the message."

Idris stood with his hands at his sides. "He used to say the sea keeps better time than any man and doesn't take a bribe. He built it the year the lane shut. I was away at school on the mainland. I came home for the winter and there was a cable running down into the vault and he wouldn't say what for."

"And the five seconds of it. Three rising tones and a flat note. That's not a call sign."

"No. It's a key. He showed me once, the year before he died. He said, it's not a signal to be heard, it's a signal to be read. Four tones to a group, and the group makes a number. The height of the water when the set fires is the digit, because he set the float to fire only above a mark and he can read the height off his own gauge. And the gap before the group is where the group sits in the message, because a gap is coarse and slow and it will still come through on a bad night. The tide is the number. The gap is the place." He swallowed. "I got that much from him and no more. I was nineteen and I wanted to be back at school. I told him it was a hobby gone strange. He said, no, son, it's a lock. And then he died, and I couldn't read it."

Mara was quiet a moment. On the water she had thought the gaps themselves were the tide's whole message, the shape of the rise and fall written on the air. That had been a true reading and a partial one, the way a first look at a strange machine is true and partial both. The gaps were only the clock and the place. The heights were the words.

"It spells coordinates," she said. "Or it spells something in numbers."

"What's the difference, out here?" Idris looked at the tape on the plinth. "He'd write them on the tide table when he got a run of them. A whole night's worth. And then he'd burn the table." He reached inside his coat and brought out a folded square of paper, soft with handling. "I didn't burn mine."

They sat on the dry ledge with their backs to the wet wall and worked it out by lamplight.

Idris unfolded the paper. The tide table was for Little Farrow, a year old, with a column of numbers pencilled down the margin and a second column in a smaller hand beside it. "These are the tide heights at the pulse times. He set the float so the set only fires when the water's over a certain mark. So every pulse is at a known height, and the height's the digit. High water spring, high water neap, midway, low. The first group of the night is always the start of the message. He'd wait for the tide and then run the whole message all night, one group at a time, and the rest of the night was repeat."

"So a full night's worth of pulses is the whole message."

"A full night's worth on the spring tide. The other nights the tide doesn't get high enough for the set to fire. That's why you only hear five seconds and not the rest. You were hearing a fragment."

Mara took the paper and turned it so the lamp fell on it. She read the first group off his margin, checked it against the height he had written and the place in the run, and did it again because she did not trust herself the first time.

"Sixty-one," she said. "Then sixty-two. Then forty-three."

"Degrees and minutes. North."

"And then west." She worked down the page until the numbers assembled themselves into a place she could put her finger on. "That's a position. Two points off the Farrow Sound, in about ninety metres of water. There shouldn't be anything in ninety metres of water there. That's a clear channel."

"There isn't," Idris said. His voice had gone very quiet. "There's a wreck."

Mara looked at the tape on the plinth, For the tide, and thought of a man sitting in a flooded vault with a tide table and a pencil, one group a night, for years. "Why coordinates? If you've found a wreck in a shipping lane, why not write a letter?"

"Because they sank it." Idris said it to the wall. "Somebody put a ship down in the lane and the lane closed and the insurers paid out and everybody was very sorry, and my father found out, and he went to the office in St. John's with what he knew, and they gave him a contract to keep the station. And he came home and he didn't write a letter. He built a lock instead. You don't telegraph a thing like that. You hide it where the water will keep it. And you tell one person where it is."

"Who did he tell?"

Idris's jaw worked. "Me. And then he went up the hill to the light, and he didn't come down. They found him at the foot of the lighthouse stair. The inquest said he fell. Twelve years he'd gone up and down that stair twice a day in weather that would take the roof off, and suddenly he fell." He said it evenly, and the evenness was worse than grief. "The morning before, he told me the vault was safe and the lock was set, and don't open it for anybody who comes asking. And the next night there was a man from the office at the door, very polite, very sorry for my trouble, and he asked whether my father had left any papers. And I said no. And he looked at me the way a man looks at a door he means to come back to." Idris folded the tide table along its old creases. "I've kept the lock set ever since. Three years. Since then I've wondered whether that was a brave thing or a coward's thing, most nights of the week."

Mara did not tell him it was brave. He would not have believed her. She put her hand flat on the cold concrete between them, where he could see it, and left it there.

"The rest of the message," she said. "After the coordinates."

"More numbers, and then the flat note, and then it starts again. I never got past the position. Every spring tide I get the same coordinates, and then the tide's down and the set's quiet till the next spring. It'd take a full night's watch, and the last one I could have done it, my father's old dory went on the rocks and I had no boat to get the tide table back from the mainland." He looked at her. "You'd have to sit the night in the radio room and log every pulse and every gap and not sleep."

"You've a tide table now. When's the next spring?"

"Tonight's the third night before it. It comes on the fourth." He waited, and she could see him not asking.

"Then I'll sit it with you. Tonight we get what we can off the top of the tide. Four nights from now we take the whole message."

Something went out of his shoulders then, a tightness he had carried so long it had set in him like a knot in timber. "Thank you," he said, and then, because he was Idris, "you'll want a better chair than the one in the office. It's got a list to port."

They were climbing the stair, wet to the thigh, when they heard the engine.

It came from the northeast, a hard busy note under the wind, and it was not the note of a fishing boat. Around the shoulder of Great Farrow a launch came into view, grey and long and new, with a rubber boat slung on its foredeck and a stack of gear under a tarpaulin aft.

Idris stood beside her with his lamp hanging from his hand. "That's the Rourke tender. The Petrel. She's been up and down the Sound all summer and never come in. She's coming in now."

"Go up to the station. Put the tide table where it won't be found and put the vault door to. Don't say one word about the transmitter. You're the man who keeps the radios. Nothing else."

"And what are you?"

"The engineer who's here to silence a beacon. Which is what I'm going to be."

Sella Rourke came ashore dry.

She came up the slip with her coat buttoned against weather that was not touching her, and behind her came four of her crew carrying the rubber boat's load up the rock in two trips. Two aluminium dive cylinders per trip, a crate of tools, a coil of line, and a long pelican case with a cutting torch's regulator chained to its lid. They set it down on the concrete apron, and one of them, a thin man with a shaved head, began sorting the tools on a cloth with the neatness of long practice.

Sella stopped in front of Mara and looked at her trousers, wet to the thigh, and smiled. It was a good smile. It had been practised.

"Ms. Kell. You've been wading."

"There's a flooded vault under the station. The beacon's supply runs through it. I needed to see the junction."

"You told me your diagnosis was a supply fault. I didn't know it required a bath." Sella turned to Idris, who stood by the door with his hands in his pockets, and her eyes went over him the way a surveyor runs a level over ground. "And Mr. Vane. The keeper's son. I've heard a great deal about your family."

"I doubt that."

"Oh, people talk. About the beacon and the lane and the old keeper, who was a great loss. Twelve years ago, wasn't it. A fall." She turned back to Mara before he could answer, and the turning was the answer. "I've brought a site inspection team. Insurers' requirement. Any decommissioned installation the lane reopens past has to be certified secure, top to bottom, including flooded spaces. So we'll be opening that vault ourselves, and I can't have unqualified personnel in the water while we do. For your own safety."

Mara studied the gear on the apron. Inspection divers carried a torch, a slate, and a camera. These were wreck divers: two cylinders each, a reel of line, a cutting torch with its hoses coiled and chained, a crate of tools that included a hydraulic spreader with its jaws still wet from the last job. And the pelican case, whose stencil she could read from where she stood: BURY — 2 OF 4.

"A site inspection with a cutting torch," she said.

"Structures have to be made safe. Doors dogged shut, cables cut." Sella turned to the thin man at the cloth. "Mr. Reeve."

"Ms. Rourke."

"The vault will want pumping. Have the pump ashore by dark and the water down by morning. Then the cables. I want the beacon circuit cut at both ends and the trunking opened and certified. Anything you find in there that isn't cable, you bring to me. Not to Ms. Kell. To me." She said it without turning round, and the words were for Reeve but the sentence was for Mara.

"Certified," Mara said. "By whom?"

"By me. I'm the contractor of record. That's what the clause means. I sign, I certify, and the lane opens. Nobody else's name goes on it." She stepped closer, and her voice did not change, and that was the thing about her: the smoothness never cracked. "Your contract is for a beacon. Not a vault. Not a conversation with the keeper's son about old times. The beacon goes silent in forty-eight hours, and you'll do nothing else until it does. You will not go down into that vault again, you will not open that fault record again, and you will not send any report anywhere except to me." She brought her eyes back. "I'd hate to think you were paid to do one job and doing another. It would make the contract irregular. And when a contract's irregular, Ms. Kell, people get let go."

The wind took a scatter of grit off the concrete and rattled it against the beacon's steel leg.

"Forty-eight hours," Mara said.

"Forty-eight." Sella walked back to the apron and said something that made Reeve laugh, and the laugh carried on the wind the way laughter does in a place that has not heard much of it.

Idris had not moved from the door. His face was the face he used for strangers, and only his eyes were wrong, going from Sella to the cutting torch and back.

At the slip, as she got the oars into the dinghy, he said, not looking at her, "She's going to open the vault and clear it out. The transmitter, the tape, all of it. By morning it'll be scrap on a tender and the lock's broken and my father's twelve years are a bad fall and nothing more."

"Not by morning. By morning the water's down. That's all. Then they open the trunking and certify. That's two days, Idris. She gave us two days without knowing it. She thinks the clock's hers."

"It isn't?"

"No." She put her hand on the gunwale and shoved off, and called back across the widening water, "The clock's the tide's. It always was."

She rowed out with her back to the island and her eyes on the Steadfast and the grey Petrel beyond her. Two days from Sella, and the vault would have its water down and its secrets lifted out and laid on an apron for a woman in a buttoned coat. Four nights to the spring tide, and Mara did not need four nights. She had a set in a dead station and a pencil and a tide table, and the sea was already turning toward the high water that would make the next group. The transmitter would tell her the rest of Dan Kell's message before the sun came up, and she meant to be in the radio room to hear it.
