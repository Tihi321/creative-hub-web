The Clause

The station office smelled of paraffin and wet wool. Sella had set a lamp on the desk and a man at the door, and she had squared her folder to the edge of the wood with a pencil laid parallel to it. Behind her the wind worked at the window. Above them, somewhere, the anemometer turned and told nobody.

Mara stood with her fingers curled over the back of the chair. She did not sit.

"You'll want dry clothes," Sella said.

"I want my boat back and my crew paid off and my name off your sheet."

"The Steadfast is chartered to my company until the survey's closed. You'll have her. What you won't have, Ms. Kell, is a clean invoice, because you broke the terms you signed." Sella opened the folder. "Sit down. This is a conversation. It will be shorter if we both behave like professionals."

Mara sat. Her knees were still cold from the dive and the chair took her weight badly.

Sella turned the folder so it faced Mara. A single sheet, typed, with a line at the bottom and a pen already uncapped beside it. Mara read it twice. The first time she read the words. The second time she read what the words would do.

"Addendum to the salvage agreement," Sella said. "It acknowledges that all materials recovered from the site and its adjacent waters, including the wreck designated in Annex A, are the property of the claim, and that you, as master and engineer, took possession of salvage in good faith and surrendered it intact. It further records that you are not able to attest to the wreck's condition, having dived without survey authorisation, and that your evidence on the matter is therefore limited to the fact of recovery." She smoothed the paper with two fingers. "Sign it, and the stolen-launch matter goes away, and the trespass matter goes away, and the unauthorised salvage matter goes away. You finish the survey. You cut the beacon circuit as contracted. You take your money and your boat home."

"And I'm the one who says under my hand that I don't know what was in the hull."

"You say you can't attest to it. That's the beauty of it. You're not lying. You were down there without authorisation, in the dark, in ninety metres. Any honest engineer would say the same. The court would say the same." Sella's voice did not change. It carried the flat weight she gave to clauses. "This is what the secure-the-site clause was always for, Ms. Kell. It wasn't a formality and you knew it wasn't, and you signed it anyway, and now you're standing inside it. Findings reported through one channel. No discussion with any other party. You're not being asked to lie. You're being asked to file."

The lamp ticked. Mara looked at the pen and thought about a shaped charge, and a wall of plating folded inward like a hand closing.

"No."

"That's the wrong answer, and you know it's not the last one."

"It's my answer."

Sella sat back. She looked past Mara at the man on the door, and she nodded once, and the man went out into the passage.

"You are on an island you were contracted to survey, in possession of a launch that was chained, having removed property from a wreck under claim." Sella counted them on the desk with the end of the pencil, tap, tap, tap. "Trespass. Theft. Interference with salvage under claim. In the morning I can put a call out on the emergency channel. Little Farrow has no constable, but St. John's has a good many, and a cutter, and a genuine interest in a theft. It's storm season. The cutter would take a day to get here and a day to get you back, and in those two days a great deal of equipment can be properly secured." She put the pencil down. "You're not a criminal, Ms. Kell. You're tired and you're grieving and you have decided it's all one thing. Sign, and I will keep it from being one thing."

The door opened behind her. Idris came in with a man's hand at each shoulder, and his coat half off one arm, and his jaw set in a way Mara had not seen on him. "That paper's a confession," he said.

"Mr. Vane," Sella said. "You're not a party to this contract. Sit down and be quiet, or stand there and be quiet."

"You can't hold a man in a room and call it quiet."

"Reeve," Sella said.

Reeve put Idris in the second chair. He did it without force, in the way a man moves a table, and Idris sat, and there was a bright line of anger under the red on his cheeks, and Mara watched his hands and willed him to keep them still. He did not.

"What did they pay you for, Mrs. Rourke?" Idris said. "For the burying. Whose number was on it?"

Sella looked at him a moment, then looked back at Mara. "You want to know what's on the tape, Mr. Vane. I don't have to guess at it. I've heard what there is to hear." She drew the folder an inch closer to her own side of the desk. "There's a ship named, and a man's voice giving an order, and enough of one night to sink this company's survey division and the claim with it. It says the sinking was no weather. It says other things." Her eyes came up. "I have known what was on that tape since before it was raised. You may take that how you like. My employers have paid for two things in this matter. The first was a channel kept closed. It stayed closed for three years, because a beacon made a bad noise and no insurer would risk a hull on a bad noise. You were hired to make the noise stop. That was the job, and that was all the job was meant to be, and then it was not."

Mara felt the floor of the room settle under her, the way a deck settles when the weight shifts and the bilge takes it. Before it was raised, Sella had said. The recorder had come up off the bottom in Mara's own hands at four that morning, and Sella's men had taken it off her at the slip at five, and no machine on the island had read it in between. Sella was bluffing, or Sella was talking about something that had been written down before the capsule was ever opened, and Mara found she could not tell which of those frightened her more. She filed it, the way she filed everything, and kept her face still.

"And the second thing they paid for," Mara said.

Sella looked at her without hurry. "To make sure the first thing stayed done. There were three of us on the sealing, and two of them are dead, and neither of those deaths was a storm. So don't tell me about the cost of the truth, Ms. Kell. The cost of the truth is on my invoice and it is enormous, and I am the only one of us who carries it with a survey plan and a clean signature."

Three of us. Mara turned it over once, slowly. Sella had put her own hand in it and named it out loud, and the two who were dead were dead without a weather report to cover them, and the third was sitting at the desk with a pencil squared to a folder. She marked it and let it lie. There would be a night to think about it, and a day after that, and she meant to be alive for both.

Reeve shifted his feet. Idris said nothing at all, which was worse than anything he could have said.

"Now," Sella said, and pushed the sheet a little toward Mara. "The pen."

Mara made herself look at it. A typed page. A line. A beacon circuit is a simple thing, she thought. You can cut it at both ends, and the man who cuts it thinks the circuit is dead. But a transmitter in a vault is not a circuit; it is a switch, and a switch that's wired to a battery does what it's told no matter who holds the fuse key. She thought that the tide table was in Idris's coat, behind the distribution board, unless they had found it. She thought that a storm was coming up the Sound behind that pressure drop, three hundred miles wide and dragging a low behind it, and a storm did not care about a man at a door.

She thought that Sella wanted this signed before the cutter could be called, which meant Sella did not want the cutter called.

"I'd want to read it again," Mara said. "In the morning. With the light."

"You have the light now."

"You've been up as long as I have, and I've been ninety metres down. I'm not signing a page I can't read because my hands are shaking and there's a man watching the door." She let her voice come out flat and tired. That part came easy. "If the answer is no you get it in the morning. If the answer is yes you get it in the morning. Either way I want it in daylight and I want my boat's papers in my hand and I want to hear what I'm getting for what you're taking."

Sella considered her. A long, level consideration, like a gauge read twice.

"Twenty-four hours," she said. "Six tomorrow evening I call the cutter or I don't. You will stay on this island until I have your answer. You will not go near the vault, the regulator's house, or the beacon circuit. You will not use a radio for any purpose other than the survey channel." She closed the folder and stood. "And Idris Vane stays with my crew. For his own safety, and yours. A man who keeps old radios is a man who talks, and I find I'd rather he didn't."

Idris stood up too fast and the chair went back and Reeve had him by the arm and the shoulder before he had gone a step. "Mara," he said. "Mara. Don't you dare sign that page. My father died so that a man like her would have to do a thing like this out in the open." Reeve pulled him around toward the passage, and Idris turned his head back with his coat dragging. "The regulator's house is the deepest cupboard on the island," he said, very clearly, into the room. "The deep cupboards keep what they're given. That's all I'll say. That's all."

Reeve took him out. The door stayed open and the wind came in and the lamp leaned over on its wick.

Sella put on her hat. She looked down at Mara, who had not moved from the chair.

"You should have taken the money," Sella said. "I'd have slept well."

"You wouldn't."

"No," Sella agreed. "But I'd have slept." She went to the door and stood in it with the dark behind her. "Six tomorrow. Bring the pen, Ms. Kell. Bring the page signed, and we will both forget the night. That's the whole of the offer and it will not be made again."

The door shut on the lamp and the wind and the anemometer. Mara sat in the empty room with her hands in her lap. Three of us, she thought. Two dead and no storm in it. She got up and went out to the man on the step and let him walk her down to the water.

They let her go up to the Steadfast at midnight with a lamp and a man behind her, and she stood on the deck and looked down the Sound at the weather building out of the southwest and did what she always did with a thing that had turned against her. She read the gauges.

Sea, running high. A long swell under the chop. The barometer down eleven millibars since the dive and still falling, and the wind not at its quarter yet. Cloud coming on fast and low and ragged, with a shelf to it. Every instrument of her trade telling her the same story and telling it in the same order. Storm before dawn tomorrow, and a big one by evening, the kind that took the top off the Sound and put spray against a lighthouse window.

She went below and set the barograph and stood over it in the dark of the cabin, and the drum turned and the pen drew its slow descent, and she thought it through from the floor up.

The cutter was a day out at best. Two days in a storm, or it would not come at all and Sella would have to find another way to make her afraid. Sella had the recorder in a bag aboard the Petrel, one bag against the whole weather, and a company man who could take it out in a launch and lose it over the deep water before the cutter was past the cape. Sella had Idris, and a man at the door, and words that had already been decided.

And Sella had made one error, and it sat in the middle of the room like a bucket of water. She had said the second payment, and she had said it to a woman whose trade was to know how a system fails.

Mara opened her log and turned it to the back and wrote nothing. She had learned that. The book had ears now. She stood in the dark with the lamp off and put the plan together in her head in the order of an operation.

The regulator's house. The second door. The cut in the concrete, which the station's own drawings called a flood hatch and which nobody had looked at since the water came up. A hatch like that had a ring pull and a hinge and a state it was in, and she could find the state in the dark. The vault was pumped and the transmitter was still bolted to its plinth, wired to the battery bank, and the battery bank did not consult a folder. She did not need to be at the vault. She needed to be under it.

And the transmitter needed a voice. The recorder was in Sella's bag. But the tape was only a recording, and Dan had said two of us or none, and there was a microphone on a marine set that had never once been asked to carry a truth.

The storm came first. The storm heaped over the islands and turned her from a problem to a length of static, and static was a wall in front of every cutter and every call Sella could make, and static cut both ways. A bad night for a launch. A bad night for a man with a bag. A bad night to be standing on a heaving deck with a case you needed to be quit of.

She went up on deck. The man in the storm coat was where the man in the storm coat had been, his back to her, his collar up, and the wind had come round and was starting to bite. Off to the northeast the sky was blacker than the sea and the barometer had stopped being a gauge and become a countdown.

Six tomorrow evening. She had a night, a storm, a hatch, and the words a dead man had spoken and the woman in the hat had listened to before Mara ever heard them. She had no recorder, no light, and no one she could trust with the words.

The steady thing in her, the part that had kept a tugged hull alive through worse weather and had buried her brother and gone back out, said to get it down to brass, then. The storm takes the cutter. The storm covers a launch and covers a second one. The signal is a signal and a signal only needs a wire and a power supply and a place to start from. She had the wire. She had the place. What she did not have was the page that would let her keep her name, and she was not going to sign it.

She let the wind take her coat and looked at the lighthouse hill and the small dark shape of the station under it, and thought of Idris saying the deep cupboards keep what they're given.

"Right," she said, to the barometer and the turning drum, and nobody but the storm heard it. "Twenty-four hours."
