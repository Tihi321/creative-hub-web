The Evidence

Mara came down the ladder with the recorder over her shoulder, feeling for each rung, and the last of the sky closed above her as Idris pulled the hatch and spun the wheel. It rang the shaft like a bell and then they were shut in with the smell of cold iron and old paper.

She got her thumb on the sump valve and turned it three full turns and left it. The water came in steady and unhurried and found its level in the dark.

"They can pull that hatch and come down the ladder at us," she said, "or they can pull it and let the sea in with them. They'll know which."

She set the lamp on the plinth and turned it up. The pump crew had left their mark in the corners: a hose coiled and cut, a gauge with its glass out, boot prints dried to grey rinds on the floor plates. The transmitter stood where the man had bolted it, grey steel, service grade, built by someone who had wanted it to outlive him.

Idris cleared a space on the plinth and set a soldering iron against the battery bank to warm.

Mara unslung the canvas and opened it herself. The lid came up with a wet tearing sound, and the inside smelled of old tape and cold iron. She lifted the head block to check the tape path, and that was when she saw the splice. A clean transverse cut, three-quarters across the spool, the ends butted and held with thin copper foil.

Idris's humming stopped. "That wasn't on the transcript she read out. A bump like that would have gone on the page as a gap."

"It plays across a splice. It's a bump, that's all. But somebody cut this tape and joined it, and the sea doesn't cut tape in a straight line." She turned the spool with her thumb. "One join means they opened the spool to look, and joined it back. Or they spliced two records end to end. One recorder, two voyages."

"Play the whole of it," Idris said. "From the head. We've taken a snatch and stopped every time before this, or read another woman's transcript. This time we take it end to end and do not stop until the spool runs out."

He clipped the recorder's line output to the small amplifier. The battery read eleven point nine and would fall all night. Mara threaded the tape through the guides, freed the stiff take-up reel, reset the counter, and pressed play.

The first voice was not her brother's. A carrier's flat inventory ran under the ship's bell and the weather and the engine note, drums and seals intact, and the watch changed, and another voice cut in.

"Captain. The Levenia is on our port quarter and she's been there since the light failed. She's made no signal."

Her brother's voice. Twelve years dead, and flat and steady as a gauge. Mara put her hand on the plinth and kept it there.

"How far?" another voice asked.

"Half a mile. Closing. She's not showing running lights and she hasn't answered the lamp."

"Get off a flare."

The flare hissed and went up, and nothing answered. Then the hull struck, cast iron on steel, and the speaker overloaded into a hammering roar for six seconds before it cut off clean, and the water came in behind it.

"She's holed us below the waterline. Two frames aft of the engine room." Not shouting, and that was the worst of it. "She didn't shear. She held the contact. She's backed off and putting the lamp on us now."

"Is she offering assistance?"

"She's asking us to stand by and make no signal. That's a steamer that ran us down and now she wants to be our friend."

"Then she wants us quiet. How long have we got?"

"Not an hour. Not if the bulkhead holds."

The man went back and forth to the damage and came back each time with a smaller number. Then her brother's voice again, close, as a man speaks when he thinks he is alone with a machine.

"This is the Amity Rose, sinking in the Farrow Sound, by the running-down of the Levenia, and it was no weather. She came up on us in the squall and she ran us down and she means to let us go, because the sea's tidier than a court. If the boat hears this, the boat hears it on the tide, the way the old man used to work it, and gets it to the air. Not to the office. Not to a file. To the air, with witnesses. Two of you, on the air. A man alone is weather. Two men is a court."

The hull's sound grew. The last of it was the bell, struck slow. Then silence, and the counter ran on, and the head came round and bumped across the recorded seam, and the carrier began reading its inventory again.

Mara pressed stop.

"Two takes," she said. "He recorded it twice. The first with the hull going. The second after the water took the power, on the recorder's own battery. It's all still there."

Idris had gone to the far wall and stood with his palm on the cold stone, and when he spoke it was slowly, and not about the tape. "All these months I have known the hull was cut and known my father's charge made the cut. I made my peace with that the night I told you the rest. What I did not know was the name in the box." He came back to the plinth. "A ship's name in a dead man's mouth is a rumour. A ship's name on a bill of sale is a fact."

Mara's fingers found a waxed tube clipped under the battery. She unrolled a log page on the plinth and weighted it flat with the lamp. Upright, clenched writing: 0200. Levenia four points on the port bow, no lights. 0212. Masthead lamp out. 0230. Struck aft of number two bunker. Signal from Levenia: stand by, make no signal.

Idris turned the page sideways so the light fell across it. "That's not his hand under the last entry." A smaller hand, pressed hard as if the pen were cold. The cutter in her bow port side was rigged forward. Recovered and stowed steel mount, marked as per. Saw to it the office had the ship's name and not my own. The ship above the water, not the ship beneath. Her registry in the box with the tape. — A.V.

"Alun Vane," Idris said, and his voice was not his own. "He wrote the registry in with the tape. He did that, and then he gave the office the name and they killed him for a fall down a stair."

Mara went through the empty case and found a flat oilcloth packet stitched into the lid lining, sealed with wax. She peeled it open on a brass tally and a folded paper, ink gone brown along the creases, a company seal stamped hard at the foot.

Idris read it twice before he said anything. "The tally matches the note in my father's tide table to the numeral. The Levenia, sold in the spring to a company at St. John's, Rourke Salvage and Towage Limited, and Sella Rourke was the signing officer." He set it down very carefully, as though it might go off.

Mara read the log's last line again in the lamplight, and the phrase the small hand had written, and it stopped her the way a steel dogging bar stops a door. The ship above the water, not the ship beneath.

She put her finger on the three facts and held them in a line, and made the shape of it aloud, because saying it was how she saw it.

"The Levenia struck and kept afloat long enough to put her lamp on them, then went down in the same weather," she said. "A ship on the bottom cannot be sold in a spring sale. So what the office sold was not the ship. It was her papers, her registry, her hull lines, her name, no questions asked. Sella's firm raised her, or says she raised her, emptied her, and put a hull to sea under that name with the numbers ground off and the bill to prove she was the thing she wore." She looked up. "It wasn't the Levenia that struck. It was the thing made to wear the Levenia's name. Two of them that night, and only one stayed long enough to offer us a lamp. Alun Vane saw which was which and wrote it plain in a cold room with the sea coming in under him."

Idris put his hand flat on the bill. "And Alun Vane was on the sealing of her. He would have known the certificate did not match the steel. Sella told you there were three, and two are dead." He stopped. "If he was the third—"

"Then Sella was the fourth," Mara said. "I marked it when she said it and let it lie. It can lie a while longer. We cannot prove the sealing from a paper in a box. We can prove the sale and the ship and the voice, and a court can find the rest once a coast officer is in the room with her."

She folded the bill of sale and the log page and put them inside her shirt. "Now I record."

She set her pocket logger beside the big recorder. "Four minutes, maybe five. We say it once and we say it well." She pressed record and folded her hands.

"This is Mara Kell, engineer aboard the Steadfast, at the Farrow Islands. I have recovered a ship's recorder from a wreck in the Farrow Sound in about ninety metres. The hull is cut by a shaped charge, and it is not a collision. The voice on the tape is Dan Kell, my brother, master of the Amity Rose, and it names the Levenia as the ship that ran him down. The papers with it name the Levenia again, sold after her loss by Rourke Salvage and Towage. That sale and the tape go together. The sea did not do this. If you are hearing it, make it official, because we can't."

She stopped the logger and put it inside her coat.

Idris looked at her. "You said if you're hearing it. You didn't say if once before that."

"I do not say things that can't be proved. The sky can prove it or it can't." She felt the cold of the concrete through her boots and the water at her heels. "Now the transmitter."

They worked at the plinth a metre at a time until she had it in her head whole. She had learned the machine in his dead father's own words on the first night, and she said it back to herself now the way a diver says back a schedule. One rule. The set did not choose, the key chose, and the key was the tide. The float in its well lifted, the relay pulled, the carrier ran while the water held it. And the float held a level, not an hour, and on a night like this the surge held a foot of water out of the Sound and the key ran out with it and the keying came all wrong. A tape would break in pieces, a man's words cut off in the middle.

"So we do not use the float," she said. "We do not cut it, either. It's our proof and the court will want it turning as his father left it. We bridge it. We find something that lifts where the tide lifts and stays lifted while the wind says so."

Idris looked up at the ceiling, at the vinegary tangle of cable he had hung there in the years when nobody was listening. "The anemometer. The cups come round on the wind, and a spindle with a contact makes a switch that keeps time to the weather and not to the sea."

"And we bring both switches into one circuit. The float's contact stays where it lies, and the cups' contact sits beside it in the same loop. A break in any part of a circuit stops the current, so the key opens only when both switches are open. The one that is closed keeps its hand on the key, and the water and the weather take turns at it. Wire it."

He wired; she crimped. The pair went up the wall and into the conduit to the regulator's house, out to the roof, along the guy wire to the spindle that had not been asked to do a day's work since the office stopped paying for it.

The battery read eleven point two when they were done. The recorder sat at the foot of the plinth with its head open and the tape wound back to the first word.

"The battery will run the tape and the lights," Idris said. "The lamp over the hatch is the last thing."

Mara put one finger on the tape and thought about the sky. The ionosphere was a wall tonight, everything under thirty meg swallowed, and no way to know when the storm would punch a door through its own absorption. The tapes were five minutes long and the door gave you three of them if you were lucky and the ship that was listening had a watchkeeper awake.

She looked at the drip on the bracket. It had found a new seam now and was working at a tick in four seconds, and it no longer sounded like a clock. It sounded like a tide coming back.

"Then we don't wait for the door," she said. "We open it. The fleet's been sitting on seventeen all night with nothing to hear, and they'll hear a carrier come up. We run the tape and the inventory and the bell, and the first one who writes down the time and the frequency is the witness. Then the storm does what it does. Thread it."

He wound the spool and checked the path, and his hands were steady. "Play it end to end, the first take and the second after the splice. He asked for two of us on the air."

"You take the head of it. I'll take the tail."

Above them the rock thudded. Water was coming into the sump at a steady rate and would keep coming, and the hatch above was a metre of concrete and two men who were by now out of extinguisher and out of patience. The window was minutes, and the door in the sky would not stand open for a woman who was late.

She set her thumb on the key, and the carrier meter answered, and the needle sat steady at fifty watts. The wind found the cups on the roof, and the wire to the anemometer carried the spindle's turning down into the vault, and the needle climbed and stayed climbed, and the machine kept its one rule.

Mara leaned to the microphone, and the head of the tape came round, and the station's old oscillator put out its rising tones into the storm.

"Now," she said. "Run it."
