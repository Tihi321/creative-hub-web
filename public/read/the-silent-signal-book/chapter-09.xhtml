The Broadcast

The first window came at 03:40, and it was nothing like what Idris had promised.

Mara had the lamp turned down and the recorder head cleaned twice. She sat on an upturned battery crate with the marine microphone taped to her knee, watching the carrier meter. The needle lay flat and dead. Through metres of rock and concrete the storm worked at the island like a man with a crowbar.

"Listen," Idris said. "That squeak. That's the anemometer cups. You can hear them through the pipe."

The meter kicked. The old oscillator put out its rising tones and the flat note, and the receiver in the corner gave up.

"Pulse went out. She keyed."

"The tide switch is still pulling. The bridge hasn't taken the weight off it yet."

"It will, when the cups come round." She checked the battery. Ten point eight. "Two hours in the bank at this drain. We need the window in the first hour. Get the fleet."

He spun the dial. "Forty metres is all noise. Twenty, nineteen. There's a carrier on seventeen out of Reykjavik, coming and going like a door in a gale."

"That door's the window. Keep it."

In the openings they heard a woman reading a gale warning, the words coming through in pieces like a man climbing a wall. The meter kicked again. Three bars.

"Now," Idris said, and hit the play lever.

Her brother's voice went into the microphone's tin cup and out through the oscillator, and the needle swung. Mara watched the meter and the battery and the drip in the sump and kept her ears off the words.

"He's on and clear. The inventory's going out." The five-second key cut the voice in the middle of a word and the flat note followed. "That's the first bite. Wait for the cups."

They waited. The wind backed, the meter kicked, and the hull striking came through, cast iron on steel, and the key cut it at the end. Idris laughed out loud, a wild raw sound.

"That's the collision on the air. Anybody holding a dial between Rockall and the Grand Banks just heard a steamer run down another steamer."

"How many ears?"

"Every ear that's got a reason to be up at this hour. It's not a call, Mara. It's a transmission, and it's going out to whoever's listening."

The Petrel came out of the murk at 04:10 and stood off the west slip with her deck lights burning.

From the vault they heard her engines and nothing else. Idris had the receiver on the fleet's working channel now, and it gave them the Sound in fragments: a trawler skipper asking what the devil was on seventeen, another answering that it was a bell and a man's voice and he'd heard a bell like that at a funeral.

Then a flat American voice cut across them both.

"This is the Halcyon Ridge, bound for St. John's. We have the signal, we have the bell and the voice, and we are recording it on the bridge. All ships with a watchkeeper, take this down."

Idris turned to her, and his face in the lamplight was younger than she had ever seen it. "That's a real ship on the lane, and she's writing it down."

"He named the Levenia in the last bite. The tape's got to go round three more times and the battery's at nine point six, and the cups are easing as the wind backs."

Command voices crowded the receiver now, and they were not calm. A trawler out of St. Bride's asking whether the Levenia was Rourke's Levenia. An older voice saying it was and that she'd been lost these twelve years, and how did a lost ship run a man down. Somebody asking who Rourke was. Somebody answering, salvage, St. John's, and going quiet in a way that was worse than talking.

Sella came up into the Petrel's wheelhouse at twenty past four, still fastening her coat over her nightdress, and she came in expecting to take a man over to cut the hatch before daylight. The speaker on the chart shelf was giving her a trawler's voice and then Dan Kell's, and Reeve stood at the chart table with the log open in front of him and not writing in it.

"Turn that off," she said.

Reeve stayed where he was. "It's every channel, madam. Seventeen, the working channel, the distress band. There's a ship at Rockall taking it down and a Norwegian asking can we confirm the registry." He looked at her steadily. "They're naming us."

"Then name the source of the transmission as interference. It's an unlicensed transmitter putting out false evidence, and any man who repeats it repeats a lie. Cut the beacon circuit and it stops. Get the torch on that hatch."

Two of the deck crew came in behind her, wet through, and stood by the door. One was the young man who had held Idris in the office, and he was looking at her with his cap in his fist.

"Mrs. Rourke," he said. "Was the Levenia ours?"

"Don't be absurd. It's a name on a voice in a storm. I want the crew at the vault hatch in ten minutes."

"Only, the tape says the ship above the water was the one sold, and the cut was rigged in her bow, and my uncle served on the Amity Rose—" He stopped himself. "He went down in her. We were told the weather took her. We had a service and a plate on the wall at home, and my mother cried at it. Was that a lie?"

The bridge went quiet. The receiver gave them Dan Kell saying this is no weather, and the key cut him off.

"Get out," Sella said.

"I'll not get out. Not until you tell me whether my uncle was let drown by a ship with your name on it."

Mara counted the keys by the meter. Fourteen. Fifteen. The tape had gone round twice and was coming to the splice, and after the splice came the second take and the two witnesses.

"Battery."

"Eight point four."

"Enough for a round and a half."

Then the rock carried a new sound down the shaft into the vault like a bell down a well. A man with a bar, working at the flood hatch in the regulator's house.

"He can't get through the sump," Idris said. "He'd drown before he got to the bottom of the shaft."

"He's not getting into the shaft. He's cutting the line." She had the transmitter's output cable in her hand, the one that ran out to the beacon tower. She remembered Sella's order: cut the beacon circuit at both ends. "The transmitter feeds the beacon's carrier. It's the same circuit."

"Then they're running out of time at the same rate we are."

The carrier meter dropped and lay flat against its stop. The oscillator's tone went out.

"That's the feed. They've cut it." Idris was at the terminal bed. "We've got the battery and the keying, but the tower puts it out. We're a voice in a box."

"Then put the box out the hatch."

"Forty feet of flooded shaft and a man with a torch on top of it."

She was looking at the recorder and hearing her brother say not to the office, to the air, and thinking about what a beacon is. A beacon is not a transmitter. A beacon is a light.

"The lamp," she said. "The anchor light's cut but the lamp's not. Get it going and they can't say we're dark."

"There's no power in it."

"There's a battery bank the size of a truck and the pump crew's cable coils and a man with an iron who knows every wire in this rock." She set the lamp in the bracket by the hatch. "They cut the feed at the base of the tower. If I run a line up the outside of the tower and into the lamp, the lamp burns and the tower's a mast. It'll carry a signal. It'll carry the tape."

"And who runs the line up the tower in this?"

"I do."

He caught her sleeve. "The cups are easing. There's another window in forty minutes and it's the last one. You go up the tower and you'll miss it."

"Then you'll be down here and I'll be up there. The line runs down the spine into this room, and the tape takes the tower." She put the pocket logger in his hand with the bill of sale and the log page. "If they take me, you've got the evidence and the voice. Say it into the microphone and be two witnesses instead of one. Your father kept the registry, and kept the tally with it. Let him have his say."

The tower was salt-pitted iron bolted to the seaward end of the station, and the wind on it was a wall. Mara went up the outside with her coat knotted and the cable coiled across her back, and her hands did the thinking. She knew the tower from the base up to the light room, the cable trays and the spine conduit and where the salt had eaten the steel.

Halfway up the wind took her feet off the rung and she hung by one hand with the sea below moving like poured iron. She waited for the gust to spend itself and put her feet back on the iron.

At the top the lamp sat dark in its glass. She found the terminal block and put her wire on it and ran it down the spine and into the vault's cable way, and her hands were dead and steady.

The lamp came on at 04:52. A white hole in the storm, turning.

Down in the vault, the carrier meter kicked as the tower took the load, and the pulse came back with the tape behind it, and under the ship's bell Idris said into the marine microphone his name and his father's name and the two of them as witnesses.

Sella was on the foredeck with the torch man and Reeve when the lamp came on.

The crew had come up the slip and around her, and the young man with the cap had a marlinspike and was not holding it like a tool. The lamp turned, white and slow and steady.

"It's back on," the young man said. "Cut the feed and it doesn't matter. She's put the wire up the tower." He turned to the others. "You hear that? That's a man in the vault telling us what happened to his father. My uncle's still down there and we've been standing by while that woman—"

"Stand back from me," Sella said, and her voice stayed level, and that was the last time it worked. "I am the certified party on this site and I will have any man of you in irons for mutiny. Reeve, take their names."

Reeve closed his log and put it under his arm and did not take their names.

"Mrs. Rourke," he said carefully, "the Halcyon Ridge has logged this and is bound for St. John's. The trawler's asking the coast station to relay. This is not a valve on a beacon. It's on the air, and I'll not be the man who cuts a wire a court is listening to." He stepped back and made room, and the others made room, and she was alone in the middle of her own deck.

"The torch," she said. "You work for me."

The torch man looked at the lamp. He set the torch down against the bulwark and stepped back from her too.

The last window came at 05:20 and it was the widest of the night. Mara, coming down the tower with her hands raw, heard it through the conduit before she heard it in the air: her brother's voice, whole, no fixed key cutting him, because the wind had gone round and the tide's late surge had come back into the switch and the bridge had found its own rhythm in the two together.

A man alone is weather. Two men is a court.

The fleet in the Sound heard it, and the Halcyon Ridge out on the lane heard it. At St. Bride's, a watchkeeper carried the record to the desk with both hands. In St. John's, the duty watch at the rescue centre held a pencil over the log and set down the time, the frequency, and the name the voice had spoken.

They took Sella off the island at nine that morning in her own boat, with Reeve at the wheel and the young man standing by the wheelhouse door. She said nothing. She walked down the west slip between her own crew like a woman crossing a court.

The evidence went over on the first launch with Idris: the recorder, the log page, the bill of sale with its tally, and the pocket logger with Mara's voice on it, and the coast station's written record of a signal heard on seventeen metres between 03:41 and 05:20.

Mara stayed with the lamp. She sat at the base of the tower with her hands wrapped in a rag and watched the turning light put its white arm out over the Sound, where a bell had once been struck slow and gone down.

At noon the rescue centre on the mainland asked for the master of any vessel in the Farrow Sound to stand by to give evidence, and Reeve, who was not the master and had no business answering, answered them, and said he had a log and a witness and no reason to keep either of them quiet.

At four the officer of the watch at St. Bride's called the station, and Idris took it at the set with the door open to the wind, and Mara heard him say the word twice before he said anything else.

"Say that again."

"It's not a loss in the register," the officer said. "It's gone over as a criminal matter. The Amity Rose, sunk by the running-down of the Levenia — and your second engineer among the lost, the one the young lad's been telling us about. The registrar's office is reopening the registry and the coast guard is asking for the position and the depth. It's a crime, Mr. Vane. It's not weather. It's a crime."

Idris put the handset down and stood with his hand on it.

"Did you hear that?" he said, without turning round.

"I heard it," Mara said.

She went out and stood under the tower with the wind in her face and the lamp turning above her. She didn't cry. It was a crime now, and it could be proved, and her brother had not been lost the way they said he was lost. He had been run down by a ship with a name and a signature and a bill of sale, and he had put that name on the tide and waited twelve years for someone to work the key.

She looked up at the lamp and thought: it's a beacon. It always was.

And she went in to get warm, because there was a great deal of work to do before the light could be left.
