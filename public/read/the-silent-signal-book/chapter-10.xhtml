The Light

The wind dropped before dawn on the second day. The sea went from iron to slate. By eight the Sound was running long grey swells with white on their shoulders and no anger left in them. Mara had slept four hours in the radio room, coat for a blanket, receiver turned low. She woke to sunlight on the salt at the window and the smell of Idris burning porridge in the office.

He had found a pot and a bag of meal nobody had claimed in twelve years. He was stirring it with the wrong end of a screwdriver.

"There's more of this than there was," he said. "Of everything. Or I'm seeing it fresh." He set a bowl down in front of her and sat. "The Petrel's coming back at ten. Reeve's on the radio. The coast guard's launch comes on her, or behind her, or sometime after. And there's a cutter out of St. John's that nobody told me about until it called St. Bride's at four."

"Cutter's for her," Mara said. "The launch is for the evidence."

"Or the launch is for us. We did take a ship's recorder and go into a vault we were forbidden." He stirred again, stopped. "I'm not sorry. I want that written down somewhere, so it's clear."

They ate. The porridge was bad and neither of them said so aloud.

At ten the Petrel came around the head with the coast guard launch behind her, grey and low, a rib on its davits. Mara went down the west slip to meet them, hands still wrapped, the rag gone grey with salt. The officer who came off the launch was young enough to be careful, and careful enough to write down everything she said twice.

She gave them the recorder. She gave them the log page and the waxed bill of sale from inside her shirt, still faintly warm, and the pocket logger with her own voice on it. Then she walked them into the vault and showed them where the transmitter sat on its plinth, where the float switch had been bridged to the anemometer cups, where the sump had been flooded to keep men out. She would not have anyone say afterward that the evidence had been tidy. They took photographs of the flood hatch, the bar marks on the rim, the cable she had run down the spine of the tower with her own hands.

"Who is the certified party on this site?" the officer asked.

"There isn't one," Mara said. "There's me, and I'm an engineer. That's all I'll sign to."

He wrote that down too.

They came for Sella at noon. They came the way people come for someone who has kept a contract tight and a fleet quiet: without hurry, and with a warrant. She was not on the Petrel. Reeve had put her ashore at two in the morning, and she had spent the night in the station's own office with the door shut and her folder open in front of her. Nobody had guarded her. Nobody had come near her. She had not slept either.

She came out into the sunlight with her coat brushed and her hair set. She looked at the sea and the rock and the tower with the white lamp turning, and she looked past everyone on the slip.

"Mara Kell," she said, as they put her in the launch. "You'll find I kept records. Every meeting, every hour. You'll find your own signature on a contract with a secure-the-site clause, and you'll find it does not say what you decided it said."

"Then a court will read it out," Mara said. "That's what courts are for."

Sella arranged her coat and looked at the tower once. "Little Farrow was my father's station, girl. I signed a paper over its lamp when I was twenty-two years old." She said it the way a keeper reads a barometer. The rib took her out toward the cutter before Mara could ask her what she meant.

"Let her talk," Reeve said, coming up the slip with his log under his arm. "She'll talk to the investigators, because in that room she's the injured party and not the accused." He watched the rib shrink. "She was raised on this island's books. The Rourkes were lighthouse people before they were salvors. She knew the name Vane because the family's been on every station list since the last century, and she knew what a tide switch is because she was taught to read one as soon as she could read. Nobody had to tell her what your man's father had built. She knew the shape of it, and it frightened her, and that's why the Petrel came out so fast."

The young deckhand stood on the slip with his cap in his fist, waiting for the officer in the radio room to be free.

"He was on the Amity Rose," Mara said. "Your uncle."

"Second engineer. Forty-one." He turned the cap round. "I'll say it all to the man with the book. I've said it to the others already, and they'll say it too. We were told it was weather, and we were paid not to wonder, and I've wondered every watch for two years." He stopped, working something out. "There were four went down in her, you know. My uncle and then three from the engine room the water took getting the boats away. Isaac Prouty and the two Aitken boys from Catalina. The office only ever sent us word about the one. My mother wrote twice for the others and got no answer either time. I'll put that down as well."

"You'll lose your ticket, maybe."

"Maybe." He looked out at the Petrel. "I'll have the plate on my mother's wall mean what it says. That's worth a ticket."

The authorities worked the island for three days. Divers went down on the wreck and came up with the shaped-charge cut and the cradle and the second ship's name in the registry. A clerk sat in the radio room with a laptop and read the recorder's transcript back to Idris line by line. Idris corrected him where the tape had thinned, and he named his father Alun Vane every time the clerk came to the gap, and the clerk wrote it each time.

There was more in Sella's folder than anyone had expected. It came to the investigators in a grey case with her initials on the clasp, and what it held filled three tables in the office: accounts, hire slips, a letter from a name that appeared nowhere else in the file, and a receipt for a second payment, made in the autumn after the sinking. The letter did not say what the payment covered. It said the first thing was to stay done and enclosed a figure, and the receipt beneath it in Sella's hand read to keep the first thing done.

The letter was signed by the third of the three men who had signed the sealing at St. John's. The investigators found him in a care home in Harbour Grace, and he talked for two days into a recorder. He named the two men who had gone down on the wreck when the charge was set, the diver and the man on the winch who had argued about the size of it, and he named who had killed them, which was himself and the man holding the boat's fall, and he swore that neither of them had died in any storm. The second payment had come from him, to keep the first thing done. The man from the office who had come for the father's papers had been his, and had been arrested in St. John's that same week and would be a witness for the crown by the spring.

The men Sella had "let go" had scattered to the mainland and were already being sought. One would come forward by the time the trial opened. The others were named and found.

On the third day the shipping authority's man came out from St. Bride's with a folder of charts, and the two of them sat in the office with the anemometer turning above them and the tide table on the desk.

"The lane stays shut until it's surveyed," the man said. "That's the rule. The rule was made for a reason, though the reason got lost somewhere."

"It got lost in a contract," Idris said. "The lane was closed because a ship was sunk in it and the men who sank her wanted it closed. Then the insurance went quiet and the lane stayed shut because closed is cheap." He turned the chart round. "Put the survey line through here, two points off the sound, and mark the wreck as a wreck, not an obstruction. Then open it to traffic with a rule that says the position of every abandoned hull in this lane is published and kept current. Not in a cup—" he caught himself, and said it plainly "—not in an office. Published. So that the next man who finds the tide running against a bell can look it up."

The man wrote it down and said it would have to go to the authority and the authority would have to argue about it. Idris said that was fine, he had time.

The lane reopened on a Tuesday three months later, under a sounding-and-survey rule and a published-wreck register. The first ship through was a freighter that blew her whistle once at the tower going past. Idris, up in the light room with a spanner in his hand, heard it and was obliged to sit down on a battery crate until his hands were steady.

Mara stayed nine days, and most of them she spent on the lamp.

The tower had taken what the storm gave it and had taken it badly. The outside rungs were salt-eaten where her boots had been, and one of them would not take a man's weight, so she cut it out and bolted a new one. The lamp itself had been a navigation light once and could be one again, but the gearbox that turned it had run on a drive nobody had serviced, and the main bearing made a dry gravel sound. She stripped it on a tarpaulin at the foot of the tower with the wind working at the edges of the sheet and the gulls working at her temper. She found the bearing, sent for a new one, and set the old one in the office window as a doorstop, because Idris asked her to.

"The light doesn't need to be a mast anymore," she told him at the base of the tower on the seventh day, with the gearbox in pieces around her. "It needs to be a light. Fixed white, one flash every fifteen seconds, visible fifteen miles. That's the specification. That's what the chart will say and that's what the fleet sees." She laid the lamp's drive gear on the cloth. "It ran on the tide for twelve years and it ran on the wind for one night, and now it runs on a shaft and a bearing and a winding that I can service, which means it will keep running after I'm gone, which is the whole point of a machine."

"Can it still be keyed?" Idris said. "For the record. Not for— I mean—"

"I've left the tap," she said. "You can put a signal on the tower any night you like. But the lamp turns steady and the pulse doesn't go out with it. The pulse is gone, Idris. It was a message and the message got through and the message is finished. What's left is a light."

He paused.

"Good," he said. "That's what it was for."

He told her on the ninth day, at the west slip, with her bag at her feet and the Steadfast waiting out in the Sound with her engine turning over slow.

"I'm staying."

"I know."

"You knew before I did." He rubbed his jaw. "It's not that I've nowhere to go. I've a flat in St. John's with a damp wall and a woman two doors down who'll have given my plants up for dead. It's that the radio room needs someone in it, and the station needs a man who can sign for the power, and the tower needs—" He stopped, and started again, and did not trail off. "The tower's got a light in it again, and my father kept this rock for twelve years without a light, and somebody ought to be here now that it's a place with a name and a register entry and a rule about it. I'll do the weather. The authority will pay a man to keep an obs station and a beacon, and if they won't, I'll argue with them until they do, because I'm better at arguing than I was."

"You're not," Mara said. "You're terrible at it. But you're honest, and the authority's had enough of the other thing to last it a while." She held out her hand, then took it back and hugged him instead, which was not a thing she did, and she did it badly, and he held on anyway. "Your father's name is on a warrant and a registry and a charge sheet. It's written down, Idris. It's written down in five places and none of them will let anyone rub it out."

"That's all I wanted," he said, into her shoulder. "Twelve years, that's all I wanted."

She took the Steadfast out herself. The tug had come through the storm with a bent stanchion and a fouled anchor chain and a windscreen wiper that had given up out of spite. She had spent two of her nine days putting her right, because a machine you have not serviced is a machine you do not trust, and she had decided she wanted to trust this one.

She cleared the head at dusk with the sea running a light swell out of the west and the sky going the colour of a cut on the horizon. Little Farrow fell behind her to starboard, the big island to port, the tower on its seaward end. She stood in the wheelhouse with her hand on the throttle and watched it.

It had not been a fault. It had been a man, and then his father, keeping a light on.

Ahead, the Sound opened into the lane, and the lane was open, and a freighter's lights were coming down it out of the west. Behind her the light turned and turned and put its white arm out over the water, saying the same thing every fifteen seconds, which was: here is the rock, and here is the channel, and here is the way through, go on. Go on.

She opened the throttle and the Steadfast took the swell under her forefoot and put her shoulder into it. Mara Kell did not look back for a while, and then she did, and the light was still there, and she let it be.
