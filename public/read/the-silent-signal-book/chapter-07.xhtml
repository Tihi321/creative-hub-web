The Storm

By three in the morning, the barometer needle had dropped below the lowest mark on its dial. The wind shifted into the southwest, pressing down with a weight that felt warm and salt-heavy. Mara lay in her bunk, boots still on, listening to the Steadfast groan under the strain. The anchor chain ran out at near twenty fathoms, but the lee of Little Farrow offered no shelter anymore. Gusts ripped off the hill and slammed the tug over, holding it there before letting it snap back up with a crack that traveled down the mast and into her teeth. Every quarter hour, she climbed to the wheelhouse, read the strain meter, and did nothing. There was nothing to do but let the ground tackle work or fail at it.

Pavel, the man in the storm coat, had been put aboard to keep her from leaving. By three, he had stopped being a guard and become a passenger, slumped against the winch drum with his head back. Mara brought him a mug of tea and set it in his hands. The wind tore the steam sideways from the surface.

"I can't raise them," he said.

The set in the wheelhouse gave her a sand-coloured hiss on the survey channel, no edges to it at all. The emergency channel offered the same blank noise. There had been a call from the Petrel at ten, Sella asking for her answer by six, and since then the air went dead. Not jammed. Jammed had a rhythm to it. This was emptier.

"Geomagnetic storm riding on the weather," she said. "The sun's thrown something at us and the sky's not bouncing any more."

"How long?"

"Hours. A day. It always comes back."

Pavel looked at the mug. "You could take the boat. I'd have to get up."

"You'd fall down."

"I'd fall down," he agreed.

The station light turned on the hill, steady as a clock in a room where everything else had come apart. Somebody had left it burning. She knew who.

"Get some sleep," she said. "I'll watch the chain."

The centre of the low came over at four. For twenty minutes, the wind dropped to a hard breeze and the rain stopped. The sky opened between cloud and sea, a strip of cold grey light in the east, and Mara used it.

She went over the side into the dinghy and rowed for the west slip. The slip emerged from the dark a length at a time, weed on its steps slick and green and shining under the lamplight.

No man stood on the slip, and no man stood at the top of it. Mara understood why before she had gone ten paces: the Petrel had dragged. Beyond the point, she could see her, a black shape working at her anchor, deck lights swinging through ninety degrees and back, men on the forward deck with torches. Sella's crew were all on the water, keeping a ship off the rocks, because a survey vessel on the rocks was a claim file and a recorder and a man like Reeve all open to the sea.

The station was empty and lit. A lamp burned on the office desk and a folder sat squared to the edge of the wood. The anemometer turned above it, telling nobody. Sella had left the bag behind when she went to save her ship. Mara found it in the entry closet where Reeve had stowed it, canvas and salt-stained, the recorder inside in its padded case, heavier than a thing that size had any right to be. She put the strap over her shoulder and stood with the steel lip of the desk under her fingers and let herself breathe once.

Then the door opened behind her and the two men from the west slip came in. She knew them by their coats. The big one had carried the recorder up the steps; the other had zipped the bag. They filled the doorway the way floodgates fill a lock.

"Put it down," the big one said.

She took a step sideways so the desk was between them, and her hip knocked the lamp. The light swung across the wall and came back.

"The boat's dragging," she said. "You've got men on the deck and a man on the bridge. Do you want to be counting a recorder with me, or counting your own men off a lee shore?"

For a whole second, they both looked out of the window at the swinging lights beyond the point. Then the big one reached across the desk, quick for a man his size, and got the strap in his fist and hauled. Mara went with it, off her feet and into the desk edge, and her hip took the lamp and the room went down to grey window light.

That was the moment Idris used. The station door at the end of the passage banged open, and down the passage came a rising, crackling, keening sound, like a man dragging a chain across a steel deck, and under it a voice shouting in pure panic. Then every light in the station went at once, and someone was shouting fire, fire, the set's lit up.

The big one let go of the strap to put his torch on the sound, and Mara drove the heel of her hand into his wrist and the torch went spinning. The other man had the rope off his forearm. She went under his arm and through the window.

She landed on the wet turf with the recorder against her ribs and lay a moment while the rain did what it liked. Something hot ran down her jaw. She put a hand to it and the hand came away red. The glass had got her on the way out, a shallow cut under the ear, and it would keep. She got up and ran.

The regulator's house was a low concrete box at the foot of the tower with a door swollen shut in the wet, and she put her shoulder against it twice before it gave. She went along the wall with her hand, counting, because the plan had been drawn in the dark. The pump room. The pipe gallery. The floor plates. And at the end a square of concrete set into the floor with a ring bolt in it.

She got her fingers in the ring and the hatch came up an inch and a half and stuck. She got her boot in the gap and it came up and fell back with the flat crack of a slammed door. The third time she got it all the way open, and the dark below smelled of cold iron, and she swung the recorder over her shoulder and went down.
