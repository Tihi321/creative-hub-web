The Contract

The Steadfast rode at anchor in the lee of Little Farrow, and Mara Kell lay in her bunk listening to the sea work at the hull. She knew that sound the way other people knew their own names. Water slapped the steel plate and pulled away. The chain groaned its deep harmonic when the swell lifted the bow. Forward, a loose shackle rattled, the one she had meant to seize with wire and had not. Three years she had run this tug, and she could have told you the state of every weld on her from memory. She could have told you the hour the port engine ran hot if the raw-water intake fouled. She knew the exact note the generator made before it needed oil, and which of the wheelhouse windows leaked in a following sea. Machinery gave you back exactly what you gave it. It did not change the terms.

What she could not tell you was why the contract felt wrong.

She rolled out of the bunk at 02:00 because she had not slept, and sleep was not coming, and there was work. Her boots found the deck plates without her looking. The engine room was warm and smelled of diesel and hot iron and the faint sweet tang of coolant, and she walked the length of it with a torch, touching things, because touching things was how she thought. The beacon on the island had a fault record a year old and a power draw that made no sense for a decommissioned station. She had spent the afternoon with the survey chart and the tide tables and the vague unease that came over her whenever a job was described too simply.

In the wheelhouse the heater ticked. She put the kettle on the small gas ring, and while it heated she opened the log and wrote the day's positions in her square engineer's hand. Wind southwest four, sea moderate, anchor bearing zero-four-five to the island light. The numbers settled her, the way they always did. Then she sat with her tea and let the radio run.

The set was old, a fixed-mount VHF that also pulled in the HF bands if you knew how to coax it, and Mara knew. She ran a slow scan the way she did most nights, listening to the traffic of the North Atlantic. The coast guard. A trawler working the bank. The long mournful weather broadcasts that always sounded like they were reading a will. Static. A voice reading numbers. Static. The wind leaned on the wheelhouse glass and let go, leaned and let go, and the tea steamed and cooled by turns.

And then, at 02:14 by the wheelhouse clock, five seconds of something that was not any of those.

It came in on 4.8 megahertz, a band she would not have expected to be doing anything at that hour. No speech, no navigation beacon. A pulse, three rising tones and a flat one, and it repeated then stopped, as cleanly as it had begun, and the band went back to hiss.

Mara sat very still. The heater ticked. She had spent half her life at sea and she had never heard a signal like it. Morse was patterns, a hand on a key, ragged and human. This was something else, machine-cut and exact, like a door being knocked on by someone who knew she was home.

She looked at the clock. Five seconds. Then she reached for the tide tables, because that was the kind of thing that bothered her, and high water at Little Farrow had been at 02:11. Three minutes.

She wrote it down anyway. 02:14. Five seconds. 4.8 MHz. High water plus three.

She replayed it in her head, trying to break it into the pieces it was made of. The tones were all the same length, or near enough, but the gaps between them were not. Tone, a gap, tone, a shorter gap, tone, a longer gap, and the flat one at the end like a full stop. She had heard men transmit their whole lives and no fist on earth held a rhythm that steady. You could set a clock by it. You could set a lathe by it.

She waited, the tea going cold in her hand, and the pulse did not come again. At half past she went back to the log and drew a careful line under the entry, and then she sat in the dark of the wheelhouse with the heater ticking and watched the little red light on the set and did not go back to bed.

Sella Rourke had interviewed her in a hotel bar in St. John's, and Mara remembered the details because she remembered everything about machines and most of it about people. Sella had ordered tea and had not drunk it. She had a folder and a pen and a manner that was almost warm, the way a smooth stone is almost soft.

"You came recommended by the harbourmaster," she said. "He tells me you can take a dead engine apart in the dark and put it back together by morning."

"He's easily impressed," Mara said.

"I'm not." Sella opened the folder and turned it so that it faced Mara. "The job is simple. The Farrow Islands have a decommissioned weather station with an active navigation beacon. The beacon has an intermittent fault, and insurers won't reopen the eastern lane while it's broadcasting nonsense. Silence it, certify it, and the lane opens. Two weeks of your time, generous day rate, fuel and expenses covered."

Mara read through the contract with her tea untouched beside her, the way Sella's would stay untouched, and it was standard salvage language, precise and familiar, and she had signed a dozen like it. The boat would be fuel and stores on charter, herself as master and engineer, daily rates itemised, survey costs separate, liability capped. All of it she recognised. Then she came to the clause.

"Secure the site," she read aloud. "You require me to secure all equipment, structures, and materials found on the island, and to report only to you on any findings, and not to discuss the site with any other party. Why?"

Sella smiled. "Insurance. Anything we do on a site that might later be the subject of a claim needs to be documented through one channel. It's a formality."

Mara met her eyes. The clause had the feel of the machine about it, of a part that had been made to fit something else and been filed down until it did. "It's not standard."

"It's not a standard job," Sella said. "The lane's been shut three years. There are losses outstanding. The insurers want no loose ends." She closed the folder, and the interview, with it. "You have a reputation for being thorough, Ms. Kell. Be thorough."

Mara walked out into the salt wind off the harbour with the contract folded in her coat and the feeling (small, uneasy, a bad bearing starting to whisper) that she had agreed to more than she had read. She told herself she was being foolish. She told herself it was a job, and jobs wanted doing, and Dan had left her nothing but debts and the radio habit and a tug she had worked herself half to death to keep.

The terms of the secure-the-site clause were never mentioned again. Not at the dock, not in the emails, not in the two days of preparation on the Steadfast. And it was that silence, more than the clause itself, that Mara carried out to the island with her.

She went back to the radio at dawn, which in that season on that latitude meant it was still black and the wind had not eased. She had the tide tables open. She had the log. She had swept the band every half hour through the small hours and heard nothing but hiss and the far-off mutter of a ship called to harbour.

At 08:36 in the morning, six and a half hours after high water and a little past the turn of the ebb, the pulse came again. Five seconds. The same machine-exact four tones, and the small needle on the signal meter jumped, and Mara put down her pen.

She looked at the tide table. High water at Little Farrow that day had been at 02:11. The first pulse had come at 02:14, three minutes after the high. The second had come at 08:36. That was not three minutes after anything.

Unless she was counting wrong.

She sat back. The kettle ticked. The Steadfast swung on her chain, and the loose shackle rattled, and Mara reached for the chart and the pencil and did something she had not done since her brother died, because it was the only thing she could think to do.

She began to listen as though it were speaking to her.

Her brother, Dan, had been a radio operator before he was anything else. Before he was first mate, before he was lost in the North Atlantic in a storm that the reports had called sudden and violent and the insurers had called an accident. He had taught her Morse in a caravan in Donegal when she was nine, tapping on the table until their mother threatened them both. The trick, he had told her, is that a signal isn't noise. A signal has a hand behind it. Find the hand.

She had not found the hand that sank her brother. They had shown her a report and a chart and a line of weather, and she had read it the way she read everything, looking for the fault that caused it, and there had been none she could see, and that had been the end of it. But he had left her the habit, and the habit was awake now, and the habit said: listen again.

The pulse had no hand she could hear. It was machine-perfect, the tones spaced with a regularity that no human fist could produce. But the spacing itself, that was the thing. She began to measure it. Tone, gap, tone, gap, tone, gap, flat. Five seconds, but not evenly divided. The gaps were of different lengths, and they were not random.

She whistled the first one back to herself, softly, under her breath. Then again, and marked each gap with her pencil against the squared paper. She reached for the tide table and the chart and a sheet of ruled paper, and she drew the island, and the lane, and the wreck-markers with their little crosses, and she began to convert the pulse into numbers, and the numbers into minutes and seconds, and the minutes and seconds into a shape.

The shape was the outline of the tide at Little Farrow. The slow rise and fall, reversed. Every gap in the pulse stood in the same relation to every other gap as the water's height at one hour stood to its height at another. Not the tide. The shadow of the tide. A signal that had been taught to keep the sea's time and turn it into something else.

She checked it. She did it again from the start, with the tide table squared off against the chart, and she got the same answer, and the answer made her hands cold.

The pulse was not random. The pulse was not a fault. The pulse was keeping time with the water. The gaps were the tide's own shape, pressed into the air and sent out on a band nobody listened to, twice a day, on the schedule of the sea.

She was still at it when the wheelhouse radio crackled and Sella Rourke's voice came through, smooth and unhurried.

"Steadfast, this is Rourke. You're at anchor. You were due to report on the beacon by noon yesterday."

Mara looked at the clock. It was 08:52. She had not seen the noon yesterday, or she had seen it and forgotten it, and she did not say so. "I'm working the fault. Signal's intermittent. I'll need to go ashore."

"Then go ashore," Sella said. Her voice did not rise. It never rose. That was the thing about Sella Rourke; she spoke in sentences that had already been decided, and you were only hearing them. "But I'd remind you of the secure-the-site clause, Ms. Kell. Anything you find, I hear first. Is there anything I should hear?"

There was a pause on the line. It was the kind a person makes when they are deciding whether to lie to someone who already knows the answer.

"No," Mara said. "Just a bad oscillator, probably."

"Good," Sella said. "Report to me before you go ashore. I like to know where my people are."

The line clicked. Mara stood with the handset in her hand and the cold tea beside her and the ruled paper covered in numbers, and she understood two things at once: that the beacon was not simple, and that she was being watched.

She looked out at the island. The black rock of it against the grey water, the low roofs of the weather station, the mast of the beacon catching the first dull light. A seabird went over, close, and cried once, and the wind took the cry away. Somewhere out there a pulse was waiting for the next tide, and behind it a hand, and behind the hand, if Dan had taught her anything, a reason.

The old weather station's navigation beacon ran on a small wind turbine and a bank of solar-charged batteries, the only power that could be relied on out here where the cold Labrador Current met the warm Gulf Stream in fog. That was what the survey file said, and she would want to look at the turbine and the batteries and every cable that came off them before she certified anything, because a beacon with a fault record and a power draw that did not match its lamp did not have a simple fault. It had a second load.

She did not report to Sella. She would have to, later, and there would be a price for the delay. But not yet.

She went down to the boat deck and unlashed the dinghy, and while the wind pulled at her jacket she checked the oars and the painter and the little battery lantern, and she thought: whatever made that signal is not a fault, and nobody who wants a fault silenced pays this well to have it done.

The cold was bright now, salt and light on the water, and the Steadfast lay behind her with her engine ticking as it cooled. Mara Kell turned the dinghy's nose toward the Farrow Islands and began to row, and the sea pushed back against the oars the way it always did, patient and heavy and going nowhere.
