The Recorder

The skiff's diesel settled to a lumpy mutter. The Sound rocked the hull and the dark went on in every direction.

Mara ran the dive plan a last time. One line down, one line back, both clipped to the stern cleats. No penetration past the hatch. Twelve minutes on the bottom and no more, and if she ran past that the whole thing came apart. She had written the schedule out twice on the back of her log and checked it against the tables. She did not like it. She could not change it. Ninety metres on open-circuit air gave a man a breath of working time and then a long cold climb with stops in it, and she had thought about the depth until thinking stopped paying. A serious thing, that. She had not come to the Farrow Islands to be sensible in small ways, but she had not come to be dead either.

Idris talked too much and too fast and she let him.

"He'd have known the regulator's house is the deepest cupboard on the island," he said. "He'd have known the sea would keep a thing if you put it where the sea lives."

"That's your father now," Mara said. "He built the lock. He hid the coordinates in the tide. A man who builds a thing like that knows where to put the evidence." She pulled her hood back and looked at him. "Get the box up first. Worries after."

They kitted in the dark with the lamps off. Idris's suit was his father's, cut for a taller man, and the boots came up wrong round his shins and he stopped to fix them twice before he would listen. Her reg gave the dry click of a valve stored too long in salt before it bit, and the breathing was the only honest thing in the boat. She checked his gauge, and he checked hers, and neither of them pretended the number on it was small. She touched two fingers to her chest. Idris copied it. Let's go.

The Sound was less angry beneath the surface. Ten metres down the wind thinned to a hiss. Thirty, and it was gone and the water was a flat blue full of drifting matter that hung at the edge of the beam, and the blue went on and on and went grey and the grey went colder. She clocked the watch, clocked the line, breathed slow and followed Idris down the scarp she had seen on the sounder.

At sixty metres the cold got into her hands. At seventy it got into her head and made things slow, and she had to stop and think about her own name, which is the thing that tells a diver the depth has opinions. She held the line and counted her breaths and made herself make the decision she had already written down. Two minutes on the bottom and one pass. That was the plan and it was a good plan. The rest of the plan was hers, and she would not spend it.

The wreck came up at her out of the dark and for a moment was only broken grey litter on the bottom, and then the beam found the line of her hull and she was a ship.

She lay over to port with sand drifted to her rail, and the damage showed six metres from the stern in a wound of folded plate that opened inward in petals of rusted grey. Mara hung off the line and felt her scalp go tight under the hood. Weather works the seams and cracks them and lets the sea do the slow argument. This had been a shape held against the plating and fired.

She wrote on her slate and turned it for Idris. CUT.

He read it and braced his palm on the hull, and the shudder went down the line to her hand.

They swam in through a gap where a hatch had burst, two metres into a dark room that had been a passage. Idris's lamp found a locker door hanging open and a white board with a name printed on it and a man's handwriting in a strip across the corner, gone grey but legible. Dan Kell. She read it and said nothing. Her brother had put his name there so a man could know a locker was his, and her brother had grown up in a house by the water and could tie a bowline when he was six, and she had married no one and buried both her parents and now she was reading her brother's name at ninety metres in the Farrow Sound.

Idris tugged her sleeve. A pocket of steel had folded and landed upright and kept some of what was inside. She saw the orange of the casing first, and then the dark plastic and the steel of the cradle bracket. The recorder was still in its mounting, and the bracket had pulled from its bolts and the assembly rested on its side against a frame.

It weighed more than it should. She was ready for that; she had not been ready for it to come free so easily, the bracket parting with a crack she heard through her teeth, and for an instant it swung loose, and she had the full weight in one hand, and the bottom was suddenly underneath her, black and close. She got her reg in and was moving up before she had decided. Idris took the other handle. They went up together, the box between them.

The plan they had written and the water they were in were two different things. She got five minutes off the bottom and her gauge said she was in it deeper than the whole next stage, and she knew the long cold climb was the part that could kill her, so she went by the book she had been taught and not by the clock in her head. Two hundred and fifty to a hundred and ninety, and hold it. A minute and a half. Then up again. Slow as an old woman on a stair. Idris hung below her on the line and did not hurry, and once his lamp swung up and found hers and he nodded, and she nodded back, two people going up at the speed of the penalty they had earned.

At twenty-one metres she stopped and ran the hour her body had bought itself. Idris stopped above her, and the cold came on and she did the stretches she had been taught, and she watched her watch and did not let the mind wander off. Up to twelve and stop, and the sea rolled the line and the swell reached down and touched them. Up to six and stop, and that stop ran long, and it is the long stop that keeps you out of a wheelchair. Then the skiff's hull came up grey over them, and they broke the surface into wind and cold, and Mara shoved the recorder over the gunwale and got her reg out and made a pair of breaths sound like a decision.

"We did it," Idris said, when he could.

"Get aboard before the sea hears you boast."

It took them both to heave the capsule in. The orange casing was pitted and the label had lifted at one corner, but the seals were intact, and it sat on the boards with the water running out of its lugs. Idris got the lamps on and his hands were shaking, and he wiped them once on his suit and laid them flat and still.

The Petrel's cabin light burned a hundred metres off. "She'll dive at first light," Mara said, "and find the cradle empty, and know who took what. We've got until she works out where it went." She ran the skiff in toward Little Farrow, the throttle down and her running lights dark. "Open it."

The access cover came off with a quarter turn. Inside was the data capsule in its foam bed, the size of a big thermos, battery still seated. Idris pulled a playback cable out of his bag and rigged it to the station's spare radio in the boat, and they sat with the wind pulling at their collars and the lamp between them.

The first recording was engine room audio, hours of it, the ship's beat under a man's voice giving the hour and the watch. The machine was a camera for sound.

"Where does she take water?" Idris said.

Mara ran the control. Zero four hundred, all well. The sea went on. A man said something about a pump. Time went on. Then the beats were no longer steady, and the bridge channel broke through, sudden and much too clear.

A sound like a hammer on a bell, and another, and the pumps thudding, and a man's voice that had been calm saying where it was and saying it fast. Then a new voice cut across the bridge, young and not calm: It's down. It's in and it's down. Take her to make water where the weather can be blamed.

Mara had her hand flat on the deck. The wind blew salt into her eyes and she did not close them.

Idris ran the tape forward: more flooding, orders given and not given, a chaos of men doing their jobs in a hull condemned from underneath by a sound they had heard and not understood. Then another channel took it, a handset keyed and left open, and a man's breathing in the dark of a place that was filling. Mara heard the water behind the breathing, and she heard the ship's pump go quiet, and she put both her hands on the gunwale.

If anyone picks this up, it's Dan Kell off the Amity Rose. It's not the weather, whatever the report says. They put us under and I saw the men who did it. My ship knows the machine that was in the number and knows the hull that put it there.

The breathing took itself in and went out.

I'm not the only one who saw. There were others and one of them is dead already for saying so, and the man who did it has the office's ear. So it has to come off the water, and it has to come off alive, and when it comes it comes with two of us behind it, or there'll be none of us. The box comes up with whoever comes for me. If I'm not there, she'll need the rest of you.

The recording ran into the sound of water, and then into nothing, and stopped.

Mara sat and looked at the dark water with her hands on the rail.

"Dan," she said. "It was Dan. I told you the transmitter was my brother's and I was wrong. My brother wrote the message. Your father built the lock. Dan found out what was done to his ship and he wrote it into a machine and gave it to a man he trusted, and he's been answering every spring tide for twelve years." She took a breath and made it count. "And your father took a stranger's message and made it a thing that would outlast him. The two of them built a lock that talks better than either of them could, and nobody answered except the two of us."

"That's the part I've got to own," Idris said. He was quiet a moment, and his head went down over the radio. "The cradle, Mara. The bracket. The box came down with the ship. He never put it in the mountain. He put it where the sea could hold it. Sheet steel. A jig exactly that size." He turned the lamp so it fell on his own hands. "My father's workshop has the jig in it yet. The packet came up with the papers twelve years ago, and the night he died he came home with it and would not say where it had been, and he went to his bench and he made a mount for it and he never told me why. I found it after. I found the mount, and I found where he'd been." He swallowed. "He went down and he put the box in a bracket of his own making, and then he went up to the light and gave them the name, and they gave him a contract and sent him home, and he died for it. And I sat on it because I was frightened and nineteen and didn't know if a man who talked about locks all night was mad. He wasn't. He was doing what your brother asked, and he was doing it alone, and I had known it for years and I had said it wrong, because it's easier to say a man fell than to say a man was killed." He put his hand on the edge of the radio case, and his knuckles went white on it. "I'm telling you the whole of it now because you'll be sending this out, and I don't want one thing kept back. Not one."

The wind came round the island's shoulder and the skiff bucked at her lines.

"He didn't keep it back," Mara said. "He put it where it could be found." She folded the playback cable. "Get us in to the station, and keep the lamp off the recorder, and don't look at the Petrel."

They ran in past the Petrel's quarter with the engine low and the recorder under a fold of canvas. Two men stood at the rail in the spilled cabin light and saw her, and one turned and went into the cabin.

The west slip was dark and the chain lay where she had left it. Idris jumped for the steps with the line and she cut the engine and reached for the capsule, and Idris had the canvas half back when a torch came on and went into their faces, and a voice she knew said:

"Hands off the cargo."

Reeve came down first with a lamp and a length of cutting rod in his right hand that was not a cutting rod. Two more behind him. Sella Rourke came last, coat buttoned, hat in place, and looked down into the skiff where the belly of the recorder showed under the canvas, and surprise was not in her face.

"You brought it up in the dark," she said, "which is efficient, and I don't object to efficiency. But you used a launch I believed to be chained, and you went to a wreck I believe to be nobody's business." She came to the bottom step and stood with the water forty centimetres from her boots. "Give me the capsule."

"It's evidence," Mara said. "Of a crime you're standing in the middle of."

"No," Sella said evenly. "It's property. It's on a wreck in a channel surveyed and closed at the insurers' expense, which means every scrap of it belongs to a claim. That is the legal opposite of evidence. It is the fact of a matter having been settled, and every hour a thing like this exists unsettled is an hour somebody has to explain." She pulled her glove tight at the wrist. "Reeve."

Reeve had a bag open on the concrete. He came down to the gunwale and put his hand out, and Idris stood with his own hands loose at his sides and looked at Mara. Mara understood, clearly and not slowly, that she had three options and each one was a person.

"Hand it up," Sella said. "There's no version of this night where you don't, and I'd rather not have one of my men take it off you."

Mara breathed out. She turned and lifted the recorder with both hands and laid it in Reeve's arms, and the weight came off her fingers like a hole. Reeve set it in the bag. The other man zipped it and carried it up the steps, and the plastic made a flat sound on the wet concrete and went away into the dark.

Sella watched it go, and there was nothing in her face at all. "Forty-eight hours. I've told you once. I won't tell you a third time." She turned and went up the slip after her men, and the engine of her boat started out in the dark.

Idris let out a breath that had been in him a long while.

"Leave the skiff," Mara said. Her hands were steady and her voice was not, and the difference was the only thing she had to hold on to. "Get the tide table and your coat and go up to the regulator's house."

"The vault's been pumped. She's got the station watched."

"Then we go the way the water goes. There's a second door on the regulator's house, and a cut in the concrete that the station records call the flood hatch. Your father's generation made it to keep the station's feet dry, and I found it in the drawings Sella gave me and never once looked past. The vault's not the only way under this rock." She looked after Sella's boat and the bell buoy tonguing out on the Sound and the dark where the recorder had gone. "She's got the mountain in a bag. She hasn't got the mountain. The transmitter's still wired to the batteries, and the batteries don't care who's holding the case. Dan said take it out on the air and don't do it alone." She climbed the wet steps with the wind pushing her back. "We've got one thing she hasn't counted on. The storm."
