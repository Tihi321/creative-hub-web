The Coordinates

Sella's crew pumped the vault all day.

Mara watched from the radio room window and did the arithmetic she was good at. The pump was a four-inch trash unit on a fuel-driven frame, and it hammered through the afternoon in a hard flat chatter that carried clear over the rock. By four the hose at the vault mouth ran thinner. By dusk it spat. Reeve went down with a lamp on a line and came up with his trousers black to the shin, and he and Sella stood over a slate on the apron and talked into the wind. Twice the thin man pointed back down into the vault. Sella pointed at nothing. She listened, and once she turned her head toward the station and looked up at the radio room window. Mara held still. The look ran a second too long before it slid past her.

They had found the transmitter. Of course they had. Bolted to a plinth, wired into the battery bank, no way on God's grey earth to hide a thing like that, and Mara had known it from the moment the door came open. What she had not known was what Sella would do about it, and that was what kept her at the window: what a contractor did next told you who was paying her.

Sella did nothing. The pump came up at dark. The gear was stacked and sheeted on the apron, the crew went aboard the Petrel for their supper, and the vault door was left open behind them, a black mouth in the rock with a cold breath coming out of it.

"She's leaving the set in the water," Idris said. He had come in from the turbine shed with his hands black to the wrist and his mind nowhere near the turbine.

"She's had a look and she's taken a decision. Pull it now and a dozen men watch a marine transmitter hauled out of a dead station's vault, and that's a question. Tomorrow she'll have a reason ready. Tomorrow it's 'obsolete equipment removed as a hazard.' By the time the lane's open it's a fact and nobody's asking." Mara turned from the window. "Where's the tide table?"

"Behind the distribution board in the office. Nobody puts a hand back there for anything."

"Good. Sit down. We've got until high water the night after tomorrow and a whole message to read off a set that fires when the sea tells it to."

The tide came to its mark at 21:52, and the pulse came four minutes later.

Idris had the office set warmed and the speaker on the shelf and his tide table unfolded between two lamps. Mara sat in the chair that listed to port with her log on her knee and her pencil where she could reach it without looking.

The four tones. A gap. The four tones again.

She counted the gap by watch and by ear, and Idris did the same, and they compared at the end and not before, because a message is only as good as a second reader. He wrote the tide heights off the float schedule he had kept. She wrote nothing but seconds, and the tide table did the rest.

"The set fires at the mark," Idris said. "Anything over three point one metres on the gauge. So it only talks the top half of the tide."

"Two hours, maybe less." Mara turned her page. "How many groups before it repeats?"

"Never got that far. The tide was always down before I'd written enough."

The next signal came. Then the next. The office was quiet but for the wind in the roof and the speaker's small breath of static. Out in the dark the Petrel showed a cabin light. Somewhere down the Sound the Steadfast swung at her anchor. Mara wrote, and Idris called the height, and the tide table did the tide's half of the work the way it had for twelve years.

At 23:40 the gaps changed.

"Different group," Idris said. "The gaps are longer. That's a separator. He's saying the number's finished."

Mara counted back. "The first half is the position. The second half starts now, and it's been starting there every spring tide since your father died and nobody's ever heard it."

They went on. The water fell away from the mark. The pauses grew. Near one in the morning the set fell silent and stayed silent. The two of them sat with their logs open and did the arithmetic.

Mara applied the scheme exactly as it had been given her: the height of the tide at the pulse for the digit, the length of the gap before the group for its place in the message. That was all of it, and it had held for twelve years, and she read down her column twice.

"I've got a chain," she said. "Position first. Then a name spelled out in letters. Dan Kell."

The pencil stopped. The wind pressed at the window and the lamp flickered and the office shrank to a very small room with the whole sea outside it. Mara looked at the four letters in their careful row. She did not turn the page. For a while she did nothing at all.

Idris let the quiet stand. When he spoke it came out gently. "Your brother."

"He was on a ship that went down in a sudden storm. Twelve years ago. I was nineteen. They found three of the crew and never found him. The board said the weather came on them and there was nothing anyone could have done." She looked at the letters. "My mother wanted a stone put up. I told her we should wait. I told her he'd been a careful man and careful men don't drown, and if they hadn't found him there was a reason and the reason would come." She put her palm flat on the page, not to hide the name but to feel it under her hand. "Twelve years I've been waiting. I've read every board of inquiry I could get the paper of, looking for a ship's name I'd know. I told myself it was work. It was never work."

"You never said."

"There was no one to say it to." She turned the page then, deliberately, the way you take your hand off a hot surface. "And now a dead man's transmitter spells his name at one in the morning, and I've cried about it exactly as much as I'm going to tonight. Give me a minute, then read me the next group."

Idris offered her nothing. It was the right thing and she was grateful for it. He waited, and she looked at the dark where the window was, and she let the name sit in the room where it had earned the right to sit.

Then he looked at his log and his mouth pulled. "The next group. It's not a letter. It's a number. It's a name off a hull."

He turned the paper round. A ship's name and a registration. Mara did not know it. Then Idris pushed the tide table across the desk and laid his finger on a pencilled line in his father's careful blue ink: the same name, written twelve years ago in the margin beside a row of digits.

"Same ship," he said. "My father put it down in his first message. He found out who sank your brother's ship, Mara, and he wrote the name down, and then he went up to the light."

Mara looked at the flat note at the end of her log. "Say it plainly."

"My father didn't fall." Idris said it to the desk. "He went to the office and gave them the name and the position and asked them to act, and they gave him a contract and sent him home. A few nights later he was dead at the foot of his own stair, and the man from the office came round asking for papers, and I've spent twelve years telling myself it could have been the wind and the wet step." He looked up. "It wasn't the step. He was killed because he found what you've just written down. And I sat on it because I was frightened and nineteen and didn't know if a man who talked about locks all night was mad. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, Mara."

The turbine shed door banged in the wind and the beacon's cable hummed in the trunking and the office was as cold as the vault.

"He wasn't mad," Mara said. Her voice had gone very quiet and very even, and she could hear it doing it and could not stop it. "He was an engineer. He built a thing that worked and died for it, and twelve years later it told the truth to a pair of us at one in the morning. That's not mad. That's the only sane thing anyone on this island has done for a decade. Sit up straight. We've got work."

They laid it out on the desk, the two of them, a pencil and a chart.

"The position puts the wreck two points off the Farrow Sound, in about ninety metres of water," Mara said. "The channel is where the insurers closed the lane, and the channel is where the Petrel is anchored, and the Petrel is a wreck-diving unit." She tapped the chart. "She's not here for the vault. She's here for the wreck, to get the recorder off it and put it somewhere it will never be found. The vault was only the thing she had to deal with first so the beacon would stop talking. If we clear the vault she can take her time on the wreck. If we don't—"

"If we don't, the beacon keeps talking, and she has to go down on a clock, and a thing done on a clock gets done badly." Idris nodded slowly. "So we go down first."

"We go down first, and we bring up the box, and we don't bring it to her. A ship's recorder is a steel capsule built to survive the bottom. If a man sank that ship he knew the recorder would tell what it heard. Either way it's the only thing on that wreck that says sabotage instead of storm." Mara looked at him. "Have you ever dived to ninety?"

"No."

"Neither have I. So we do it the way a job gets done when nobody's qualified: slowly, on a line, on the slack, and not one metre past what we've planned." She drew the pencil across the paper, then stopped and looked at what she had drawn. "I want to be plain about this, because I'm the one breathing the air. Ninety metres on open-circuit air isn't a working dive. It's a climb and a wait in the cold and a set of stops on a line, and the bottom time is one pass and no more. If it goes wrong down there it goes wrong faster than I can fix it, and there's no chamber on this island and no help nearer than St. Bride's. I know all of that. I'm doing it anyway." She met his eye. "You should know I'm doing it anyway, and not because I think it's safe."

Idris was quiet. "I'd go instead, if I could," he said. "I've no more diving than you and less nerve. But I'd go."

"I know. You're the one who kept the lock. I'll do the deep." She went back to the chart. "How's the station's boat?"

"The station hasn't had a boat since before the flood. My father's dory is up on the slip, but she's got a plank out and hasn't been in the water in three years."

"What about the skiff on the west slip?"

"The old survey launch." Idris's head came up. "She's chained to the ring and the yard's put a padlock on her and the key went to St. John's with the rest. The lock's a good one. It's not a thing you—"

"What kind of lock?"

"Padlock. Brass or bronze, with a shrouded shackle."

"Show me the chain and the ring."

They went out into the wind with lamps. The west slip was a flight of wet steps and a ringbolt set in concrete, and the skiff lay against it on a sagging line with a padlock the size of Mara's fist through the chain and the ring. She crouched and turned it in her light.

"Bronze body, brass barrel, six-pin. That's a good lock." She stood. "And it's on a chain, and the chain goes round the ring, and the ring's a bolt-through set in the concrete with a hex head on top and the nut under the lip of the step. I can't reach the nut and I can't turn the bolt against it. But the concrete round the stem has cracked. Old pour, sea water, frost, twelve years of a chain working at it. The concrete's the weakest thing here, and it's the part nobody thought about." She got the wrench out of her coat. "We don't touch the lock. We work what it's holding."

She knelt on the wet steps and put the wrench on the hex head and worked it loose by degrees, and when the bolt turned the plate came up with it and the padlock rang against the ring, still locked, still a good lock. The wind flung water at her back. It cost her twenty minutes and one cut knuckle, and at the end the ringbolt came out of the old concrete with a grind like a tooth coming out, and she had chain and ring and padlock in one hand and the skiff's lines in the other.

"Machines," Mara said, and got her breath. "You can trust a machine to be exactly as strong as it was made to be, and no stronger."

The battery was nearly dead from a winter on the slip. The diesel turned twice and died, and turned a third time and caught on three cylinders and would not take a fourth. Idris had the lines off and the fenders out. The little launch nosed out of the west slip at two in the morning into a dark broken by the Petrel's cabin light, and Mara took her past the island's shoulder with the throttle down and her running lights dark and her ears on the wind for any engine but her own.

"A hundred metres off the Petrel's quarter we'll be nobody," she said. "A dory and a skiff and a dozen boats like us. The whole Sound's full of men who don't want to be seen at night. She won't turn a spotlight on a fishing boat unless she wants a story to explain."

At the position the sounder gave her a bottom at ninety metres, a broad lump with a scarp beside it, and the chart said clear water.

"There she is," Idris said. "And nobody's marked her, because she's in the lane, and a ship in the lane means the lane stays shut." He stood in the bow with the lamp shaded. "Mara. There's a thing about the Petrel I've been not saying for an hour because I didn't want to say it out loud."

"Then say it."

"She's anchored on the wreck. Near enough. A hundred metres off her quarter and we'd be on top of it. She's not here to wait on our clock. She'll put a diver in the water at first light." He set the lamp down. "So whatever we bring up, we bring up before the sun does."

Mara looked at the dark water and the sounder's number and the Petrel's light, and understood that she had broken her contract and Sella's orders both, and that if the pump had stayed another day, or the padlock had been two pins better, or the old concrete had held, she would not now be sitting over a wreck in a borrowed boat with a dying battery and a plan that had no margin in it.

"Get the lamp aft and keep it off the Petrel," she said. "We've a line to run and a bottle to rig, and I'd like both done before the sky knows what we're about."
