Descent

The descent began before dawn, and it was not one descent but three, each with its own mountain.

First the col. They came out of the tent into a thin grey light and found the wind had dropped and the snow stood in soft unbroken banks around the stove. Dawa had water boiling. Nima was already up the ridge with a rope, checking the line they had walked down in the dark. Teodor sat in the litter with his bad foot packed in snow and a face like a man who had decided to be cheerful and was finding it hard work. Ilse walked the circle of the camp once, boots on the snow, and felt nothing but the cold, clean floor of it. That was the best reading of the week: the ground had stopped moving.

They went down to the second terrace with the rescue men on the ropes, and the glacier in daylight was a different animal. All night she had known it through her knuckles and her heels. Now the light lay across it and showed her the sags and the long blue seams and the hollows where a man could go in to the waist. She walked with one hand on the line and her eyes open and her feet still awake. It was strange to have the mountain outside her again, plain as a field, when she had carried it all night inside her hands.

Priya came down with the case strapped high on her back, and every so often she stopped and looked around as though she had not expected to see any of it again. Her face was grey and her lips were cracked and she was crying in a tired, absent way, the way a person does when the danger is over and the body has not yet been told. Hesk came behind her with the bar in his fist and his shoulders raw where the cradle had ridden. He said nothing at all, which was the newest thing about him.

At the first flat place the doctor, a thin valley man with a face like a folded map, took Teodor's boot off and studied the ankle a while without speaking. Then he laced it again in slow words Ilse had to watch his mouth for and told him he would not walk on it again that day.

"I have walked on it all night," Teodor said.

"Then you have done enough walking on it for one man," the doctor said.

That was when Ilse knew how much it hurt. Teodor argued about everything: about the weight of the packs and the colour of the tents and the number of days in the forecast, and he had told Ilse at the foot of the great face, in a voice that could have cracked the ice, that he did not care how many deaf women the mountain had killed, he would not carry a bell up a wall on the strength of a feeling. But he did not argue with the litter. He sat down in it and let them lift him and lay back and closed his eyes, and then he lifted one hand and made the two-fingered wave that meant he was all right and she was not to fuss.

She set her palm on his shoulder and felt the shake in him under the coat.

The icefall had changed its face in the night.

Coming up it had been a climb. Going down it was a ruin. The track they had cut was gone under a slope of broken blocks that had frozen together into a grey rubble which looked solid and was not. Nima took them east under the wall, the way he had come up in the dark, and in the light the way was plain and terrible: a long run of fallen ice with a black chute of water moving somewhere beneath it, and the wall standing over them with its snow hanging in great soft ledges.

They stood at the top of it and nobody spoke.

"One at a time," Nima said. "The bell on the litter. The litter on the fixed line. Nobody walks beside the bell."

Dawa set the anchors and whistled between his teeth while he worked. Ilse stood at the corner where the chute bent and felt the litter come down past her, its weight in the ground as much as in the ropes. Fifteen kilos of bronze that had gone into the mountain a hundred and twelve years ago and had not been meant ever to come out, coming down now by the hands of people who did not own it. Each time the litter settled at an anchor she felt the small dense thump of it through her soles, counted, waited, and the ice answered and held.

Priya came down the fixed line behind the litter and said nothing for the whole of the fall. At the bottom she set the case down on the clean glacier and stood with her hands at her sides and looked back up at the ruin they had come through.

"I want to do it properly," she said. "The paper will be in the valley by tonight. There is a man down there who has been sending me letters about the bell for a year, and I have not answered one of them, and now I cannot not answer." She took a breath. "The archive first, before the paper. I want to stand in the east hall and give the book over with my own name on the wrong end of it. Not the way I meant to. The proper way. And then I want to face the man with the newspaper, whatever he makes of it."

Ilse looked at her. "The paper will not be kind."

"I have had enough of kind," Priya said. "Kind is what my mother gave me instead of the truth."

They came onto the last terrace and set the bell down and Dawa got out the stove, and the sun was full and the glacier ran pale and safe away to the moraine, and Ilse sat down on the ground without first feeling it through her hands.

Hesk sat with his back against the wall and his hands hanging between his knees. He had carried the front of the cradle the whole way and his shoulders were shaking with the work and he made no attempt to hide it. The tea came round in a tin cup that had been the doctor's and was now everyone's. Below them a rescue man laughed and the laugh carried flat and clear in the thin air.

When he got up he made no speech. He came over and put a folded paper into Ilse's hand and stood there. It was the pay slip, signed across the back.

"The bet," he said. "It is yours. Put it in the school."

She turned it over. "A week's pay is nothing."

"It is a start." He turned his hand up and showed the coin-sized burn on the palm where he had crushed the page. "And I am going to be the first one through the door. You will teach me the one thing I still cannot do." He scowled. "I will be some trouble."

"You will be some trouble," Ilse said, and put the paper in her pocket.

That was the whole of the apology, and it was enough, because he had made it on the ridge already and this time he had made it with his hands.

Base camp came up out of the glare like a small miracle: the orange tents, the flags, the mess door flapping, and people running. They had been seen from the moraine an hour before. By the time the litter came down onto the rock the whole camp was out, the cook and the porters and two men Ilse did not know in clean jackets, and the doctor had a lamp lit before the litter touched stone.

Teodor was carried in and put on a stretcher and the mess tent filled with people and lamp light. Ilse stood outside it in the sun and let the doctor come and find her.

"Sprained badly," he said, in his slow careful mouth-shape. "Perhaps a small crack in a bone. Not broken. He will be off it weeks and on it by winter." He saw her face and said, "The foot will heal. He is lucky."

"Teodor is not lucky," Ilse said. "Teodor is careful, and he was carrying a bell."

The sponsor's man found her before she had finished her tea. He was round and soft-faced, from the city, and he had come up expecting to write about a lost archivist and a failed errand. He stood over the bell with his mouth open. The old cook had come out of the mess tent and put his palm on the bronze and kept it there and said something to Dawa in a language Ilse did not have, and the sponsor's man watched all of it without writing anything down.

"This is it," he said at last. "This is the bell from the cave. The whole story." He turned his clipboard over as if he had forgotten what it was for. "How did you get it down?"

"By hand," Ilse said.

"Miss Rask," the man said, and turned to Priya with the case in her arms. "The office told me. About your family. About the guide. I had it from the valley before I came up, and I have not known what to do with it."

"Write it," Priya said. "Write the whole of it. The cook rang the bell and the guide lay under a rock and the record gave the guide the credit. I am his great-great-granddaughter and I came up here to burn a page and I did not. Say that too." She stopped and steadied herself on the case. "There is a burnt place on the first page the size of a man's glove, and a note beside it in my own hand that says how it came there. Show it to your newspaper man. Nobody has to be flattered in it and nobody has to be spared."

The sponsor's man made a small sound in his throat, then lifted his chin and looked away down the glacier, and for once he looked like a man who had come to the end of what he had been paid to say.

They let her alone a while, and she used it to walk the camp and feel it under her feet.

The moraine was solid. That was the first thing and the best thing. She stood at the edge of the shelf with her boots on the old rock and felt the glacier below, and the glacier was quiet and slow and going nowhere but down, and her feet told her nothing at all, and nothing at all was the sweetest reading of the week.

She counted them as she had counted them going up, when the counting had been a thing she did not let herself do out loud. Nima at the stove with Dawa, both of them whole. Teodor asleep in the mess tent with his foot up and the doctor watching over him. Priya in the mess doorway with the case on her knees, reading the burnt page out loud to nobody, getting it right. Hesk at the bell with his hands behind his back, looking at it the way a man looks at a thing that has beaten him and that he has come to love.

Eleven had walked up. Eleven were on the shelf. The mountain had taken nothing from them but a week, an ankle, and a good deal of their pride.

She had begun to think it was over.

The sponsor's man came out of the radio tent at a trot with a flimsy in his hand, and there was something in his face that made her put the cup down before he spoke.

"From the valley," he said. "The forecast desk."

Nima came over. Dawa came over. Ilse set her hand on the bell and felt the bronze take her palm and go quiet.

"Storm coming in from the west," the big man read. "They say four days, same as before. They say to hold the camp and the last of the climbers until it passes." He turned the flimsy over. "But the valley is already white. They say the barometer dropped faster than the model, and there is a man in the city who has looked at the same figures and he says two days and he is usually right." He looked up. "The radio says the road out is under snow as far as the bridge."

Ilse went out past him to the edge of the shelf and stood on the old rock and took her boots' reading.

The glacier was not quiet any more.

She had been standing in the sweet nothing of it, and a sweet nothing was not the same as a true one. Under the nothing there was a pressure she had been too glad to notice, a long low shifting in the deep ice, the ground gone very slightly heavy, the way a slope feels a moment before it decides. She laid her palm flat on the rock and felt the cold come up through it and behind the cold something else, a restlessness, a breath being drawn. She looked west and saw the light had gone flat and yellow at the edge of the sky, and the flags on the mess tent had begun to turn like things looking for a direction.

She came back to the group and said, "Two days. The valley is right and the desk is wrong and it has been wrong about the same weather all week. We do not have four days. We go for the bridge at first light and we take the bell and the book and the boy with the bad foot, and if the bridge is gone we go over the moraine the hard way and we go on foot."

"On foot, with a litter, in a storm?" the sponsor's man said.

"On foot with a litter," Ilse said, "or we are still on this mountain when it shuts."

She went to the mess tent and stood in the door. Teodor was awake and watching her from his stretcher, and he did not ask what had happened, because he could read her as well as she could read ground.

"That quick, then," he said.

"That quick," Ilse said. "Can you travel?"

"I can be carried," Teodor said, with enormous dignity. "It is a different skill and I have been practising it all morning."

Hesk had come up behind her. She turned to him and found him already looking west at the yellow edge of the sky, his jaw set, and she did not need to tell him anything.

"The stakes go with us," he said. "The tent, the rope, the stove, the shovel. I will see to it tonight." He hesitated, then said the thing he would not have said a week ago. "Tell me what you want me to feel for, on the road, in the dark. You have taught me the floor and the anchor. Teach me the weather."

Ilse held his eyes. Then she put out her hand and turned his burned palm upward and laid her own over it.

"The wind will come from the west before the snow," she said. "Put your hand on the ground and feel the change in it. When the ice goes from quiet to listening, come and tell me. Ring the bell if the radio dies. You know the four now."

He closed his fingers over hers and let go.

The camp was already moving around them, porters folding tents, the doctor packing his bag, the sponsor's man in the middle of it with his flimsy and no notion what to do with it. Ilse walked out to the edge of the shelf once more and stood with her back to the camp and her boots on the rock and the whole white length of the glacier going down toward the valley.

The light was going out of the west. The glass was falling. The mountain was not letting them go, after all.
