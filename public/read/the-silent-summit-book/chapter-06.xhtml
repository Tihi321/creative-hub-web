Ridge of Echoes

They left before the light came, and they left badly.

Ilse would carry that afterward, longer than she carried the cold. Half the night had gone into making ready, and a night spent making ready leaves a body unrested. Priya had sat with the case wedged between her boots and her hands folded on the lid. Hesk repacked his pack three times and then left half of it scattered on the rock. Teodor made Ilse sit down and eat soup she did not want. "The bell comes down in the cradle, in the middle of the line," he said. "We go at the pace of the slowest, and the slowest is the bell."

She stood in the grey before dawn with her palm flat on the shelf rock and felt the wind turning over in the upper bowl. The forecast desk had it wrong. She had it right. The difference would be counted in hours nobody had to spare.

The first hour down was the good hour. Dry rock, hard snow on the traverse, and the icefall below them pouring down the mountain in steps like a river caught mid-fall over stones. Ilse took the front left corner of the cradle, Teodor the front right, Hesk and Priya the rear. Every fifty metres she called the line tight and groped for the old track under the new snow, and the track held. The ice remembered where they had cut it.

They reached the first terrace before the light had any colour in it. Above the far wall the sky wore a flat iron look she did not like. She laid her hand on the bell and through it felt the terrace, and the ice beneath the terrace, and the ice was on the move, slow, the way ice moves when some great weight above it is shifting and has not yet decided to fall.

Hesk had stopped. He stood with the rear rope slack and his head turned back up the way they had come. Then he came on and held his tongue until they had crossed to the second terrace.

He spoke then, quietly, at the bell and not at her.

"We should leave it."

Ilse watched his mouth. He was not angry. That was the thing she noticed. He had the face of a man working a sum and disliking where the numbers landed.

"Say it again," she said.

"Fifteen kilos slung between us, and it is going to cost us." He put his bare palm flat on the bronze, the way she had taught him. "I can feel it. The weight is making us slow. Slow is how people die on mountains, Ilse. You told me that yourself. We can cache it. Mark the spot, come back in the spring. It is a bell. It has waited a hundred years."

"That is the best argument you have made since we left the road," Ilse said. "Look down there." She pointed into the icefall, where the cloud had gone from white to grey and was climbing the mountain instead of falling down it, which is what cloud does once the wind has turned. "In an hour that is a whiteout and we will not see the next terrace. When we cannot see, we go by rope and by voice, and a voice carries nowhere in a whiteout. But you can follow a bell. You put a bell in the ground and ring it and a man feels it in his bones and walks to it. It was rung through a night to bring six men home over ice like this. It already knows the way."

"So the bell is a radio," Hesk said, "and the radio is dead."

"The radio is not dead yet."

"It will be. And then we are four people in a whiteout carrying a bell, when we could be four people running."

"Four people who can find each other beat four people who cannot," Ilse said. "We take the bell."

Hesk held her eyes, and the snow went between them, and at last he took up the rear corner of the cradle. "Your call. It is your rope." That was not the end of the argument. Only the pause.

The bad hour began with Teodor.

They were crossing the long slope above the third terrace, where the slab had come down, and new snow had filled the old scar and laid a soft white apron over it. Ilse put her full weight on every step. Behind her, Teodor went into a hole.

Not a crevasse. A hollow under the new snow where a boulder had shifted and left a gap. His right boot punched through the crust to the knee, his pack swung round on his shoulder, and he went over sideways with the bell rope still in his fist. Ilse felt the rope jerk, threw her weight onto it, sat down hard. Hesk was on him before she had finished moving.

Teodor did not shout. He sat in the snow holding his ankle and breathing through his teeth. "Well," he said. "That's the day buggered."

Ilse cut his bootlace and worked the boot off. The ankle had already swollen and the skin had gone the colour of an old bruise. She felt it front and back with her thumbs. The bones were whole. She was almost sure the bones were whole. But a man with an ankle like that could not carry a bell.

"Can you stand?" she asked. He stood, put his weight on it, and went down at once with a sound like a sigh.

"That is not a sprain you walk off," Priya said. Her voice held steady. "In the archive we had a lacing ledger, and the shipping clerks wrote it down every spring, the ones who went up-country. The rule was, a bad ankle and the man rides. Two men carry him and the third goes ahead and finds the way."

"Nobody rides," Teodor said. "We have no litter and we are not building one. I can hop. I can do a three-legged crawl down a slope if it comes to that." He looked at Ilse. "Do not put a rope on me and hang me off your shoulder. You need your shoulder for the bell."

Ilse wrapped the ankle tight with the long bandage, pulled the boot back on, and laced it until the leather creaked. "You walk in the middle of the line now," she said. "Between Hesk and Priya. One tug for a good step. Two for a bad one. Three and we stop."

"That is the slowest pace on the mountain," Teodor said.

"That is the pace. Now walk."

He walked. It was bad. Each step was its own separate business, the good boot forward, the bad one dragged up with a little jerk, sweat beading on his grey face in the cold. They took the slope at a third of the pace they had come up it, and the cloud climbed the mountain and met them.

It came the way winter comes into a house when the fire goes out. The far wall went first, then the icefall below, then the ridge above, and the whole world folded itself into a grey room the size of a rope. The wind went from a hiss to a roar with no direction in it, and the snow was already in the air and going nowhere.

Ilse stopped the team, turned them, made them get down and crowd together with the bell between them. Her feet on the ice felt the wind through the ground, a hard high hum, and under the hum the deep irregular counting of the mountain. She pulled her mitts off with her teeth and laid her hands on the bronze, and through it she felt the snow under the cradle shifting. Not the ground. The snow on the ground, new snow and old slab beneath it, sliding down the grade toward the drop.

She felt her way back along the rope to Hesk, put her mouth to his ear, shouted. He shook his head, pointed at his own ear, and made the sign she had taught him. Nothing here. Then he raised his other hand, shut one eye, and made a second sign, slow and clear. And on this side the storm is too loud to hear.

Priya's face loomed out of the grey. She had the case up out of her pack, and she pulled off one glove and laid her bare hand flat on the wood and felt through it, and then her face changed and she grabbed Hesk's arm and put his hand where hers had been. Hesk looked at Ilse and needed no words. The ground under the case was still. The case sat on the solid old ice of the terrace. The bell sat on the moving snow.

The radio cracked against Ilse's shoulder. She got it up, worked the dial, heard a voice in the valley for four seconds. Then it ran down into a hiss, and the hiss flattened and went dead, and she shook it and worked the dial and got one more click and nothing. She put it away and did up the straps. There had been a promise in that radio, every four hours, and she marked it now as broken, and thought: the valley will keep the count even if we cannot. When we miss the fourth hour they will know. It will be dark by then, and the cloud will be down, and their knowing will not be able to help us.

Now the choice came. She could not shout it. The wind took every word and the snow took every hand shape past arm's length. So she gathered them into a huddle and used her hands on their shoulders, made the signs slow and wide. Radio dead. Bell goes on the ridge. Follow the ring.

Teodor's face came close. He read her lips. "Above the icefall?"

"They cannot find us in this. They can find the bell." She held up four fingers, pointed down the slope, then made a flat cutting sign. "Four hours and the cloud lifts. The icefall will be a mess below. Now."

Hesk crouched in front of her, his face close to hers. "It is the ridge. If we go up on the ridge in this we cannot come down the icefall at all. We will be above it with no way down."

"Yes."

"And if we stay here we are on a slope that is already moving."

"Yes."

He held her gaze, and the grey moved between them, and then he made the flat decision sign with his hand, took up the front of the cradle, and looked back at her, and Ilse understood that he had stopped arguing and started climbing.

They went back up the slope they had just come down, four on a rope with a bell between them and no world past the end of the rope. Teodor went up on the good leg and brought the bad one after and sobbed once between his teeth and went on. Twice Ilse felt the snow surge and settle under her boots, and once she felt the whole slope let go somewhere below in the grey and did not hear it and never learned where it had gone.

It took an hour and a half to gain ground they could have made in twenty minutes in clear air. Then they came up over the last of the slope onto the shoulder, and the Ridge of Echoes lay along the top of the icefall like a spine, black ice and packed snow and no cover, and the wind came over it with nothing in the way.

Ilse got them down against a great flat slab of rock. She ran the line to a piton she drove with four blows and tied Teodor off, then Hesk, then Priya, and made them sit with their backs to the rock and their knees together. Then she got the bell out of the cradle.

A high point on the ridge held a spine of rock sticking up out of the snow. She found a crack in it wide enough for the crown, laid the bronze in, wedged it with two flat stones, drove a piton above it and tied the crown so it could not walk away. Then she took the bar from the cradle's lashing and struck the bell.

The sound did not come through the air. She could not hear it and neither could any of them, and the storm ate it up. But her bare hand lay on the rock, and the rock took the sound and gave it to her, and it went down the ridge and into the frozen mountain in a long slow wave she felt in the bones of her wrist and the soles of her feet. She struck again and set the rhythm she had learned in the cave. Four slow beats and a rest. Four and a rest. Then she put the bar into Teodor's hands, got his hand on the rock, made him feel it, held up four fingers and pointed at the bell, and Teodor took the bar and set it on the bronze.

The hours that followed were not hours a person counts. They sat in the lee of the slab and kept the bell ringing, and the wind kept blowing, and the world stayed folded in grey.

Priya sat with her back to the rock and the case in her lap. Her hood was down; the wind had torn it back. Her hair was stiff with rime and her face had the flat far look of a person who has stopped being cold and does not yet know that this is worse.

At the turn of one of the rings she said to Hesk, "I lied to all of you."

"I know," Hesk said, without heat. "You tried to burn the page."

"That is not the lie. I told Ilse I was a woman who had found some papers. I told base camp the archive funded the equipment. It put in a little. The most of it was me. My savings and a loan against my flat and a bad arrangement with a man in the city who calls himself a banker. If this had failed I would have gone home to nothing. And I thought that was why I was doing it. So that at the end I could say I had paid for the truth and it was expensive and I was brave." She looked at the case. "That is a lie too. I was doing it so I would not have to read the truth about my own family."

Hesk looked at her while the snow went between them in the wind. "The guide," he said. "Karel Vasek. He was the one with the painting."

"He was the one with the painting. The east hall has him with the bell-rope in his hands and his mouth open, singing, coming down out of the sky to save them. He was in a tent with his shoulder broken and his face broken and could not say what day it was. And every plaque in my country says his name, and my great-great-grandfather's name is the cook's shame. My mother whispered it. I was not allowed to ask." Her voice was flat. "He rang the bell all night in an ice cave and my mother taught me to be ashamed of him."

"He rang it all night," Hesk said. "With no way down and no way up. Why ring it at all, if no one could hear and he could not hear the answer."

"Because it was the only thing he could do," Priya said.

Hesk looked down the ridge to where Ilse stood at the bell with the bar in her hands.

He got up, came along the rope, stood beside her and put his own bare hand on the rock.

"I see it now," he said, speaking slowly so she could read it. "You are not calling the mountain. You are asking it where it is going. He did the same. Weiss did not ring it for the men who could hear him. He put it in the ice and asked the mountain to carry his voice where he could not."

Ilse struck the bell and felt it go down through the ridge, and gave him a nod. He gave her one back, the nod he had given her in the icefall, the one that meant he had felt the ground go wrong and was glad she had felt it first.

"Your bet is unpaid," Ilse said.

"Let it stay unpaid," Hesk said. "It was a stupid bet."

The light failed without their seeing it fail. The grey went darker, the snow turned to a moving blackness, and there was no night and no day, only the ridge and the bell and the four of them.

They rang through the first of it. Teodor rang first, and his hands were good, and Ilse lay in the lee of the slab where she could feel the strokes through the rock and corrected the rhythm with her palm when it drifted. Then Hesk rang, then Priya, then Ilse again. None of them could hear the bell and all of them could feel it.

"They might not come," Priya said, in a lull between the sets, her hands between her knees and her voice slow with cold. "Even if they hear. No one can climb in this."

"They will have missed us by now," Ilse said. "The valley keeps a clock even when we do not. They will be at the foot of the icefall, and they will hear nothing, and they will stop and wonder. Then one of them will feel the ground go small and tight under his boots, and he will put his hand down, and he will walk up the mountain to the place it is coming from. The rescue is not a thing you hear. It is a thing you follow."

"We could try the descent," Hesk said. He had been silent a long while. Now he crouched by the slab, and he was asking, not arguing. He was a guide asking a guide. "The ridge runs down the east side of the icefall. The old line went that way. If we roped the bell and lowered it down the east face, slowly, four at a time, we might make the bottom before the worst of the cold. If we stay here we freeze. Might is better than sitting."

"You have not felt the east face." She got up, went to the edge, put her bare hand on the ice through the snow. It was loose and grainy and moving against itself, the whole face sliding, a slow slide in the dark. "Feel it yourself." Hesk came and put his hand where hers had been and stayed there a while, and then took his hand away and wiped it on his coat and said nothing.

"It is a sieve," Ilse said. "Four hundred feet of a sieve on a face you cannot see. We would be down the first pitch and the whole thing would take us, and the bell would be under it, and no one would have a bell." She stood. "We stay. We ring. We wait for the mountain to tell us when."

Hesk made the flat decision sign and went back to the slab and sat down.

"Have you ever been in one that did not end?" Priya asked.

Ilse let the question sit. She was thinking of another mountain and a sentry line she had set too late, and a partner who had not come home. That was a long way from here and the two things were not the same. She made herself look at Priya. "I have been in one when it ended wrong. The mountain does not tell you which kind it is until the end. So you keep ringing."

She struck the bell. Four and a rest. The rock took it and she felt it go down the ridge into the dark and out into the mountain, and somewhere far below, down in the valley and the icefall and the black, the mountain carried it on.

The night settled on the Ridge of Echoes and the wind went on, and four people and a bell sat in the lee of a slab of rock above a shattered icefall with no radio and no way down, and kept a sound alive in the ground.
