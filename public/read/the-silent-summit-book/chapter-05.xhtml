The Bell and the Book

They cut in turns. Teodor took the first shift, Hesk the second, Ilse the third, and Priya held the lamp and the bag and took no turn at all, and nobody asked her to. Wind kept swinging around the shelf and throwing fine dry snow into the mouth, and every time Ilse stopped to clear her eyes she felt the weather window swing a little further shut.

Most of the morning went into opening a hole a person could look through.

Ilse went in first. She put her face to the gap and cold air came out against her skin, old and still, faintly sweet with the smell of iron. She struck the lamp and pushed it through, and the light went into a room.

The room was not large. A low blue ceiling pressed down over it, the walls curved away into a dark the lamp could not reach, and black ice lay smooth and clean across the floor. Nothing lived in it. Nothing had fallen in it. In the middle, hanging from the iron bar that ran across the cave mouth, hung the bell. Silent in the dark, its mouth turned toward the floor, its crown bright with a thin skin of frost where the bronze had not gone green. The bar took its weight. The bar had taken it since 1908.

Smaller than she had imagined. Heavier than it looked. Green at the rim, brown along the shoulder where the wet airs of a century had gone at it, and letters stood cast into the band below the crown, worn but clear enough. Ilse laid her hand flat on its side. The bronze met her palm with a cold like water, and she felt nothing at all. No hum. No wire. The bell had kept its silence all those years and it stayed silent, and the only thing in it was the cold.

Behind the bell, against the far wall, stood a wooden case about the size of a soldier's footlocker, bound with iron and half swallowed by the ice that had crept up around it. The lid was shut, the lock rusted hard, and on the top, where the frost had frozen around a shape, lay a parcel wrapped in oilskin.

Priya came through the hole and stopped dead.

She went past the bell to the case and stood over it with the lamp held high, and Ilse watched her hands begin to shake, and then Priya set the lamp down very carefully on the ice and said, in a voice gone small and flat, "It is his. The case is his. It has the expedition's mark burned into the lid, the three crosses and the ring. The letters described the case. They described it exactly."

"Open it," Teodor said. "Gently."

Hesk and the small axe freed the lid from the ice that held it. The parcel on top came away with the frost still clinging to its oilskin, and Hesk set it aside and cleared the ice from the lock, and when the ice was gone Priya knelt and worked at the rust with a bar and her own gloved hands until it gave and the lid came up. Inside, packed in wool gone black with age, lay a second bundle wrapped in oilskin hardened into a shell. Beneath the bundle sat three empty bottles, a tin of hard biscuit, and a coil of rope so old it had gone to powder when Priya touched it.

She lifted the oilskin bundle out and laid it on the ice and peeled the shell away with her thumbs, careful and slow, the way a person unwraps something that might break. Inside was a book. A plain ledger, the kind any clerk would have kept in any valley town, bound in cloth gone black, its pages stiff and swollen with the wet and then the years, and Priya opened it with both hands flat and read the first page aloud.

"This is the book of Anders Weiss, cook to the expedition of 1908, who cannot hear and who keeps his account in writing that the truth may be here when the storm is done."

Nobody spoke. The lamp hissed. Priya turned the page and read on.

"The evening of the eleventh of September the mountain came down upon the camp. The master Vasek was struck in the tent and took harm upon his shoulder and his face and did not wake. The others ran to the lower camp in the dark and the snow took the path behind them. There were seven living and the storm was upon us and there was no way down and no way up and no voice that could carry. So I took the bell that was kept for the signal, and I hung it upon the iron bar and I rang it, and I rang it, and I rang it through the night, and the men came to the sound by threes and by twos and the last of them came at the grey of the morning with his hands froze to his chest. Six lived. One did not. I rang until my arms were dead and then I rang with my feet upon the rope and the ringing went into the ice and the ice carried it down the mountain like a road."

Priya turned another page. Her voice had steadied into the flat academic tone she used in the archive, and it held that steadiness the way a hand holds a heavy weight at arm's length.

"And I will set down here that the master Vasek was a good man and a brave one and that he did not know what happened in those hours, for he was in the tent as I have said. But the account that walks down the mountain will be written by men who were not here or who wish the story to be another story, and I cannot stop it, and so this is my book and it is the truth and I leave it in the place of the bell for whoever comes."

Ilse looked at the bell. The lamp threw its shadow long across the ice floor, and the letters on the band below the crown stood out sharp. She moved the lamp along them and read: Cast by the foundry at Saint-Vincent for the Alpine Signal Service, and below that, in smaller letters worn almost away, Let the mountain answer.

She put her hand back on the bronze, and this time something came. Not from the bell. From the ice under her boots, the whole shelf and the peak above it, the slow deep pulse of the mountain going on, and inside that pulse, small and certain, like a struck wire, the memory of what the bell had sent down through the ice all those years ago. It had gone out through the rock and the snow and the frozen ground, and the men below had felt it in their feet and walked to it. She had the bell now. It spoke through the ground and not through the air, and it spoke to the deaf as well as the hearing, and the man who rang it had known that in his hands and in his bones.

"He rang with his feet on the rope," she said. "He felt it through the ice. He knew the mountain would carry it."

Priya gave no answer. Her face had changed while she read. The tightness was gone out of it and in its place was something worse, something loose and bright and close to breaking, and her hands had begun to shake again on the book.

"Hesk," Ilse said. "Take the book and put it back in the case and close the case. We photograph everything now, in place, the bell and the case and the book open where it lies. Teodor, the lamp high. Priya, step back and breathe."

Priya stayed where she was. She had the book in both hands and she was looking at the lamp on the ice, and then she was looking past it at the flame, and her thumb had found the corner of the first page and turned it, and the paper, dry with a century of winters, lifted.

"Priya," Ilse said.

"I cannot," Priya said. "You do not understand. My name is in this book. My great-great-grandfather. He was not the hero. He was the man they blamed. Every schoolroom in my country, every plaque, the whole record of him, the whole thing. My mother would not say his name. She called it the cook's shame. I have spent my life in the archive that hangs his disgrace on the wall in the east hall, and I told myself I was going to correct it." Her voice cracked and the page bent in her hand. "But it is not a correction. It is a confession. My family is the shame. The cook rang the bell and the guide took the praise, and if this book comes out it will say my great-great-grandfather let another man's name be carved into the stone for a century and said nothing."

"He could not read the stone," Ilse said. "He could not hear the praise. He left his book in a cave where no one would find it. He was not hiding. He was waiting."

"I am the one who is hiding." And she put the corner of the page into the lamp flame.

The paper caught at once. It went up the leaf in a bright orange ripple, faster than any fire Ilse had seen, and the smoke was white and the flame ate along the brittle edge, and Priya stood holding a burning page with both hands as though she wanted it to come to her.

Hesk crossed the ice in two strides. He did not grab the book. He took her wrist and pinched the burning corner flat between his glove and his palm and crushed it out. The flame died against his leather with a small wet hiss. He held it crushed a moment longer, watching the edge for any glow, then opened his hand and let the smoke go, and there was a scorch on his glove the width of a thumb.

The page was saved, but not whole. Where the flame had begun, and where Hesk had crushed it, a bite had gone out of the corner, brown-edged and ragged and the size of a thumbnail, and the first line of Anders Weiss's account, the line with his name in it, ended now in a charred absence; a smudge of soot lay across the rest, and the words beneath it were black and clean and legible. The smoke hung in the roof of the cave in a thin white breath.

The shelf outside had gone very quiet. The wind had dropped. Ilse had not felt it drop.

Priya stood with her wrist still in the air where Hesk had held it and looked at the book. "I am sorry," she said. "I am sorry. I am sorry." She said it three or four times and then stopped and stood there breathing.

Hesk let her go. He stepped back and pulled his glove off and looked at his palm, where the leather had burned through in a small brown coin, and then he looked at Priya, and there was no contempt in his face that Ilse could see. He looked like a man who had woken up on a mountain.

"Get the case," he said to her. "You are the archivist. Get the case and pack the book and do it like you were taught. I will hold the light."

Priya knelt and did it. Her hands had steadied. She wrapped the book back in the oilskin and the wool and set it in the case and closed the lid and locked the rusted lock with the bar, and she did it slowly and carefully and without a word, and when she had done it she sat back on the ice and wiped her face with the back of her glove and looked up at Ilse.

"Ask me," she said. "Ask me what was in the pack."

"I know what was in the pack now," Ilse said. "I have known since the storm, when you could not stop speaking of him. It was never your guilt I was watching, Priya. It was your hands."

"I was going to bury the truth," Priya said. "I told myself I would photograph the case and take the bell and say the book was too damaged to read. I have spent a year telling myself that. And then I read the first page and heard his voice and I could not bear it." She looked at the ruined corner of his first page. "I nearly burned his name out of the world."

"Nearly," Teodor said from the wall. He had not moved all this time. "And the rest safe in the case. It will make the record longer, not shorter. A book that survived a fire is a better witness than a book that did not."

They photographed the cave before they moved anything. Priya did it herself, with the camera from her pack and the plates in their case, and she worked the way Ilse had seen her work with the permits and the map, precise and slow and thorough. She photographed the bell where it hung from the bar, the crown letters, the iron bar itself and the mark of the rope upon it, the case with its lock and its mark, the first page open with its burned corner black against the ice, and she wrote every exposure in a small book in a firm hand and signed the bottom of each page. When it was done she packed the camera and then she turned to Ilse and signed, awkwardly, with the three signs she knew, book safe, name safe, and Ilse signed good and put her hand on Priya's shoulder, and it was the first time Priya had let anyone touch her without flinching since the moraine.

The bell came down off the bar with three of them working and Teodor holding. It weighed more than fifteen kilos. Teodor took it at the crown while the others eased it off the iron, and when they laid it into the rope cradle Ilse had built, its mouth turned down, the cave seemed emptier for its going. It did not sing. Not then. It lay in the cradle and settled and was still.

When they were through the hole and out on the shelf, the light struck the bell and the bronze took it and held it, and for a moment in that white flat afternoon it glowed as though it were warm.

They carried it to the tent and ate and drank and melted snow. Then Ilse took the radio from its case and set it on the rock outside the tent and turned it on, and Teodor stood behind her with the antenna wire and held it high while she worked the dial. The air on the other side of the shelf was clear. The barometer had been falling since noon, but the sky was still open, and the radio found the valley after a short hiss of static and a man's voice came through the speaker, thin with distance and surprise, asking who was on the channel.

"Base camp, base camp," Ilse said, because Teodor could speak for her and would, but the radio could not carry her hands and her voice could carry this. "This is Marchetti on the shelf. We are all four alive. We have the bell and we have the book. The book is whole but for one scorched corner, scorched today and by our own hand, and the rest of it entire. The cook is the man. Anders Weiss rang the bell and his own hand wrote it, and his account is with us."

The speaker was quiet a moment. Then the man in the valley said something to someone behind him, and then he said it again to her, slowly, as though she had to be told. "Say again, Marchetti. Say again the cook."

"His name was Anders Weiss and he was deaf and he was not the man they blamed," Ilse said. "He saved six. He wrote it down. The bell is with us and the book is in its case and we are coming down."

The man in the valley began to shout, and his voice came through the speaker clean and loud and close, and the people in the valley made a noise like a room full of hands.

"Marchetti, listen to me," he said. "I have the forecast desk on the other line. The window has not changed. You have four days by the forecast and I will hold them to it, but I do not trust it and neither should you. There is cloud coming down from the north that the models have not placed and the barometer at this end has fallen nine millibars since noon. If it comes early you will not get four days. Call me every four hours. Do you hear me? Every four hours."

"I hear you," Ilse said. "Four days by the forecast. And the mountain has not read the forecast." She turned the dial down and wrapped the radio back in its case, and she stood with her hand on the cold rock of the shelf.

"I have never liked a radio that agrees with me," Teodor said, and he said it to Ilse's face so she could see his lips. "Look at the ridge. Look at the far wall. The cloud is down on the north."

She looked. The far wall had been white and clear when they carried the bell across the shelf. Now the north ridge had gone grey and soft and the ridge of echoes at the top of the icefall had disappeared into a low whiteness that was not snow but cloud, and the cloud was coming down, and the wind was coming with it. She had felt it in the ground when the wind dropped, long before the man in the valley said his barometer had fallen. The radio had agreed with her. That was what she did not like.

"The forecast says four," she said. "The mountain says two. Maybe one. The cloud is early and the wind is with it, and the ice over the col will be closed by it before the forecast desk knows what has happened." She looked at Teodor, and at Hesk, and at Priya holding the case against her chest, and she put her hand on the bell where it lay in its rope cradle against the rock, and through it she felt the shelf, and through the shelf the mountain.

She thought of the call the man in the valley had asked of her, every four hours, and of the hiss that was waiting in the radio for the moment the weather took the ridge. There would be no call at the fourth hour. There would be no call at the eighth. Somewhere in the dark of the next night the valley would begin to wonder why, and by the time their wondering became a search the mountain would have decided for all of them. She would not be able to tell them that. She would only be able to go down.

"We leave at first light," she said, "and we go down through the icefall in the dark, and we carry the bell, and we do not stop until the col."

The book was in its case and the case was in Priya's arms and the bell was in its cradle and the cloud came down the north wall and took the ridge of echoes, and the shelf went grey, and the four of them stood in the lee of the rock with the bell between them and the night and the descent still to come.
