The Archive

The city met her with a noise she had not expected.

Buses talked through the pavement. Trucks talked through it deeper. The building she stood in front of had machines in its belly, and the turning came up through her boot soles as a fine continuous hum, not unpleasant, like standing on the skin of a sleeping animal. She pressed her palm to the entrance stone. A man in a coat asked her twice whether she needed help, slowly, the way people did. She smiled, shook her head, and went up the steps.

The archive was a great grey stone building built to look as though nothing in it could ever be wrong. That was the first thing she distrusted. The mountain had never once looked as though nothing in it could be wrong.

Reporters crowded the steps, more of them than there had been climbers on the whole mountain. Priya went up ahead of her with the case in her arms and her chin lifted, and the reporters made a lane for her the way a river makes a lane around a stone.

The east hall was full. Ilse stopped in the doorway and felt a hundred people through the floorboards, all leaning the same way, the way a boat leans when everyone aboard has gone to one rail. At the end of the room hung the painting, two men high, showing a strong man in a climbing coat with a rope in his hands and his mouth open wide as though he were singing, and at his feet a small dark figure with a tray of cups. Karel Vasek with his mouth open. The cook at his feet.

The bell stood on a plinth beneath it. The logbook lay open in a glass case beside it, and the burned page had its own small frame and its own small light.

Priya spoke first, because Ilse had told her she would and because Priya had asked for it.

"For a hundred and twelve years," she said, "this painting has told you that a guide named Karel Vasek rang the bell in the storm of the eleventh of September, nineteen hundred and eight, and that his courage brought six men down from the ice." She stopped. "I am Anders Weiss's great-great-granddaughter, and I am here to tell you that the painting is wrong. The man who rang the bell was the cook. The cook was deaf. The guide lay unconscious under a rock and did not wake until it was over."

She lifted the logbook and the burned page together so the lights caught them.

"This is his ledger. He wrote down every hour of that night, and every name, and how he rang. The first page was burned by me, in the ice cave on the mountain, with the flame I brought up there for the purpose. I meant to finish the job and burn the rest. Hesk Vandry crushed it out with his glove, and it has a hole in it the size of that glove, and both of those things are the record now." She let that sit. "I carried the flame up the mountain because I had made a plan to keep it burned. I intended to come home with a book that agreed with me. I intended to bury him again. I want that written down with the rest of it. There is a preface in the copies you will be given today, written by the woman who brought the bell down off the mountain, and it is not a kind preface, and I asked her for it."

She set the logbook down and put her hand flat on the glass.

"He rang through the night. He had no way up and no way down, and he rang anyway. I have been ashamed of my family for thirty years and I have been wrong about the shape of the shame." She lifted her head and looked at the open mouth in the painting and held her eyes there. "The shame is not that my great-great-grandfather was a cook. The shame is that this building hung his picture and called the cook a coward and did not look for the truth for a hundred and twelve years. I have brought it home. I ask the archive to change the wall."

Nobody clapped. A hundred people holding still, and the holding went on so long she could feel it as pressure in the floorboards. Then a woman in the front row stood, and another, and the clapping came unevenly. Near the back a heavyset man in a work coat was on his feet shouting that the whole thing was a lie and a cook could not have rung a bell he could not hear, and the people around him were turning away from him, and two of them were telling him to sit down. Ilse felt his stamping through the boards the way she had felt the slab move on the hanging slope, and she was not surprised. He was shouting anger, and anger was honest. The fawning was what she did not trust.

The committee sat in a room off the east hall with a long table and a wet window. They were five, two of them from the newspaper, and the sponsor's man wrote on his clipboard now that he had something to write. Priya sat at the end with the logbook in front of her. Teodor sat in the chair beside Ilse with his bad foot up on a second chair. He had come on the train with a stick, and the doctor had told him to keep off it another month, and he had listened to the doctor the way he listened to everyone, which was to say not at all. He had been cheerful all morning, and Ilse knew what his cheerfulness cost, and had stopped trying to take the foot off the chair.

The archive's own man produced the letter first. He was a careful young man with ink on his cuff, and he laid it on the table and smoothed it flat with two fingers as though it might otherwise get away.

"This was delivered to us a week ago," he said. "It is a claim. A nephew of a nephew of Karel Vasek writes that the bell was Vasek's property and was carried up by Vasek's men, and that the archive is holding it without title. He asks the committee to determine ownership before the bell is exhibited." He looked up. "There is a second letter, from the newspaper. Their man in the valley wants the claim heard in public and has asked us for a statement."

"That is what we shall write down," the newspaper man said, pleased with himself. "Whose bell is it?"

"Vasek's men carried it up," Priya said. "And a cook rang it. A nephew of a nephew is welcome to the account and not welcome to the bell."

The five of them looked at one another, and the young man with the ink on his cuff took up his pen and did not write with it. Ilse felt the hesitation go round the table through the wood and the floor, the way a rope goes soft when everyone on it has stopped pulling.

"The claim wants a public statement," the newspaper man said again. "I should like to know what the archive intends to say."

"Then say this," the thin old woman said. She had been watching Ilse since she came in and had not laughed at anything, and now she spoke in a voice long used to doing business in that room. "The archive has examined the claim. The bell was raised by an expedition and has been given to the people of this city, and any man who believes he owns it may put his belief before a court, at his own cost, and the archive will meet him there." She turned her glasses on the newspaper man. "That is the statement. Write it exactly and print it under whatever headline you like. And tell your man at the back of the hall that we have heard him shout and that a bell rung by a deaf man is a bell rung by a man who could feel it and not hear it, which is the whole of what the building has failed to understand for a hundred and twelve years."

The newspaper man wrote it down. The young man with the ink on his cuff wrote it down also, and his mouth said nothing about whether he liked it.

"The bell came off the mountain in the hands of a deaf guide and a cook's great-great-granddaughter and a boy who bet against them both," Ilse said. "It can sit here while the lawyers enjoy themselves. It is not going far."

The old woman turned to her. "Miss Marchetti. You led the expedition. You are deaf. And the boy who bet against you is enrolling in your school."

"He is the first one through the door," Ilse said. "He says he will say he was wrong every year until they are sick of him."

The old woman held her a while with her glasses. Then she said to the committee, "Then I move we put the cook's name on the wall before the lawyers put the bell in a cupboard. The archive has been telling a wrong story for a hundred and twelve years. I shall enjoy writing the correction." She looked back at Ilse. "And I should like to hear about the school. Education is our business here. If we have been telling children the wrong story, we should be the ones to tell them the right one, and tell them who carried it down."

That was how it began. A thin old woman, a sentence, a wet window. Before the committee rose it had become a grant.

Hesk came in from the street with his coat wet through and did the thing he had said he would do.

He had told Ilse he would do it at the school. He had not said he would do it here, and when she asked him why, he had said, "Because the trouble with a quiet apology is that the ones who need to hear it are usually not in the room."

"I bet a week's pay that a deaf guide would call a crevasse that was not there," he said. "I did it on the first morning at the moraine in front of the whole expedition. I said it because I did not want to believe someone could feel the mountain in a way I never would, and because a bet was easier than being frightened. She called the real crevasse on the glacier and the serac in the icefall and the slab on the hanging slope and the hollow under the new snow that took Teodor Brann's ankle, and she called the east face loose when I was all for going down it with four men and a bell. I was wrong every time. I have told her so on the mountain, and I am telling the city so in this building, so it will go in the record with the rest." He laid a folded paper on the plinth beside the bell. "My week's pay. It is the first money in the school, and I am the first pupil, and I will say this every year on the first day until they are tired of me."

The hall went quiet in a soft way. A woman near the front said something to her neighbour, and the neighbour shook her head and smiled at the boy standing by the bell with his wet coat and his folded paper.

Ilse said nothing to him. He said nothing to her. They stood together in the doorway and watched the hall, and that was enough.

The school started in a room above a bakery in the valley town, and the smell of bread came up through the floor all morning.

Four pupils. That was all they had money for. One of them was Hesk, two were women who had been told all their lives that a deaf guide was a kindness and not a trade, and the fourth was a boy of sixteen from the valley whose parents thought he was there to learn rope work and had not been told the rest of it.

Teodor came up on his stick and sat on a stool against the window with his bad foot out and taught them the holds. He was gruff and he was good at it. He put Hesk on a bight of rope and made him tie it until his fingers knew it better than his brain, and he did not talk while they tied, and he watched them the way a man watches a knot in the wind.

"I am not teaching you this because I am fit," he said on the second day. "I am teaching you this because I am not. If a man cannot climb for a season, he can still teach what he knows, and a school that only teaches when the teacher is fit is a school with no teachers in it half the year." He tapped the rope. "Now tie it again. The first way was a guess. I do not teach guesses."

In the fifth week Ilse started teaching the listening. She took them onto the screes above the town before light and had them stand in a line with their eyes shut and their boots on the ground.

"What am I listening for?" the boy of sixteen asked.

"Do not listen," Ilse said. "Feel. The stones are telling you how they lie. When a man walks up the slope below you, the stones will tell you he is coming before you see him. Put the whole of your foot down. Wait."

They stood in the cold for an hour. Nobody felt anything and nobody admitted it. Then Hesk put his hand on the ground and said, in a voice trying not to be excited, "The wind is coming from the east. The stones are ringing on that side. I can feel them ring."

"Good," Ilse said. "That is the wind on the stones. Now feel the difference when it is a man."

She walked down the slope behind them and they felt her come. She watched their faces and remembered a boy in a snow cave with his palm on the ice, asking her whether the mountain was always talking. It was always talking. The whole trick was to be still enough to hear, and stillness could not be taught, only practised.

Priya came to the room above the bakery in the sixth week with a folder under her arm and set it on the table.

"Three places a year," she said. "Tuition, bed and board, boots and a coat. Two from the valley and one from anywhere. It is not enough to be rich and it is too much to be nothing, and the interest on my flat will pay for it if I am careful and do not eat in restaurants." She laid her hand on the folder. "I want it called the Weiss Scholarship. If the archive wishes to put my name to the money, they may, in small letters, at the bottom, where the lawyers can find it and the visitors will not. His name large. Mine small."

"Your name will not be small," Ilse said. "It will be in the preface. I have written it."

"I know," Priya said. "I have read the draft. You called me a coward and a fool, and then you said I brought the book down off the mountain and gave it to the men who would hate me for it, and that a woman who does that has paid for the bell twice, once in money and once in shame." She was quiet. "I have not been called a fool in print before. It is a great deal better than being pitied."

Ilse had written the preface in three days in a room in the archive while Priya slept at her flat and Teodor lay with his foot up and Hesk drilled ropework with the two women below. She had not softened it. She had put her own name at the bottom, and she had made it say, in plain words and early, that she was deaf and had led the climb by touch and signal. That line she rewrote more than twice. The rest of it she had written straight off, the way she read a slope.

They gathered one last time in the empty east hall on a winter evening before the archive closed, and stood looking at the blank wall where the painting had been.

The new painting was not up yet. A small card in the middle of the blank wall said In preparation — Portrait of Anders Weiss, Cook, 1908, and beneath it a smaller card said The ill-judged painting previously on this wall is available for study in the east annexe, where it is exhibited under glass with an explanatory label containing the word 'wrong' eleven times.

Hesk had counted the elevens. He said it was the finest thing the archive had ever done.

The bell stood on its plinth below the blank wall, and the logbook lay in its case with the burned page in its frame beside it, the charred edge dark against the light.

Teodor sat on a chair in the middle of the hall without asking. He was on the foot by then, a little, in a boot with a strap, and he carried the stick more for show than for use, and the doctor had said that by spring he could climb again if he took his time. He sat with his bad foot out and looked up at the blank wall and said, "I have climbed with this woman nineteen years and she has never once told me what she was going to do before she did it, and now she has a preface in a book that says the whole of it in plain words where anybody can read it. I read it twice. I did not recognise the woman in it. She is much better company in the preface than she is at four in the morning on a snow slope."

The thin old woman laughed until she had to take off her glasses.

Priya stood under the blank wall with her hands folded, not crying and not grey, looking at the word wrong on the card as though it were a place she had walked to from very far away.

Ilse walked to the middle of the hall and set her palm to the floor.

The building was quiet. The city talked to itself through the stones, a low steady hum, indifferent to the whole business of the hall. She felt the bell on its plinth, a small dense weight above the boards, and she felt the people standing round her, and the winter cold coming in through the door that had been left open.

"Ring it," Priya said.

"Here?" Hesk said. "In the hall?"

"Here," the thin old woman said. "We are a library. If a bell cannot be rung in a library, where may it be rung?"

So Hesk took up the bar and swung it once, and the bell spoke.

Ilse did not hear it. She felt it come through the plinth and the old boards and up into her boots, a great round tone rolling through the floor and out into the stone, and the people in the hall turned their faces toward it the way people turn toward a thing that has reached them from very far away.

It had rung through a cave in an ice cliff in nineteen hundred and eight and brought six men home. It had rung on the Ridge of Echoes in a whiteout and brought four. It rang now in a grey stone building among parked cars and wet streets and people going home to supper, and one of the people standing in it was a deaf woman from the valley with her palm on the boards and her feet apart and the whole of the bell in her bones.

She felt it go into the street and up the stones and into the low winter sky over the city, and she knew, the way she had known on the mountain, that somebody somewhere was feeling it and did not yet know what it was.

"Again," she said.

He rang it again.

The tone went through her and through the hall and out, and she stood on the old boards with the echo of it in her feet and thought about a school above a bakery and a boy who bet against her and was the first through its door, and a cook who rang a bell he could not hear and was heard anyway.

"Again," she said. "One more, and then we will go and eat."
