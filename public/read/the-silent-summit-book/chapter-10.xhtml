The Long Wait

The storm lay down over the col the way a big animal lies down. No fuss. No gusts. Just a great weight settling onto the rock. Ilse stood outside the tent with her boots in the new snow and read the weather through the stone under her: a long grinding pressure, the flat of a hand pressed on a drumhead and held there. Snow finer than sugar worked under her collar and into the seams of her gloves and into the hollow behind her ear. She had stopped sweeping it out.

They had come off the ridge in the dark, and the weather had followed them down. Nima had the tent standing before the worst of it hit. Then he went below with the doctor to bring the rescue line up the changed ridge. Dawa had the stove going in the porch. The two rescue men dug a platform the width of two bodies and stacked snow blocks in a low wall on the windward side. Hesk did most of the stacking, shoulder to shoulder with them, and she followed him through the rock, the small blunt taps of the shovel coming and going, and she thought: a man who stacks snow like that learned it somewhere, from somebody, and no book taught him.

Teodor lay in the middle of the tent with his ankle strapped and propped on a rucksack. The doctor had laced it on the ridge before the light failed and had forbidden him to put weight on it. Teodor had argued, lost, lain down. The bell stood in its cradle just outside the door, wedged in a crack of the col's rock, the bar through its crown, three slings of rope running down to a piton and a snow anchor and a loop of rope around the great stone. Ilse had been out twice to check the anchor. She had liked neither check. The wind was doing something to the slings she could not read.

"Four beats," Teodor said, his face close to hers so she could read his mouth in the grey. "Then a gap. Then four. If we get the rhythm wrong they'll think it's the wind."

"The wind does not ring clean," Ilse said. "The bell rings clean. The mountain will tell them the difference. The rescue men know the rhythm. They have rung it back to us on the ridge, and they will hear it again."

She turned her head toward the porch. "Who has the stove?"

"Dawa's man. He is better at it than any of us."

The night came down without their seeing it come. The grey went darker. The tent went from pale green to a blackness with a glow at the stove, and the wind took the steam off the pot and carried it away before anybody could feel it warm their face.

They ate the last of the nuts and four squares of a chocolate bar that Ilse's knife split into exact quarters. She watched the hands come and take their pieces and took none herself.

"You will eat," Teodor said. "That is not a request."

"I will eat when I have seen the anchor."

"You will eat now."

She put the square in her mouth and chewed. It tasted of nothing. The altitude had taken her appetite on the second day above six thousand and had not seen fit to give it back. She ate because Teodor would not stop watching her if she did not. He had been her voice on nineteen years of rope, and in all that time he had never once been wrong about when she was lying to him.

The tent held two men and two women in a space built for two men. The rescue men were in their own tent below the col's lip. Ilse had her feet against the door panel, so she could feel the wind through the fabric and the ground through the floor and, between the two, the whole troubled restlessness of the col. Teodor lay beside her with his bad ankle raised. Priya was opposite, the case against her ribs. Hesk had put himself at the head end, where the wall leaned the most, because his shoulders were the broadest.

"Tasks," Ilse said. "We do not lie here and wait for morning. The morning will come late and we want to be there to see it. Priya. The stove and the fuel and the water. Keep the pot full of snow and keep it warm. Every hour, wake one of us and make us drink."

"Every hour," Priya said. "I can do that. I want something to do."

"Hesk. The bell and the slings and the door. You go out every hour and check the anchor and dig the door clear. Do not stay out beyond counting a hundred. If the wind takes you, lie down and shout."

Hesk's head came round. "The bell will hold. I set it."

"The bell will hold or it will not. You will check it."

"Every hour," he said. "I'll check it. Every hour, and I'll shout if the mountain takes me." No heat in it, and none of the old challenge. He said it like a job he had been handed and meant to do well.

"Teodor."

"I am not to move," Teodor said. "I know. I am the cargo."

"You are the warm one," Ilse said. "You are between Priya and me, and the wind will pull the heat out of the door side first. You keep still and you keep the two of us warm and you tell me when the wind changes. You know the mountain's sounds better than any of us."

He was quiet a moment. "I know the mountain's sounds," he said, "and I am not hearing the one I want to hear."

"Nobody is hearing it yet," Ilse said. "We will. The doctor has your ankle and the rescue men have the line and we are not lost. We are waiting. There is a difference, I have been in both, and this is the better one."

The first hours were a long hard labour of nothing.

Hesk went out on the hour and came back with his shoulders white and his breath sawing, and every time he came back he said the same words: "Anchor's good. Slings are standing. Door's clear." The first time he said it with a question in it. By the third time it had settled into a flat report. By the fifth time Ilse heard something underneath the flatness that she had heard in his voice in the icefall: the effort of a proud man doing a small job properly because he had been given it.

Priya kept the pot going and woke Teodor for the water first because he was nearest, then Ilse, then Hesk. Somewhere in the third hour Ilse woke with a jolt, felt the silence beside her, and understood.

"Priya," she said. "Priya. The pot."

Priya was crouched with her hands round her knees and her face gone slack and pleasant. The stove had gone out. The pot was cold. The wind had banked snow against the outer wall up past the door panel.

"Priya."

"They'll come," Priya said. "They always come. My mother said, my mother always said—"

Ilse got a fistful of Priya's jacket at the shoulder and shook it, hard, once. Priya blinked and looked at her. The slackness went out of her face and something frightened came into it, which was better.

"There is no mother on this col," Ilse said. "There is me and there is the pot. You rest too long, you go warm and then you go cold and then you stop. Get the stove lit. Nuts. Three of them. Then the pot, then wake Hesk, then the pot again, then me. Count it out."

Priya's lips moved. Stove. Nuts. Pot. Hesk. Pot. Ilse. She got her hands working and the stove bloomed again. Ilse lay back down with her heart going and felt the col through her boots. The col felt the same as it had an hour before. Still there. Still holding.

She thought about the ones who went warm and did not come back, and she thought that she had very nearly been one of them, sitting asleep with her chin on her chest and the mountain turning under her.

Around the middle of the night the wind shifted. The tent wall on Hesk's side stopped pushing and started banging, and Teodor said, "If we get a cross-wind at this wall the blocks will go."

Ilse felt it through the floor and did not need the words. The blocks on the windward side had been stacked by a man with a broad back and a certain pride in his stacking, and a cross-wind at this angle would take the top courses off and bury the tent in one motion.

"Hesk. Blocks. Rebuild from the door end, three courses, lean them in. Take the shovel and take your time. Ask the rescue man below to lend you his and his hands if you need them. Teodor will tell you through the wall when the wind changes."

"I'll go," Priya said. "I can hand him the blocks."

Ilse looked at her. The stove had been lit again, Priya's eyes were moving, her hands were working. A body with a task in it was worth two bodies without one.

"Go. Count yourselves. One on the shovel, one handing. Both of you breathe."

They went out. Ilse lay against Teodor on the door side and followed them through the floor, the two of them in the snow with the wind on them. She felt the shovel strike and the blocks go into place and the wind hit the new wall and the wall hold. It held, and held, and went on holding. Then the two of them came back in, and the door was wedged shut behind them, and Priya sat with her back against the case and her head down, and Hesk crouched at the head of the tent with the snow melting off his shoulders onto Teodor's rucksack.

"Sixty," Hesk said. "All told. We counted the hundred and then we came in. The rescue man gave us his shovel and took the hand-offs."

"The wall is good," Ilse said. "It will hold till morning."

"How do you know?" He said it as a man asking, not as a challenge.

"I felt it hold. The shovel rang against it clean. A wall that is going to go rings dull first. Yours rang clean."

The night went on and on and did not end, and Ilse came to understand that there was no such thing as a night on the high mountain, only the storm going on for longer than a body could be sure of.

Hesk checked the bell and the slings every hour. Priya woke them to drink. Teodor told the mountain's sounds from the tent. Ilse lay with her feet to the door panel and read the col, and felt the cold settle into the rock itself. Not the air: the stone. A slow deep cold coming up out of the ground into the floor of the tent and into the hollows of her boots. Quarter past four in the morning by her watch when Teodor's hands found hers in the dark. His face was against her ear, the words coming warm on her cheek. "I have to tell you something and I will tell it once, and then we will go back to the pot."

"Tell it."

"I am frightened."

"You are not frightened," Ilse said. "You have been on nineteen years of rope with me and you have never once been frightened."

"I have been frightened nineteen years. Tonight I am frightened of something new." His hands tightened. "I am frightened of being a burden. I have been lying here since the doctor laced it, and I have been telling myself it is only the strap that has taken the feeling. But it is not only the strap. I cannot feel the foot at all now, not since the third hour, and when I try to move the toes they do not answer. The doctor said it was the strap and the cold and the height and the swelling, and the swelling has not gone down, it has gone up. If it comes to it and we have to move fast, I cannot move fast. You will have to choose what to do with me, and you will choose, and you will choose well, and that is what I am frightened of. I do not want you to have to do it. I do not want to be the thing that costs you."

Ilse lay still. Beyond the tent the wind went on with its grinding pressure on the rock. The bell stood in the crack with three slings of rope holding it. Hesk was due out with the shovel in a few minutes. She found that she had been waiting for this, had known it was coming, and had not wanted to be the one to open the door.

"I had a partner on the Kestrel," she said. "This was before your time. Marta. She was better on ice than me and she knew it and she let me lead anyway, because I asked her, and because she thought a deaf woman on a hard face was worth putting her name beside on the route." She stopped. Teodor's hands were still in hers. "We were coming down in bad weather. Not like this. Better than this. The sort of weather you descend in because the alternative is worse. I was on the front and I felt the snow bridge go soft under me and I called the anchor and Marta anchored. She anchored behind me. And I went on across, and it held me, and she came out onto the lip and the lip broke and she went. She was on the anchor and she went anyway. The anchor did not see her. Anchors are built for the fall behind you. Nobody builds them for the mountain giving way underneath."

"You have told me about the Kestrel before," Teodor said, "and you have never told me that."

"I have told you the weather and I have told you the route. I have not told you that I went first and the bridge held me and did not hold her. That is the part I have been keeping. That is the part I have not wanted anyone to know, because there is a way of saying it that makes me the woman who was kept and the woman who was let go, and I have not wanted to be either of those women." The air in the tent moved as someone breathed in sharply. Ilse knew without turning that Priya was awake and listening with her whole body, and that it could not be helped and should not be helped. "I carried it up this mountain and I have been carrying it every step of the way. I do not put my faith in a rope because I am brave. I do not put my faith in a rope because it has been tested. I put my faith in a rope because it has not cost me yet, and it will, and I know it will, and I keep climbing. That is the whole of it. That is the woman you have been frightened of being a burden to."

Teodor let out a breath that came from the bottom of him. "Then we are both frightened," he said, "and we will be frightened together, and I will not tell you again that my foot does not answer. I will tell you that it does not answer tonight, and you will tell me what to do with it, and I will do it."

"You will keep it still, and we will keep it warm, and you will tell me the mountain's sounds," Ilse said. "That was the task and it is still the task. When the light comes the doctor will look at the foot again, and he will tell us whether the strap has done the damage or the cold or the height, and he will tell us because that is what he is for. And if the time comes to move before he does, we will carry you, and there is a litter below with the rescue line and I will tell you that plainly so you can stop rehearsing it in your head."

Hesk sat at the head of the tent with the shovel across his knees. He had been listening. He reached over and put his gloved hand flat on Teodor's shoulder and left it there a moment and took it away, and then he said, in the flat voice he used for the report, "Anchor's good. Slings are standing. Door's clear. I'm going out to check them."

He went out. Ilse felt him go through the floor.

Priya said, quiet, from the far side, "I have been sitting here for an hour thinking about what I owe people and whether they would be better off without my money and my shame, and I am listening to a man say he does not want to cost the rope, and to a woman say she went first and was kept." She paused. "I do not think I have ever wanted to be useful before. I have wanted to be forgiven. It is not the same thing and I did not know that until now."

The storm began to thin before the true light.

Ilse felt it before she saw it. The pressure on the rock let go its grip and became a hand resting rather than a hand pressing. The wind came in gusts with gaps in them, and in the gaps the col told her plainly that it was still a col and still high and still above the changed ridge and the shattered icefall. She went out with Hesk while the sky was still a grey that was almost green, and they dug the door clear together and looked at the bell.

It stood in its crack with the crown bar through and the slings still standing and a cap of snow on its shoulders like a small old man in a hat. The rope was stiff and white. The piton had not shifted. Hesk put his hand on the bell and felt up the slings with his gloves and went down on his knees to dig the snow anchor out, and put his palm flat on the rock beside it and held it there.

"It has not moved," he said. "It will not move. I have felt it."

"Good," Ilse said. "Then we ring it once more, and we listen."

A man came up out of the weather below the col's lip. Ilse felt him through the ground before she saw him: the rescue man who had given Hesk his shovel, coming up the slope with his head down and a coil of line across his chest. He stopped at the edge of the snow platform and stood there blowing, and when he had his breath he said the thing he had come to say, and she read the shape of it on his mouth and felt the faint iron of it through the ground all in the same moment.

"They heard us all night," he said. "The whole line below heard the bell. The forecast desk still says four days and Nima says two, and Nima is going down to look at the icefall now the light is coming. There was never anyone lost to find. We knew where you were from the first hour you rang. You put us on the right side of the ridge in a whiteout, and a whiteout will kill a rescue line as quick as it kills a climb." He grinned. "So you have been ringing for nothing, if you count a rescue as a thing that only happens when nobody knows where anybody is."

"We have been ringing for everything," Ilse said. "The bell is what told you where we were and what told us where you were. There is no such thing as ringing for nothing."

He took up the bar himself and swung it. Ilse did not hear it and did not need to. She put her feet flat on the col and the tone came up through the rock into her boot soles and up the bones of her legs, a clean round note that went out into the thinning grey and down the col and into the long slope and the icefall below, and she stood and let it go. She felt the sound arrive in her teeth and in the bones of her hands before she could name it a sound at all, and that was the right order for it to arrive in.

She waited. The gust came and went. The col told her nothing she did not already know: the storm letting go, the ground holding, the men below moving.

"Again," she said, and the rescue man swung it again, and this time in the gaps between gusts she felt the answer come up through the ice and the ground, three beats and a pause and three beats, faint and steady, from the tents below the col. It was an answer and a greeting and a thing she had known all night was there.

Ilse turned round. Priya stood at the tent door and Teodor sat up in the doorway with the bad foot out and his face turned toward the sound, and all of them were still.

"Nima will have the line set from the ridge down by the time the light is up," she said. "The forecast desk is still wrong and the weather is letting go. We wait for the doctor and the light where we are, and when the light comes we go down to the icefall and Nima takes us by his east line under the wall, and the rescue man here will walk it first and tell us if the east line is passable. We do not put our faith in a rescue we cannot see. We put it in the bell and the rock and the four of us, and the rescue is already here and has been all night."

Teodor lifted the water bottle in his hand as though it were a glass and drank to her. Priya came out and stood beside Hesk with her arms round herself. Hesk took up the bar and looked at Ilse and waited for the word.

"Ring it," she said. "Slow. Four beats. Let them know we are here and that we are coming down."

He rang it, slow, four beats and a pause and four, and the note went out and down into the grey, and the answer came back up through the ice, faint and steady, like a man asking a mountain where it was going and getting an answer. The four of them on the col stood in the thinning storm and felt a mountain that had given them a night and was about to give them a morning.
