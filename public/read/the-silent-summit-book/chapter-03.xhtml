The White Pulse

The storm came down off the summit the way she had feared it would. No wind at first. Just a wall of white that swallowed the high bowl in an hour, and by the time the light failed it had them.

She felt the pressure turn before the wind got up, a dullness pressed against her jaw and her cheekbones, and she knew what it meant. Other mountains had taught her the feeling, and it had never once meant anything good. She called a halt at the foot of a névé slope, dropped to one knee, and let the cold climb through the joint into the bone of her shin. The whole mountain was curling inward, the way a body curls around a wound. Then the snow came, fine and dry and travelling sideways, and the world shrank to the width of the rope.

Teodor shouted. The word reached her only as the shape of his mouth and a shudder through the snow under his boots, and she understood it well enough. They dug.

They cut a shelter into the lee of a drift where the névé lay deep and stable, all six of them swinging axes and shovels while the wind filled the trench behind their heels as fast as they threw. Priya dug in her wet gloves until they went clumsy, swapped them for the spare mitts Ilse passed her, and dug again. She complained about nothing and stopped for nothing. Nima worked the left flank of the cut and Dawa the right, the two of them falling into the easy rhythm of men who had dug a hundred such shelters and expected a hundred more. Hesk swung his shovel like a man trying to prove something to the snow. The snow took no notice. An hour made a chamber big enough for six bodies and their packs. Another half hour smoothed the roof, cut a doorway, and plugged the wind with a bag of gear and a spare rope. Inside, six people breathing warmed the air fast. It smelled of wet wool, hot metal and fear.

They sat in a row with their packs between them. Beyond a metre of snow the storm roared, and to the others, Ilse knew, it sounded like a train crossing a bridge that had no end. To her it arrived as pressure, a constant low seething felt through her seat bones and up her spine, and it told her the snow was moving, the drift was holding, the shelter would last. She lit the stove, set a pot of snow to melt, and talked with her hands to Nima, who sat nearest the door with his face to the wind.

The first hours passed quietly. They ate two packets of noodles between six and drank the weak hot water the snow gave up. Priya counted the fuel canisters aloud, twice, and got the same answer both times. Dawa fell asleep sitting up, chin on his chest, and Nima shifted his legs without waking him so that Dawa's shoulder came to rest against the wall of snow. Hesk sat with his back to the drift and his eyes following Ilse's hands as she signed to Teodor, slow enough to read the shapes, though she knew he had none of the signs.

Then, near what would have been evening, the storm tore a corner off the roof. White poured in. They spent half an hour packing the breach with gear and snow while the wind reached in and found their faces. Priya held the headlamp. Ilse and Teodor packed the gap. Hesk passed them blocks of cut snow like a man feeding a fire, and nobody spoke, because there was no point.

The second morning came and was the same morning repeated, and that was its whole cruelty. Same roar, same pressure on the roof, same grey light leaking through the plugged door. They dug the door out three times and filled it three times. Hesk lost every hand of a card game Nima taught them with a deck that had lost two cards on an earlier expedition, and accused Dawa of carrying a lucky stone, and Dawa denied it with such dignity that everyone laughed, and the laughter made the shelter feel smaller and warmer than it was. Then the laughter ran out, and the day went on.

By afternoon the storm pressed so hard that even Ilse could feel the drift moving, a slow settling of the roof above their heads. They all watched the roof. Nobody said so. She signed to Nima and Nima signed back, and they rose and packed the roof with another three blocks, and it held, and they sat down again.

Teodor cracked the bank at last. He had been still for an hour with his back against the wall, and he said, quite clearly, in his flat old voice, "I do not like this one."

"You have been in storms worse than this," Priya said, glad of something to answer.

"I have been in storms where I could not see my own glove. That is not what I do not like." He turned his head to Ilse. "Tell her what the mountain is doing."

She looked at Priya, then at Hesk, then at the two porters by the door. "Under the storm there is another sound. A grinding, high up above the shelf. It started yesterday. It is getting louder. That is not the storm. That is the slope above us moving on its own bed."

"Will it come down?" Hesk said.

"Maybe. Not while the snow is falling like this. When the snow stops it will move, or it will not." She gave it to him straight, because a man in a snow cave deserves straight answers. "There is nothing to be done in this snow. So we sit, and we wait, and we do not spend ourselves."

Priya was quiet a moment. Then she said, in a tone Ilse could not read, "You always know, don't you. What the mountain is doing."

"Not always. Often." Ilse shifted her weight on the ice, felt the seething under it, and changed the subject the way she changed rope lengths. "I would know what you are carrying. Not the cases. The other thing."

Priya went still. Her hands found her pack and held it against her chest.

"I should have told you before we left," she said.

"Told us what?" Teodor said, and turned, and the whole cave turned with him.

Priya's jaw worked. She looked up at the curved roof of snow as though it might offer a way out of the sentence she was in. A little dust of snow came down and settled in her hair and she let it stay. "The money," she said. "The funding. It is not the archive's money. Not mostly. The archive put in a little for equipment and permits. The rest is mine. My savings and a loan against my flat."

Hesk sat up. "You are paying for this? All of it?"

"Two years of research and four months of grant letters that all came back no." Priya turned her palms over as though she had not expected to find them there, raw and red from the digging. "Nobody funds a correction. Nobody funds a plaque being wrong. So I did."

"That is a great deal of money for a plaque," Teodor said.

"It is not for the plaque." Priya's voice had gone flat and precise, the way it did when she was close to something she did not want to say. "It is for the plaque to come down. And for what goes in its place." Her hands came up and fell again. "The truth fits together like a key in a lock, and the lock has been shut for a hundred and eighteen years, and I am the only one who has the key. I could not hand it to a committee and wait. Nobody funds a correction," she said again, and her eyes were bright. "So I sold my quiet and my savings and I paid. And I did not tell you, because once it was said you would look at me the way you are looking at me now."

Ilse found, to her surprise, that she was not looking at Priya that way. She was thinking of the word record, caught in Priya's throat on the glacier, and the valley town she had mentioned and then walked away from. "It does not change the mountain," she said. "A rope does not care who paid for it. The bell does not care."

Priya put her face in her hands. Teodor reached across the gear and laid his big glove on her shoulder, and the shelter held. After a while Dawa found the stove again and made tea that was mostly snow, and they passed the cup around.

"You should have trusted us with it," Hesk said at last, slowly, as though he had been chewing the words. Nobody answered him, and he set the cup down and did not pick it up again.

"That is the trouble with a lie," Teodor said, not unkindly. "You tell it because you are afraid of the people you tell it to. Then you find the people are all right, and the lie is the only thing that was not." He pulled his coat around himself. "Sleep. Tomorrow we see what the mountain has done."

The storm held through that second night. It broke before dawn on the third morning, and it broke the way storms break in the high country, all at once and extravagantly. They came out into a quiet so complete that Ilse felt the absence of wind as a hand released from her throat. The sky was a hard pale blue. The snow squeaked under their boots, and every sound they made seemed too loud and carried too far.

Before they packed, she went a little way from the shelter and knelt and put both palms on the wind-scoured crust and let the mountain talk to her. Under the quiet the grinding was there, high up above the shelf. It had not stopped in the storm. It had not come down either. It sat up there, somewhere beneath the summit shoulder, moving on its bed.

Teodor came and stood beside her. "The slope above the shelf. The one we have to go under."

"Near enough."

"Then we go up fast and light and we do not linger," he said. "And we watch the ground."

They broke camp, and it was here that Nima called them together. He signed slowly, for Ilse, and Teodor spoke for the rest: his head had filled with a loud water in the night, the way it did at altitude, and he could not trust his balance on the icefall. He would take Dawa and go down by the way they had come, by the morning, while the snow was set and the weather held, and he would wait for them at the col with the radio and the spare fuel. Ilse read the signs twice and then put her hand on his shoulder and signed back that he was right to go and she would see him at the col. Dawa shouldered both their loads without being asked. She watched them down the first slope until the ridge took them, and felt the rope behind her lose two of its voices, and made herself turn around.

They climbed. The route wound through a maze of séracs that Ilse read with her feet and her hands and her eyes. Hesk had asked to walk second, behind her now that Nima was gone, and she had let him, and when the line reached a place where the route turned across a snow bridge she held up her fist and felt the stop signal travel down the rope and back up again, clean and quick, every hand in the line knowing it now.

She went forward alone, stood at the edge of the bridge, and pressed both palms flat on the snow. The bridge spanned a crevasse whose bottom she could not see, a narrow white arc over a blue-green dark. From the edge it looked solid. From the edge it always looked solid. She had watched two of them give way under other people, and she trusted none of them.

She pressed down and waited. The vibration came up through the snow, and it was wrong. Under the smooth firm drum of good snow lay a hollowness, and beneath the hollowness a slow grinding. She walked her hands along the bridge, and the hollowness thinned toward the middle into a drumhead sound, a skin stretched over emptiness. The bridge had stood there a long time and its underside had melted in the sun and there was less of it than there had been. She pressed harder at the centre, and through her palms came the faintest gravelly trickle, snow shifting down into the void. She took her hands away.

She walked back to the line and signed to Teodor, then, so the whole rope could see it, to Hesk.

"The bridge is bad," she said. "The middle is a skin. Not much left under it."

"Can we cross?" Teodor said.

"Roped and spaced and crawling, on a belay from both sides. Or we go around." She looked at the sky. Every hour lost now was an hour missing at the cave. "There is rock on the far side and a step. We go around."

"It is a bridge on the only easy route up the mountain," Teodor said. "Every party for a hundred years has crossed something like it."

"Every party for a hundred years has been one storm away from a burial," Ilse said. "We go around."

They took the rock step, and it cost them an hour and a half of hard climbing on cold rock in crampons. Hesk led the last pitch because he was light and quick and he had asked, and Ilse belayed him from below and watched his feet find the holds. He climbed more carefully than she had seen him climb before, and when he came down onto safe snow he tugged the rope three times, steady, and then a fourth, and she did not know what the fourth tug meant and suspected he did not either, but she felt it travel up the line to her hands. Priya came across last and slowly, and stood with her hands on her knees and her breath clouding around her face.

Beyond the step the ground flattened beneath a hanging slope, and the light was going. That was the whole of it: not fear, not the mountain, just the light going while the shelf still sat an hour above them. She called the camp, and they cut a ledge for the tent in the lee of a rock buttress, and while the others pitched it she stood a little apart and put her palms on the snow.

The grinding was close now, and it was not a thing in the far high ice. It was a thing above them, no more than a stone's throw up the slope, moving against its bed in small steps, the way a door moves before it opens. She had felt that once before, on the Kestrel, and she had gone first, and the bridge had held her and Marta had gone. She kept her hands where they were until Teodor came and stood beside her, and then she took them away.

"Tomorrow we cross under it," he said. It was not a question.

"In the dark, before the sun loads it," she said. "Fast. We do not stop under the crest, not for the view, not for the rope, not for anybody's bootlace. And we tell them tonight, so nobody is surprised in the morning."

They told them in the tent, over the stove. She signed it for Teodor's mouth and spoke the words herself, and she watched four faces take it. Hesk asked the question she expected, about the bridge, about whether going around had cost them the margin they needed, and she gave him the straight answer: it had cost the margin, and they were going anyway, and the crossing would be made in the dark. Priya asked nothing. Her hands lay folded on her pack, and her eyes stayed on Ilse's face the whole time, and there was something in them that was not fear of the slope.

"Ask it," Ilse said, when the others had rolled into their bags.

Priya sat up. "The cutter," she said. "In my pack. The small one. I told you it was for the document boards." She looked away. "It is for the page. The first page. If it is there. I have known since the city that if it is there I would take his name out of it, with the cutter if the fire was too slow, so that at least nobody would read it and know him for a liar." Her voice cracked and she went on. "And then the storm came and you put your hand on my shoulder and I could not say it in the snow cave with all of you looking. I am saying it now. Before the slope. In case there is no after."

Ilse turned on her side and looked at her across the stove, and the coals put a small red light on the roof of the tent. "You will bring it out whole," she said. "Both of us will. And the bell. And the page. That is the promise of the dark crossing."

"I do not trust myself to keep it."

"Then I will keep it for you until you can." Ilse put her hand out and Priya took it, briefly, and let go.

They went at four, in a dark that was more than dark because there was no moon and the lantern was hooded. The slope above them was a sound she carried in her legs more than her hands now, and she led across it at the pace she had promised: fast, and spaced, and no stops, with Teodor behind her and Hesk after him and Priya at the back. Halfway across, the ground under her right boot gave a small, terrible sigh, and she went down onto both palms without being told and felt the whole slope waiting, and she held, and it held, and she got up and went on.

Past the crest the ground smoothed. The route turned across the upper traverse and opened into the long white shelf, and the dark was thinning at its edge. She did not let herself look back until her boots were on the flat hard snow of the shelf, and then she looked back and counted the rope four times over and found every one of them.

The shelf ran back from a great hanging sérac, a broad white terrace, and on its far side the snow lay against a bank of black rock. The rock had a dark mouth in it that was not quite a cave and not quite a shadow.

"That is it," Teodor said. "We are where the grid puts it."

Ilse went forward alone and put both palms on the cold rock beside the mouth and gave it a long time. The mountain's deep pulse was in it, slow and heavy. Under the pulse, through the snow that lay against the rock, came something that was not the mountain, faint and fine, like a wire strung very tight and left to ring. She took her hands away and stood back.

"Can we cut it?" Hesk said.

"We can cut it," she said, "and we will, at first light. The mouth is plugged with a hundred winters of ice. It will take all four of us in turns." She looked at Priya, whose pack was in her arms, and at Hesk, and at Teodor with the slope still in his face. "But the slope above us is not going to wait for another evening, and a long cut at the plug is a long time under it. We take the morning to read what we can from the outside, and we cut, and if the mountain moves again we go down without it. The window is the window."

Priya stood at the mouth with her hands on the ice, and said nothing, and would not look away from it.
