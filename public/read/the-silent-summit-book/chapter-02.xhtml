Rope Signals

Day Two started in the dark, the glacier still blue under the last stars. They climbed an hour before Ilse called the halt. By then the ice had flattened into a wide white bowl, and the sun was lifting behind the eastern ridge, dragging their shadows out long and blue across the snow. She planted her axe, turned to face the line, and waited while Teodor walked the rope down and gathered them into a loose half-circle. Nima and Dawa stood at the edge of it. Dawa had already unclipped his pack and set it down.

“Signals,” she said, when they were all looking at her. “We agree them now, before the icefall. Not after. There is no arguing on the rope. There is no asking twice.”

She pulled a short length of spare cord from her pocket and held it up. “Two tugs, hard and even, mean stop. Stop where you are. Do not move. Do not turn around. Do not take one more step to a better footing. Stop and anchor.”

She tugged the cord twice, sharp. Teodor, beside her, gave a nod. Priya watched the cord as though it might bite.

“Three tugs, steady, mean climb. Move up. Keep the pace. If I tug three, the ground is good and I want you on it.” She did it. “One long pull, slow and heavy, means danger. Anchor now, where you stand, and hold. That is the one that matters. If you feel one long pull, you do not think about it. You drive your axe and you hold.”

“And if we need to run?” Hesk said.

“You do not run on a glacier. You anchor.” She looked at him until he looked away. “If you fall, the rope goes tight and I feel it. I will brace and I will hold you. You do not need to shout. You do not need to do anything but stop falling.”

She lifted a section of the rope and showed them how a tug travels. “The rope is the voice. You feel it through the harness, through the hips, more than the hands. When I tug from the front, the wave goes down the line like a plucked string. It fades but it does not vanish. The last on the rope will feel it weaker than the second. So we practice. We pass a signal front to back and back up again, until everyone feels it the same.”

They practiced in the bowl while the sun came over the ridge and the snow softened at its edges. Ilse stood at the head of the rope and Priya at the tail, with Teodor and Hesk between. She tugged two, and watched the wave travel down through Teodor's hips and into Hesk's and then to Priya, who flinched every time. Then three, and then the long pull.

The long pull went wrong the first time, Priya mistaking it for two. Ilse had them walk the signal up the rope and back down, turn and face each other, and do it again until Priya's answering pull came back clean and quick. Priya did not complain, and she did not flinch the second time, but her mouth stayed set.

“Good,” Ilse said at last. “Now the same with the hands, for when we are close. A flat palm means stop. A beckoning hand means come. A fist held up means hold.” She showed each one slowly. “Teodor knows all of this. He is my voice on the rope. If I am out of your sight, and the rope is not moving, you look for Teodor and you do what he says. Understood?”

They nodded. Hesk nodded last, and slower, but he nodded.

“One more thing.” She walked to Priya and took her by the wrist, gently, and lifted her hand to the rope. “You are at the back. You are the anchor of the line. If the rope goes slack in front of you, that is not a rest. That means something has happened ahead. You take up the slack until it is tight again, and you hold, and you wait for a signal. Never let the rope lie on the snow. A slack rope is a rope that cannot speak.”

Priya's hand tightened on the line. “And if it goes tight and stays tight?”

“Then someone has fallen, and you brace.” Ilse held her eyes. “You will feel it before you understand it. The rope will bite. Brace, and do not let go.”

They climbed on toward the icefall, and the morning warmed, and Priya found her way up the rope to walk beside Ilse for a stretch where the glacier ran flat and safe between two long ridges of moraine.

“Tell me about the bell,” Ilse said. She did not need the answer for the climb. She had learned long ago that people spoke more freely when their feet were moving and their eyes were on the ground.

Priya was quiet for several paces. Then she said, “The official account says Karel Vasek rang it. He was the expedition's chief guide, a famous man in his day, and the story that came out of the disaster was that he held the bell all night in the storm, guiding the survivors down the moraine. There is a plaque. There is a painting in the archive's east hall. He is holding the bell-rope with both hands and his mouth is open, as though he were singing.”

“And the truth?”

“The truth, according to three letters and a shipping manifest that I have spent two years chasing, is that Vasek was unconscious in a tent for the last eleven hours of the storm. Frostbite, and a broken shoulder. He could not have rung anything.” Priya’s voice had gone flat and precise, the way it did when she was close to something she did not want to say. “The man who rang the bell was the expedition's cook. His name was Anders Weiss. He was deaf. He had been deaf from childhood, according to the letters, and the expedition hired him because a deaf cook did not complain about the noise of the wind at night.”

Ilse said nothing. She felt the rope move gently, four people breathing along its length, and she kept her eyes on the ice ahead.

“Weiss rang the bell from midnight until dawn,” Priya went on. “It was a bronze dinner bell, the kind an expedition carries to call men in from the cold. He rang it in a steady pattern, four strikes and a pause, four strikes and a pause, and the sound traveled down the moraine in the storm, and the men who had been scattered by the avalanche followed it back to camp. Six men lived because of that bell. Without it, in that wind, with no visibility, they would have walked off the edge and never known it.”

“And history gave the credit to Vasek,” Ilse said.

“History gave the credit to the man with the plaque.” Priya's mouth was tight. “Weiss was a cook. He was deaf. The men who wrote the accounts afterward did not think to ask him. Or they asked and did not write down the answer. Vasek never corrected it, as far as I can tell. He took the credit and he died famous, and the cook died in a poorhouse in a valley town with no one to write his plaque.” She stopped, and Ilse stopped with her, and the line bunched behind them and then went slack with a question in it.

“Why does an archivist care so much about a deaf cook?” Ilse asked.

Priya looked at her. The wind moved her hair across her face and she pushed it back. “Because the archive has been telling the wrong story for a hundred years and I am the one who found the letters. Because someone should have corrected it years ago.” She turned and walked on, and Ilse let her go, and did not ask the next question, which was the one Priya was clearly afraid of.

Behind them, Hesk had been listening. Ilse felt the rope go still and then tighten as he caught up, and he came alongside her with his mouth already working.

“A cook,” he said. “The whole reason we're dragging fifteen kilos of bronze up a mountain is a cook.”

“A cook who saved six men,” Ilse said.

“A cook who saved six men a hundred years ago. The plaque's wrong, fine. Plaques are wrong all the time. That doesn't make it worth the climb.”

“It's worth the climb because it's true,” Priya said from behind them, and her voice had an edge in it now. “Because the archive is full of plaques that are wrong and nobody has ever had the courage to correct one.”

“Courage,” Hesk said, and laughed. “You're paying for courage. That's a new one.” He looked at Ilse. “And what about the bet? Before the icefall, you said you'd call the crevasse.”

“I called the crevasse yesterday,” Ilse said. “The hollow bridge. You felt the rope go tight. Two tugs, stop. And you stopped.”

“That was the glacier. Anyone could've seen that sag. The bet was for the icefall.” He jabbed a thumb up the slope. “The icefall's the real test. Every leader's got a story about the serac they felt coming. I want to see it.”

Ilse looked at him. The morning sun was full on his face now, and he was not sneering, not quite. He was testing her, the way a young climber tests an old one, and she had been tested by better men and worse ones, and it had never once changed the mountain.

“One week's pay against one week's pay,” she said. “And the loser wears the winner's rope and does not argue the signals for the rest of the climb. Agreed?”

“Agreed,” Hesk said. “And when I win, you tell the archive the leader was scared of the ground.”

“When you win,” Ilse said, and turned away, and led them up toward the gate.

The icefall began where the glacier steepened and broke, a jumble of seracs and crevasses and snow bridges that rose in steps toward the high bowl. It was the only way up, and it was the place the mountain kept its teeth.

They stopped at the edge of it, and Ilse had them check their harnesses twice and their crampons once and their rope ends three times. Then she gave the order: Teodor behind her, then Hesk, then Priya at the back with Dawa, and Nima out front on his own short rope, reading the snow with his probe and his eyes.

“Nobody talks,” Ilse said. “Watch my rope. Watch my hand. If my hand goes flat, you stop. If my fist goes up, you hold. If I tug one long, you anchor where you stand and you do not move until I come back for you.”

They went in.

The first hundred metres were easy. The snow was firm and the seracs stood back from the route and the crevasses were crusted over and obvious. Ilse climbed steadily and felt the rope alive behind her, and the mountain's deep hum came up through her boots as it always did, steady and slow, the glacier grinding down the valley on its own schedule. She counted her breaths and kept her pace even and watched the ice.

Then, at the foot of the first steep step, where the route threaded between two great pillars of blue ice, she stopped.

No sound warned her. The rhythm under her feet changed: the steady grind faltering, a small quick shudder running up through the ice and into her shins. She went still and let the shudder pass, then felt for it again, fingers spread on the cold face of the nearest pillar, and it came. A vibration, faint and high, different from the glacier's slow river. Something above them was moving against something else. Ice against ice. She counted the pulses. Three, four, five, faster now, then a pause, then a deeper grinding, and she did not like the shape of it.

She dropped her hand and tugged the rope once, long and slow, the signal that ended with every one of them driving their axes into the snow and holding.

Behind her, the line stopped. She felt the anchor take, four bodies braced, the rope going rigid between them. Then a question came up the line, one short pull from Hesk, the signal he had not been given.

She gave it no answer. She was listening with her feet.

The grinding above them deepened. She looked up along the route and saw the pillar of ice on the left shiver, a fine dust of snow sliding from its crown. Then the pillar leaned.

Ilse turned and looked back down the line, caught Teodor's eye, and pointed up and to the right, the clear side, and held up her fist. Hold. He gave a nod and braced and passed the fist down the line, and she watched it go from hand to hand until Priya at the back had seen it and braced.

Then the serac let go.

She felt it before she saw it: a great shudder through the ice, a blow that came up through her crampons and into her knees, and then the pillar was falling, slow at first and then all at once, a wall of blue-green ice the size of a house breaking away and hurling itself down the slope. It struck the snow forty metres to their left with a concussive thump that knocked the breath out of her chest, and then the debris came: a hissing cloud of ice fragments and snow that swept across the route and buried the track they had walked ten minutes before.

The rope held. Ilse kept her fist up and her eyes on the fracture line and counted, the old habit, ten seconds, twenty, until the last of the debris settled and the mountain went quiet. Then she let her fist down and tugged the rope three times, steady.

Climb.

They moved up and to the right, over the clean snow on the far side of the pillar, and Ilse led them through a narrow gap and onto a shelf of firmer ice where the route turned toward the high bowl. She called a rest there and they stood in a huddle, all of them breathing hard, the dust of the collapse still hanging in the air behind them.

Priya was white. “That could have killed us,” she said. Her voice was thin. “That could have killed us and we didn't hear anything.”

“You don't hear a serac fall until it's fallen,” Teodor said. “Even with ears. It's the snow that kills you, the dust. It comes faster than the ice.”

Hesk said nothing. He stood at the end of the rope with his axe still in his hand and looked back at the buried track, and his jaw worked once and then stopped.

Ilse watched him a moment and then went to Nima, who had come back down the short rope and was signing before his hands were still.

The rattle above us is not finished. There is more loose ice on the right side of the step.

She signed back: We take the left line and we take it slow.

At the top of the third step, where the route broke out onto the long snow bowl that led toward the cave, she stopped and looked back down the line. Hesk was looking at her. He held her eyes a moment, then gave a single nod, and turned his face forward.

Nima came up beside her and signed, his hands small and quick: The boy has stopped betting.

Ilse signed back: He has not paid yet.

He will, Nima signed, and turned away, and Dawa laughed his easy laugh at something only he had understood, and the line moved on up into the bowl.

That night they camped in a hollow beneath a great overhang of rock, and the cold came down hard, and Ilse sat at the mouth of the tent and watched the stars come up over the icefall. Teodor came and sat beside her. For a while he said nothing. Then, close to her ear: “The serac. You felt it.”

“I felt it moving.”

“Hesk felt it too, when it went. In his feet. He told me so, after.” Teodor paused. “He said he felt the ground go wrong before it fell, and he did not know what it meant until you pulled the rope.”

“Then he is learning,” Ilse said.

“He is quiet.”

“Quiet is a good start.” She pulled her jacket closer. “Tomorrow we reach the shelf. Day three. The cave is under the summit shoulder and the forecast gives us four days, so we have one day in hand and no more. I want to stand at that cave mouth before dark.”

“And Priya?”

Ilse looked at him. “What about Priya?”

“She did not stop talking about the cook. At the rest, while you were reading the snow, she told me the whole story. The man blamed, the man who really rang it.” Teodor's voice was low. “She knows too much about it. She knows the letters, she knows the shipping manifest, she knows the poorhouse. She knows more than an archivist who found some papers. She talks like a woman who has been carrying it.”

Ilse was silent. She had thought the same thing on the glacier, when Priya's voice caught on the word record, and again at the rest when she could not stop speaking of the deaf cook and the plaque. There was something in the way she held the story, close and careful, as though it were a wound she was keeping clean.

“Ask her tomorrow,” Ilse said. “Not tonight. Let her sleep.”

“And the bell?”

“The bell is where it is. We go up and we bring it down and we tell the truth about it.” She stood and brushed the snow from her trousers. “The people on the mountain should tell it straight, whatever the mountain thinks of it.”

Teodor grunted and went to his tent, and Ilse stood a while longer at the mouth of the hollow, her boots on the cold ground, feeling the mountain breathe under her, slow and indifferent and alive. Somewhere above them, in a cave of ice below the summit, a bronze bell hung silent in the dark, and a logbook waited in its oilskin for someone to read it, and the weather window was narrowing, day by day, hour by hour, like an eye closing.

She tugged her glove on and went to her tent and slept, and in the morning they went up.
