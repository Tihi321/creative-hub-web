Tremor

They broke camp in the dark, because the mountain had spoken in the night and would not say it twice.

The tremor had woken Ilse an hour before the alarm. Thin and high in her shoulder blade, gone before she could name it. She lay still and waited for the second. Nothing came, and the nothing was worse. A mountain that repeats a signal is a mountain she can learn. A mountain that speaks once is telling her to move.

Nima and Dawa had gone down the evening before. His head had filled with the loud water altitude put in it, and he could not trust his balance on the icefall, so he would wait for them at the col with the radio and the spare fuel. She had read his signs twice, put her hand on his shoulder, and signed back that he was right. Then she watched them down the first slope until the ridge took them, and made herself turn around.

Four of them went up in the dark.

The route ran up a long snow face and across the shoulder toward the shelf where the cave was said to open. The cold no longer bit. It pressed. The snow was so dry it gave no sound at all, and above six thousand metres the four of them went quiet together without being told.

The face eased into a traverse beneath a hanging slope, and here the snow changed. White and wind-packed, loaded above a soft grey band. She felt it through her soles before she had words: a crust that rang a little under her weight, and beneath it a snow that gave too readily.

She halted the line and read it. She did not like it, and she said so.

"We cross fast and spaced," she told them, "and nobody stops under the crest, not for a bootlace, not for a view. Hesk, you are second. Priya, keep the rope snug and your eyes up."

They went. Halfway across, the ground spoke.

It began as a deep slow rising in her boots, and she had felt it twice before in her life and both times she had run. She drove her axe to the head, dropped her weight, and struck the snow with her palm: three hard beats to mean the ground, the ground, the ground. She signed it at Hesk with a face he could not mistake. He was three metres off the line, climbing toward a jut of rock out from under the crest, and he saw her hands and did not come down.

Teodor heard the beats and understood. His axe went in, his weight went onto the hump, and he hauled the rope tight. Hesk moved because Teodor moved. Priya moved because the rope pulled her. All four were down and anchored when the slope broke.

It opened along a crack a hundred metres wide. The snow behind it lifted as though something under it had drawn breath, and then the whole slab came off and came down, and the sound arrived as pressure on her chest that pushed the breath back into her throat.

She saw white, and felt the rope jerk, and took the pull through her shoulders and her heels. Then she was face down with her cheek on her arm and her axe in both hands, counting. One. Two. Three. The hump held. The river of white poured past below them and fell away into the bowl, and the roar went on, and then it stopped. The silence that followed was so complete she carried it on her back.

She tugged once, and the answer came back up the line: one, two, three. She dug them out and checked them with her hands, because her voice could not reach Teodor's deaf side in that wind. Teodor had a cut on his cheek. Priya was spitting snow and shaking. Thirty metres wide of them, the slab had buried the traverse.

"Everybody up," she said. "Hands and feet and faces. Hesk, Priya's face. Teodor, my back."

They got upright in the settling snow dust. Then Hesk turned on her.

"Reckless," he said. His voice had gone high and raw. "One metre more and it would have taken us. You saw nothing. You called it on a feeling in your feet."

"The crossing began eleven minutes ago," she said, quietly, because quiet was the one thing that could reach him now. "It takes fifty to be clear of the hanging slope. I called it at the end of the soft snow, where I could call it. You were off the rope, under the crest, and you would not come down until Teodor hauled you. That is the recklessness on this hump, and it is not mine."

Teodor got there first. "Listen to me, boy. I have climbed with this woman nineteen years. I have seen her call weather the forecasters missed, and ice that would have killed us all, and I have never once seen her guess. She pinned you to the hump and you asked her twice before you moved. Take your accusation down that rope quietly, or I will tie you off and leave you for the mountain."

Hesk looked at his boots. "I understand," he said, small.

They climbed off the hump and up the far side of the slide, spaced and quiet. The shelf was above the slide and below the final wall, a broad white terrace running back from a great hanging sérac. On its far side the snow lay against a bank of black rock, and in the rock was a dark mouth that was not quite a cave and not quite a shadow.

"That is the cave," Teodor said. "We are where the grid puts it."

They crossed the shelf in the flat hard light. Ilse stopped them a little way from the rock and had Priya come up beside her.

"You have carried that pack through the storm and past the slide. What is in it?"

"Archive materials," Priya said, quickly, in the flat voice of a person who has said the same sentence many times. "Protective cases, handling gloves, a small cutter, the document boards. The archive made me promise to do it correctly."

"A cutter," Ilse said.

"For the boards. If the book is there it must not be handled bare." Priya turned away. "That is the archive rule."

"Then it will have its hour or it will not. But I would know before we go in. Not the cases. The other thing."

Priya stood very still with her hands on the pack. "It is for the page. The first page. I told myself I came here to correct the record. I have begun to think I came here to bury it. That is all I can say on a shelf in the light."

"I will hold you to the rest of it when there is time to hear it," Ilse said, and Priya nodded once and picked up the pack.

They came to the mouth together and looked. The mouth was shut. It went back a little way and then stopped, filled by a plug of ice that had frozen into it and grown in it over a century, blue at the heart and white at the edges. She read the thickness through the ice itself: grey rock behind it, dark going deeper.

She set both palms on the plug and held them there until the others quieted. The cold burned rather than froze, and through the burn came the deep low pulse of the mountain, the grind of the glacier, the weight of the peak. Under the pulse was a small stillness, an emptiness where the cave went back. And in that space something shifted that was not the mountain, a vibration all its own, finer than any the rock made. Like a wire drawn very tight, struck once, left to ring.

She took her hands away. The cold stayed in them a while.

"There is a room behind this," she said. "And there is something in the room that is not ice and not rock. It has been ringing in there a long time."

Teodor put his glove flat on the plug and held it, then took it away, and did not say what he had felt or had not felt. "How much ice?"

"Two metres at the edge. More at the heart, where it has had the longest to grow."

"With the four of us and the small axe," Hesk said, "that is a day, at least. Perhaps two." He looked down the shelf, then at the sky, which had gone the colour of a blade. "And we do not have two."

"We have until the window shuts."

"The window is shutting now." He said it without heat. The argument on the hump had taken something out of him and left something quieter in its place. "I am not saying turn around. I am saying say it aloud, so that when we are cutting in the dark with the wind coming over the shelf, nobody is pretending we still have a choice."

Ilse looked at him for a moment, and thought that she had been wrong about the shape of his courage, which was not the shape of Teodor's and never would be. "Then I will say it aloud. We cut while there is light. We sleep on the shelf with our boots on. If the weather comes in the night, we go down while we still can and the bell stays where it has stayed a hundred and eighteen years. If the weather holds, we cut again at first light."

"An hour of grace," Priya said. She had come up beside them and was looking at the mouth, at nothing, in the manner of a person doing arithmetic she does not like. "An hour is what a ledger gives you when it is being generous."

"Then we are fortunate tonight," Ilse said, "and we will not need to be fortunate twice."

Hesk brought the small axe out of his pack and went to the seam, and Ilse went beside him and set her palm on the rock where the plug met it, and they began.

The ice did not break like the ice of the lower glacier. It came away in hard grey chips that rang on her boots, and each blow that went in told her what was still between her and the room: the thickness, the cold in it, the great slow pressure of the mountain holding it shut. They worked in turns through the last of the light, and the plug opened under their hands by the width of a hand and then a forearm and then no more than a fist, and the dark inside gave back the smell of old iron.

The light went off the far wall. The cold came down with it, and the wind with the cold, and Teodor stood over the stove with his back to the wind and got a flame out of it on the third try.

They made the platform in the lee of the black bank, stamping and cutting a shelf a little wider than the tent, and they ate cold ration and drank water off the stove and did not speak much. Priya sat with the pack against her knees and her hands folded on it, and once she took the flat case of the plates out and turned it in the lamplight and put it away again without opening it. Hesk slept almost at once, in the way of the young, and woke in the small hours and lay watching the sky.

Ilse lay with her ear against the rock for a while, as she had not done since the moraine. The mountain was quiet in the way that worried her, and under the quiet the wire rang on in the dark, faint and patient, as it had rung when she was not there to hear it and would go on ringing when she was gone.

Teodor came and sat by her boots where she could see his mouth.

"Your back," she said.

"Will carry a bell or it will not." He looked at the mouth, at the pale seal of the plug in the dark. "The ice is older than the books say. It has had a long time to make up its mind."

"It has," she said. "So have I."

He nodded and went to the stove and sat with his hands spread over it, and after a while the wind dropped as it drops before the weather turns, and the shelf went very still, and she lay awake and counted the stillness the way she had counted the beats on the hump.

At first light the sky above the north wall was grey and low and moving, and the window was a smaller thing than it had been the night before, and no wider than her hand. She put on her gloves where she sat and took her axe from the pack and went to the seam, and behind her the others came out of the tent one by one and took up their places without being told.
