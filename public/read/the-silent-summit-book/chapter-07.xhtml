The Silent Signal

The night had no hours in it. Wind, slab, bell. That was the whole of the world.

Ilse measured the dark by the strokes. Four and a rest, four and a rest, each set carrying her hand a little deeper into the rock until the sound stopped being sound and became the mountain itself, travelling up her arm. The rock was a bell struck a thousand times and never rung. Through her palm she read where the ridge stood solid and where it rang hollow, where the ice met the stone, where the stone had broken away and hung. She kept her back against the slab and her fist on the ground and let the mountain talk to her through her own knuckles.

Then the rhythm stumbled. She caught the gap before she heard it: the missing beat, the place where a stroke should have landed and did not. Down the ridge a shape moved in the grey. Another behind it. A third. The bar hung silent.

Hesk was up off the slab with the bar in his hand, his head turned to the down-slope. Ilse got her legs under her. They had gone wooden with the cold and she made them obey.

"What is it?" Her shout went nowhere. She caught his shoulder and turned him toward her.

He pointed at the ground. Then he set the bar down, dropped to his knees, pulled off his glove, and laid both hands flat on the snow and the rock beneath it. His face was the face of a man who has felt something good and does not trust it.

Ilse knelt beside him and set her hand where his had been.

A beat came through the rock. Faint. Regular. Not hers. Not four and a rest: one, a pause, one. Iron striking iron, down in the icefall, in the broken grey where nobody had any business to be. The strokes ran too fast and too eager, out of time with hers, in bursts, then nothing, then again from a place a little higher up.

"That is not the wind," she said. "That is not the mountain."

She laid her other hand on the bronze still wedged in its crack and struck it once, hard. The answer came back up through the ice inside a count of ten. The mountain carried it slow, the way cold carries a thing, and the answer was human.

She got up and went down the line, shouting into ears, setting her hands on shoulders, making the signs wide. They are below. They hear. Keep ringing. Then to Hesk alone, her face close to his: "Steady. Slow it down. Do not let them run to us. A man who runs to a sound in a whiteout comes over a cornice. Lead them up slow."

He nodded, and the nod had no argument in it at all, only the readiness of a man who has been given a thing to do and is grateful for it.

The next hour was the hardest of the night, and it had nothing in it to fight.

Ilse kept them working. That was the whole of the work now: keeping four cold people busy enough not to lie down. She put the bar into Priya's hands with the four-beat rhythm and made her take it; put Teodor against Priya's knee where they could keep each other's warmth; set Hesk at the rope end with his eyes up the ridge and his hand on the line. Then she walked the small circle of the packs and the slab and laid her palm on everything that would take it, the case, the bell, the rock, and read what there was to read. The wind was dying, and that was the first thing. Not dead, but the roar had gone out of it and left a hiss and a tugging, and that meant the storm was spending itself. At the east edge of the ridge her hand found the loose ice face had stopped sliding and firmed up again, the way a face does when the wind that worked it rests.

She made herself walk the circle a second time. On the third turn a beat came through the rock that was not Priya's, and then another, nearer, and she let the counting go and counted only answers. The strokes came up the ridge one step at a time now, careful, a man who trusted his footing because he could feel it and could not see it. The ringing stopped. The feet came on.

Then a shape walked out of the white into the small clear air around the slab, and it was Nima.

His face was thin and dark and haggard, and the frost on his eyebrows had made him old. He came up the last step with his head down and his shoulders in, stood still and looked at the four of them and at the bell, and made the sign Ilse had taught him for I have been listening and at last I am here. Ilse signed back You came up.

"I came up," he said, and the words came out in a shout without a question in them, as if he had counted on nothing better. "Dawa is below at the terrace with the line. Two more behind me from the rescue party with the doctor. Slow." He looked at Teodor and his ankle and made a face that was not a smile but was near one. "The bell. I felt it in the ice. My head was bad at the col, and I lay down, and the ground went small and tight, and I said to Dawa, the mountain is ringing like struck iron. So I got up and I came."

"Your head," Ilse said.

"Better," Nima said. "The ringing is better than the lying down. Come. We go before the wind comes round again."

The descent opened the way descents do, which is by not being one thing but a hundred small ones.

With Nima and the two rescue men on the rope the mountain stopped being a problem of reading and became a problem of work, and Ilse let it become work. They cut the cradle up out of the rock and set the bell back into it and put four men to it. Two more came up with ropes and a litter of spars and a doctor who took one look at Teodor's ankle and said something to Nima that Ilse did not catch and did not need to, because Teodor sat down at once and let himself be laced and strapped and did not argue about it. That was the moment Ilse knew the night was over. Teodor arguing was the mountain. Teodor sitting still was the road home.

They went down the ridge in the dark, and the ridge was worse than they had left it. New snow had filled the hollows and smoothed the steps and made a soft lie of the ground, and the ice beneath was black and hard and did not give under a boot. Nima went first with a probe and a long step and a short one, tapping ahead like a blind man in his own house. Ilse went second with her hand on the line and her feet reading the ice through the soles of her boots, and the two of them brought the party down the way they had brought them up: by feeling.

The whiteout thinned as they came down. It did not lift. It went soft and grey, and the far wall walked out of it and stood there with its black rock and its hanging snow, and then the icefall came out of it, broken and tumbled, with no track left in it at all. The track they had cut coming up was gone. Ilse stood at the edge and felt the icefall through the ground and it was a new thing, a heap of fallen blocks with a hiss of wind through it and water moving somewhere underneath.

"Not the old line," Nima said. "The new one. East, under the wall."

"The east face is sliding."

"The sliding is done," Nima said. "I felt it coming down. It went quiet."

Ilse felt it again. Quiet, and the quiet had the set of a thing that has finished moving and is not going to begin again tonight. "East," she said. "But the bell comes last. If that face goes, it goes without us on it."

They went down through the icefall in the dark with the rescue men at the points and Nima finding the way, and it took them two hours to make the terrace they had crossed coming up in a quarter of an hour. Every step of it was work. The bell came down in the cradle with four men at the ropes, and Priya ran the litter line in the middle with her hands clenched on the rope, and the case was strapped on Ilse's own back, because she was not going to hand it to anyone on a face where a man could go over.

At the second terrace the wind stopped altogether. The silence that came into the icefall then was the first true quiet of the whole night, and it had weight to it, and Ilse stood in it and let herself breathe.

Priya sat down on a fallen block of ice with the case in her lap and did not get up.

Ilse saw her from the rope, came down the step, and put her hand under her arm. "Up. We are not stopping on the terrace."

"No," Priya said. "I mean, yes. But." She was crying, and the crying had come on her all at once and had the shape of a thing held under for a long while and come up. Her face was grey with the cold and the tears were already freezing as they came. "I have to tell you something before we get down. Before base camp. Before any of them."

"You told me on the mountain," Ilse said.

"Not the whole of it." Priya looked at her hands on the case. "The page. When we opened the case, and I read it, and I had the page and I put it to the flame. Hesk stopped me. I want you to know that I meant it. I was not shaking my hands over a fire. I had planned it, Ilse. Before we left the city. I brought a cutter and boards and I had planned to take the book and to burn what did not suit me and to bring the rest home and call it the truth. I financed the whole of it so I could come up here and make my great-great-grandfather the hero of it and put Karel Vasek in the wrong. That is why I was always at the back of the rope shaking at every tug. I was not frightened of the mountain. I was frightened of what I was going to do when we got to the cave."

She stopped and looked up.

"And I would have done it," she said. "If it had not been for the boy with his glove. Say it. Say what I am. Tell me what the record has to call it."

Ilse crouched down on the ice so that her face was level with Priya's. Her feet on the terrace told her nothing was moving. Her hand on the case told her it was steady and cold. She thought of another mountain and a partner who had not come home, and of a sentry line she had set too late, and she chose her words as carefully as she had ever chosen a step.

"The record has to call it what you just did," she said. "You tried to burn a page and then you read it out loud to four people. You meant to bury the truth and then you carried the truth down a mountain in a whiteout with a bad hand and your fingers on it the whole way. Both of those are in the record. They go in the same record. That is what the record is for."

"It is a shameful record."

"It is a true one," Ilse said. "A shameful record is one that is written to cover a man. There is a man in your family who rang a bell all night in an ice cave and could not hear it answered, and there is a man in your family who wanted to take his page out of the book and did not. The archive holds the whole of it now. I will see that it is published entire, and I will write the preface, and I will not be kind. Do you understand me? I will not be kind and it will be told."

Priya sat with her head down and the case in her hands and cried in the dark on a block of ice, and the icefall was quiet round about her.

Hesk came down the step and stood over them with the bar in his fist. He had heard none of it and guessed most of it. He put his bare hand down on the block beside her and felt the ice still, and then he said, in the flat voice he had used at the cave mouth: "The case goes with her and nobody touches it. That is my part. The story is hers and the story is not mine. Get up, Priya. We go down and you will have years afterwards to be ashamed in. Do not do it on a terrace."

Priya got up. She wiped her face with her mitten and made a small sniffling business of it and then stood straight. "Hand me the rope," she said.

They came into the col before the dawn had any colour in it, and they came into it with the bell and the case and every one of them walking.

Dawa was there, and had kept a stove alight and a tent standing. When Teodor was laced and lying with the bad ankle packed in snow there was a moment when the four of them looked at one another in the lamplight and no one had anything to say that could be said in a tent. The rescue men went out again and came back with the doctor and the sponsor's man and questions. Ilse gave the questions to Nima and took the answers as they came and made no plans. She sat in the door of the tent with her back to the canvas and her hand flat on the snow that had been a mountain and was now a floor, and felt the first light come up over the icefall and touch the frozen lake of the glacier, and did not move.

Hesk sat down beside her with a tin cup and put it into her hands and put his own hand down flat on the snow beside hers, and was silent a long while.

"The bet is unpaid," she said at last.

"Do not start," Hesk said. "I have been thinking about it all night. A week's pay. It is nothing, and it is mine to pay, and I am going to pay it, and you are going to take it and say nothing clever. I am going to put it into your school, the deaf one you are always on about, and I am going to go. I am going to be the first one through the door. You will teach me the one thing I still cannot do and I will be no trouble." He scowled out at the light. "I will be some trouble."

Ilse looked at him.

"The school does not exist yet," she said.

"Then start it," Hesk said, and drank his tea.

Below them, down the long glacier road they had walked up in another life, the base camp flags hung straight in the dawn and the ice ran pale and flat to the little tents and the rescue men moving between them, and somewhere down there was a radio and a forecast desk and a man who had said four days when the truth had been two. The bell would go down on the litter that day and go into the valley and from the valley into the city and into the archive, and the record would be told entire, and Priya would be the one to tell it.

Anders Weiss had rung a bell all night to ask the mountain where it was going. Ilse had asked the same question with the floor of the world under her hands, and the mountain had answered, the way it always answered, not with a voice but with the ground. The ground had said: here, this way, come down.

She flattened her palm on the snow, and through the snow and the ice she felt the last faint regular beat, far off, a thing carried up from below.

The rescue team began their work.

She got up to help.
