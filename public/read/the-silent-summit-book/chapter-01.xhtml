The Unnamed Peak

The jeep died a kilometre short of the moraine, and in the sudden quiet Ilse Marchetti felt the mountain before she saw it.

It came up through the soles of her boots and the packed dirt of the road: a low steady hum that belonged to neither the engine nor the wind. Rock and ice under pressure, three thousand metres of it stacked against the sky, breathing slow vibrations into the ground. She stepped out and set her fingers to the hood until the metal stopped shivering. Then she turned and looked up.

The peak had no name on any map she trusted. It rose out of the Ilvar Range in a long grey sweep, its summit buried in a lenticular cloud that meant wind, and above the wind, worse. She had seen the photograph a hundred times. The photograph told her nothing. In it the mountain had looked indifferent rather than cruel, and indifference is harder to argue with.

Teodor Brann climbed out the other side, knees cracking, and made no effort to hide the noise. Fifty-three years old and built like a door, grey stubble, eyes creased from decades of squinting at far ridges. He had climbed with her for nineteen years and had never once learned to stop talking to her back.

“Road's done,” he said. Then he remembered, and stepped around the jeep so she could see his mouth. “Road's done. Half a klick to camp. They've marked the path with stones.”

She nodded. “I felt it go soft.”

“The road?”

“The mountain. It's been moving all morning. Small things. It settles in the afternoon heat.” She lifted one shoulder, which in her language meant: as expected, and also: be ready.

Teodor grunted and hauled the rope bag off the roof. He wore the look he always wore on the last day of the approach, half relief and half dread, the way a man looks at a door he knows will close behind him.

Priya Rask was already out, dragging her rucksack by one strap as though it had offended her. Thirties, city-pale, dressed in gear so new it still creased at the elbows. The archivist. The money. She had flown them here, paid the permits and the porters and two months of Ilse's time, and in four days of travel she had not once explained why an archivist would spend a fortune to reach a mountain that killed eleven people in 1908. She stood a little apart with one hand pressed over her jacket pocket, and she kept her eyes off the mountain while the rest of them looked their fill.

“Is that it?” Priya said at last. Her voice was careful, academic, each word placed like a slide under glass.

“That's it,” Teodor said. “Ilvar's last unclimbed face. Nobody's stood on that summit since the disaster, and the ones who tried got the hint.”

“People have tried?”

“Six expeditions,” Ilse said. She kept the list in her head, not on paper. “Four turned back at the icefall. Two lost porters to altitude before the ridge. The mountain doesn't want visitors in the last month of the season. The window's narrow, four days of clear weather maybe five, and that's why we carry everything we'll need and trust none of it to luck. Nobody will be up there to help us. We go self-contained, or we don't go.” She looked at Priya. “Or when we don't.”

Priya's mouth did something small and tight. “How narrow is the window?”

“The forecasters won't commit. The monsoon is gone, the jet stream is coming, and between them there's a gap like a closing eye. If we're not off the summit by the sixth night, we sit in it and wait, and waiting at seven thousand is how people die.” Ilse glanced at the cloud lying flat over the summit. “The cloud's wrong today. It should be blowing east. It's leaning south. It'll clear by evening.”

“How do you know that?” Priya said.

“The air pressure changed an hour ago. I felt it in my ears on the road. The mountain is leaning the other way now. It does that before a clear night.”

Priya frowned at her, as though deciding whether this was a joke, and then wrote nothing down. Ilse noticed. An archivist who does not write things down is an archivist hiding something, or one afraid of what a written note would say.

The last man out of the jeep was the one she had been waiting to hear from. Hesk Vandry swung his legs over the tailgate and landed like he wanted the ground to notice. Twenty-six, maybe twenty-seven. Wide shoulders, a rope coiled with showroom precision, a chin that nothing in the mountains had broken yet. A guide in Chamonix who owed Ilse a favour had recommended him, which meant Hesk was owed a favour by someone, and that made him twice as proud and half as careful.

He looked at the mountain, then at her. He faced her square when he spoke, which she appreciated, and he did not slow his words, which she did not.

“You're Marchetti,” he said.

“I am.”

“I read about your Karsten Wall. The rescue. Two years ago.” He shouldered his pack and settled it with a shrug, testing the weight. “I heard the radio calls. I heard you need a translator to lead a team.”

Teodor stepped between them, casual, but his shoulder was square. “She's standing right here, Hesk. You can say it to her.”

“I am saying it to her.”

Ilse held up one hand and Teodor stopped, the way he always did, though his jaw stayed tight. She studied Hesk the way she studied ice: for the places where the surface lied about what lay underneath. The easy stance, the chin up, the eyes that had already decided the answer and were only waiting for the question. She had seen it before, in men half his age and twice his experience. Fear wearing a good jacket.

“You want to know how a deaf woman leads a mountain,” she said.

“I want to know how you hear a falling serac.”

“I don't hear it. I feel it before it falls.” She turned and walked for the path. “Keep up. The porters are already at camp.”

The path to base camp was a scatter of flat stones set into the moraine by someone patient, and the moraine was a long brown wound of loose rock and boulder clay. Ilse walked at the front, and the rhythm of it came up into her: the tick of her heel on stone, the slide of gravel, the deep patient thrum of the glacier somewhere below, a river of ice grinding down the valley on a scale too slow to see and too vast to ignore. Behind her the others fell into a loose line, and she knew each of them by how their steps landed. Teodor, heavy and even. Priya, light and halting, stopping to look at things and then hurrying to catch up. Hesk, quick and too hard, driving his heels down as though the ground needed convincing.

She wore no hearing aids. She had worn them as a girl and given them up at nineteen, tired of the world arriving as a smear of noise she had to translate. Silence had a shape. It was the noise of everything solid moving, and with her feet and her palms and her skin she read it better than she had ever read a sound.

Halfway up she stopped and crouched, two fingers to a slab of granite.

Behind her, the crunch of boots stopped.

“What?” Teodor said, mouth close to her ear so she could see the shape of the word.

She held up one finger. Wait.

Through her fingertips: a faint tremor, steady, not the wind. She counted it. Many feet, all walking, and beneath the footsteps something heavier, a low deep grinding, slow, like a door dragged across a floor. She stood and walked on. The porters. The yaks. Nothing wrong. She filed it away, made a small sign to Teodor, hand tilted flat, meaning all quiet, and he nodded and told the others to move.

“Everything all right?” Priya called from behind, her voice pitched a little too high.

“Everything's fine,” Teodor said. “She's just listening.”

Base camp sat on a shelf of rock above the glacier's edge, a scatter of orange tents and one larger mess tent, prayer flags strung between poles snapping in a wind she could not hear but felt as a flutter against the canvas she passed. The air smelled of kerosene, wet wool, stone dust. Two men waited by the mess tent. The older one, Nima, was small and weathered, with a face like a map folded many times, and he came forward and took her hand in both of his. The younger, Dawa, was broader, quick-eyed, grinning before anyone had said anything worth grinning at.

“You walked fast,” Dawa said, mouth working. “Nima said you would walk fast. He said the leader walks fast, the team follows, nobody complains.”

“Except me,” Teodor said, arriving behind her and dropping his pack with a thud that came up through the ground. “I complain.”

Nima signed something slow and clear, hands close to his chest, and she read it: The mountain is quiet today. Too quiet. It is waiting.

She signed back: It can wait.

Dawa watched the exchange and laughed. “He likes you already. He does not like many people. He says you have good feet.” He pointed at her boots, then at his. “Good feet are better than good ears. Ears lie. Feet do not.”

“I'll tell my old climbing partner that,” Teodor said, and Dawa laughed again, and Ilse felt the tension come down a notch, which she was grateful for. A team that laughs together on the first night will still be a team in a storm.

Inside the mess tent they spread the map on a folding table and weighted the corners with mugs. The mountain's face filled it with contour lines, brown and grey, and the route she had drawn in red ink went up the western flank, through the glacier, over an icefall, across a long snow bowl, and then the summit ridge itself, thin as a hair on the page. A lantern hung from the ridge pole and swung a little, and the light moved across the map like water.

“Here,” she said, and touched the red line at its top. “The summit, day six if the weather holds. Here,” she moved her finger down, “is the icefall. It is the only place the mountain has a gate. We go through it in the dark, before the sun loosens the seracs. Nobody talks in the icefall. You watch my rope, you watch my hand.”

“And the bell?” Priya said. She had sat down across the table and folded her hands, but her eyes were not on the map.

“The bell is below the summit, in a cave of ice the old accounts describe. A bronze bell, about so big.” Ilse cupped her hands. “Fifteen kilos. It rang all night in 1908 when the expedition's camp was destroyed and the survivors climbed down blind in a storm. It rang until morning and the men who heard it lived. The men who did not, did not.”

“Who rang it?” Teodor asked, though he had heard the story from her a dozen times in a dozen teahouses.

“History says a guide named Karel Vasek. That is the account in every book. Priya has the archive citations, and she has the permits for the recovery, and I do not ask why an archive pays for ice and porters unless it loves a story.” Ilse looked at Priya. “The bell is worth nothing but its story. We bring it down so the story can be told in the right building.”

Priya's smile was thin. “That's the hope.” She cleared her throat. “The archive has been chasing that bell for forty years. Every account agrees on the cave, and the ice has kept it. Bronze doesn't rust. The logbook in the case beside it is wrapped in oilskin, or it was when the story was first set down. The man who rang it wrote everything down that night, every hour, every name. It will settle the record.” Her voice caught, very slightly, on the word record, and she went on quickly. “I'll want the cave photographed before anything is moved.”

“You'll get your photographs,” Ilse said. “And then we move fast, because the window does not wait for history.”

Hesk broke it open. He had been standing at the tent flap with his arms crossed, and now he came to the table and put one finger on the map, on the icefall. The lantern light crossed his face and he held her eyes. “Let me say the thing nobody's saying. Six expeditions failed. Two people died of altitude. And now we're going up with a leader who can't hear a radio?”

The tent went quiet. The wind fluttered the canvas and Ilse felt it in the soles of her feet, a small irregular tap.

“I can read a radio,” she said. “I have a light. Red, amber, green. Teodor calls the weather from base camp and I read the light. On the mountain, signals come by rope. Two tugs, stop. Three tugs, climb. One long pull, danger, anchor now. We agree them before we leave this tent, all of us, and if I tug, you do not ask why. You do it.”

“And if you can't feel the rope because your hands are cold?” Hesk said.

“My hands are never that cold. I keep them warm and I keep the rope short.” She straightened. “You have more questions. Ask them now, not at six thousand metres.”

“And the belay?” he pressed. “A climber falls, you can't hear the shout. You can't hear the rope run.”

“I feel the rope run. A falling body pulls the anchor in a way that is different from a body climbing. I have felt it a thousand times. I have caught men who did not know I was catching them.” She let that sit. “You want a leader who hears. I want a climber who trusts. We both have work to do.”

Hesk looked at her, and then he did something she had expected and had been waiting for since the jeep: he laughed, once, short, and shook his head.

“A week's pay,” he said.

“What?”

“That's my question. I'll bet you a week's pay. Before we leave the icefall, you'll call a crevasse that isn't there. You'll feel something, and you'll stop the team for nothing. Because you feel with your feet, and the ground lies.” He spread his hands. “If I'm wrong, I pay you. If I'm right, you pay me, and you tell the archive the leader was scared of the ground.”

Teodor started to speak. Ilse stopped him with a look.

“One week's pay,” she said. “Agreed. And a second stake. If I call a real one before you hear it, you wear my rope and you do not argue the signals for the rest of the climb.”

“Deal.”

They did not shake. That was not how she did things. She turned back to the map and felt the tent settle, the team's tension easing out of the ground and into the long hard work of getting ready. Nima and Dawa had said nothing through any of it, but Nima watched Hesk the way a hawk watches weather, and Dawa quietly moved the mugs back to the corners of the map.

That evening she found him at the edge of camp, alone, watching the summit cloud go from grey to gold as the sun dropped.

She came up beside him and waited until he turned.

“You want to see the trick,” she said.

“I want to see if it's a trick.”

She crouched and put one palm down on a slab of rock, and after a moment he crouched too, mocking it, and set his hand beside hers. Then he stopped mocking, because the evening was cold and clear and the mountain was alive under his hand, and he felt it.

“What is that?” he said.

“The icefall is moving. Not fast. The glacier is a river that never stops. When the seracs shift, it goes through the rock. You hear it with your ears when it is close. I hear it with my feet when it is far.” She took his wrist and pressed his fingers to a second stone, a metre away. “Now. Feel.”

He stayed quiet. Then: “It's different here.”

“Stronger. That stone sits on a thin crust over a hollow. It carries the vibration farther.” She stood and brushed her hands. “In the morning we check the ropes and the avalanche beacons and the tugs, all of it, together. You do not climb with me until you know the signals. And Hesk.”

“What.”

“The bet was a good way to ask your question. Next time, just ask.”

She left him crouched there in the cold, hand still flat on the stone.

They left camp at four, in the dark, roped in a line with Nima breaking trail and Ilse second, reading the ground through her boots. The cold had teeth that morning. It came up through the soles and into the shins, and the snow under the crust was hard and dry, squeaking faintly under each step. Above them the stars were thick as frost, and the summit cloud had blown away east, exactly as she had said it would.

The glacier began where the moraine ended, a white plain that looked smooth and was not. Three hundred metres out, Ilse stopped and lifted her fist, and the whole line stopped with her.

Teodor came up alongside. “What?”

She pointed to the snow ahead, where it sagged in a long shallow curve. Then she crouched, pulled off her right glove, and laid her bare palm on the surface. The vibration came up through her hand, not the deep steady grind of the glacier below but something wrong, a hollow resonance, thin and drumlike, as though the snow were skin stretched over nothing.

“The hollow one,” she said. “Under there. Three metres.”

Hesk, three places back on the rope, stayed silent, but she felt his tug, one short pull, the signal for what, not the signal they had agreed, which was to wait. She ignored the correction and turned. “Nima. Probe.”

Nima drove his probe through the snow crust. It went in, in, in, and then dropped, the pole falling into nothing, the length of it swallowed, and he pulled it back with the tip wet with black water.

Priya made a small sound.

“Crevasse,” Nima said. “It is open under the bridge. We cross here.” He pointed to a thicker rib of snow a dozen metres to the east. “There. Solid.”

They crossed one by one, spaced, with Ilse on the anchored rope, and then Teodor brought the team together on the safe side while she stood a moment over the place where the hollow had been, feeling its thin drumbeat through her boots even now.

Hesk hadn't said anything. She kept her eyes forward, but when the line moved again she felt the rope go taut in his direction, and then slack, and then taut again. Two tugs. Stop. No. She counted. Two, three. Climb. They climbed.

At the top of the first rise, where the snow was firm and the whole Ilvar Range opened out around them in the blue light before sunrise, Teodor fell in beside her.

“The boy's quiet,” he said, mouth close to her ear. “Quiet's a good start.”

“He paid nobody anything yet,” she said. “He still thinks I'm lucky.”

“And if you're not?”

She looked ahead, at the icefall, at the high grey face above it waiting in the dark. Somewhere up there, in ice older than any story, a bronze bell hung silent in a cave. Somewhere up there was a weather window that would open and close and not care who was standing in it. Behind her, she felt the line settle into its new rhythm, four pairs of boots finding the same pace, the rope alive between them.

“Then we'll all find out together,” she said.

And she tugged the rope three times and they went on up into the cold.
