The Capture

The mail boat went back at ten past six in the morning with the tide under it and the deck wet, and Isla sat on the bench with her kit bag between her feet and the drive in her inside pocket and watched the two outer islands come up out of the grey one after the other, the way they had come up every crossing for three months, and this time she counted them like a woman counting something she might have to give back.

She was on the shelf by half past nine.

Tob was in the boat shed with the door open and the light coming in low across the bench, and he had a length of rope on the bench and he was working an eye splice into the end of it with a fid and his hands, and he did not look up when she came in, and she stood in the doorway and let the wind come past her and did not say anything until he had finished the tuck and pulled it tight and set it down.

"You went to the mainland," he said.

"I went to the mainland."

"You took the jars."

"I took the jars." She came in and put the kit bag down and sat on the crate, which was still the crate from the first morning, and it still had the same two dents in the lid where his heels had rested on it for twenty-nine years of mornings. "The analyst logged them and sealed them and drove them north, and the chain of custody is clean, and I have the receipt and the form and the times."

"And Voss."

She looked at him.

"The man you survey for," she said. "The man who signs your permit and signs my permit and answered my anomaly report in nineteen minutes. He designed the basin. He built the arrays on the south wall and the canyon lip in ninety-six, and he has kept them true for twenty-nine years at a window of four and a half hours on the flood and the ebb, and the reason the water in the south basin goes soft every night is a man on the mainland with a control philosophy and a car door of cowling set in the rock."

Tob put both hands flat on the bench.

He did not say anything for a long moment, and his hands were steady, and outside the wind moved the galvanised sheet on the roof with the two-note sound it made in any weather.

"I knew it was somebody," he said. "I did not know it was somebody with a name. I thought for a long time it was a fisherman's story, what I saw. I thought I had seen the wall in the wrong light. Then I thought if it was real, then a man in an office somewhere had written it down, and the man in the office was the end of it, and the end of the office was the sea and the sea does what it likes."

"The sea does what it likes," Isla said. "That's the whole of it. That's the whole thing I've got."

She told him the rest of it the way she had written it in the log, in order, with the numbers in the order, because that was the only way she knew how to tell a thing and have it stand: Sable, the operator; ageliferin, the drug; the contract buyers; the 2001 paper and the settlement tiles and the pump pulled for a transect; the survey of ninety-four and not seventy-four; the chart lines drawn before Voss ever saw the sheet. She told him about the mooring plan, and the four anchors, and the floating spine at eleven metres, and the tethers with a margin on each of them about the width of a thumb.

"Eighty tonnes each," she said. "Margin of about twelve. At the design flow. Which is two point four."

Tob's hands came up off the bench and he turned around and looked at her, and his face had gone the way it went when he could not finish a sentence, thin and careful, like a man walking on ice he had already heard crack.

"What's the flow on the spring flood," he said.

"Three."

"And the load."

"About ninety on one anchor."

He said, "That's the whole of it," very quietly, and then he went out of the shed and stood on the concrete path with his back to her and looked south down the gravel and dead coral to where the path turned to mud, and the wind came off the water and moved the grass on the bank and he stood there for the length of a minute with his hands at his sides.

She gave him the minute. She had learned that much.

Then he came back in and said, "What is it you want."

"Two things," she said. "The first is the geometry, and I have it, and I am going to read it against the tide table tonight. The second is a face."

"A face."

"Sable's face. Or his crew's. Or the inside of that base, if it can be got at without bringing the roof down on whoever is standing under it. Because I have sponge fragments and film of a skid and a beacon pulse and a man on the mainland who will sign a statement, and none of it puts a hand on Warden Sable. A chain of custody and a confession from the engineer is a case about a paper. I need a wall with a dock in it and a rack of tanks on the dock and the man running it standing beside the rack."

Tob went to the wall where the tools hung by size and stood looking at them.

"I have been out of the water twenty-nine years," he said.

"I know it."

"I cleaned that mask every year and I put it in the shed and I never put it on."

"I know that too," Isla said. "And I am not going to stand here and tell you it will be like it was, because that would be a lie, and you would hear it, and it would be the last useful thing I said to you for the rest of this. So I will tell you the true thing instead. The true thing is that I can read the water and I cannot swim a gat. The true thing is that the base sits in a hole with a vertical wall on the west side and a bar at the mouth, and there is only one way in that does not go through the south wall and the door, and the way in is a slot above the bar that fills on the flood and empties on the ebb, and I have the chart gap and the soundings and Voss's drawings, and what I do not have is a man who has put his hand on that bar."

Tob was quiet a while.

"Twenty-nine years," he said. "It was running when I saw it and it is running now and it will be running when I am dead, and the reef will be a hole in the rock with a pump in it, and the coral will come back nowhere because the larvae go into the box." He turned around. "I'll want the shape of the slot on paper. And I'll want the tide, the hour and the minute of it, not the day."

"You'll have it tonight."

"And I'm not going in the door," he said. "I'm not going through that seam. If I go through that seam you will be reading about me in the paper."

"Then we don't go through the seam," she said.

She spent the afternoon on the reef edge at the frame Tob had built, and she read the geometry against the table and the table against the chart, and by the time the light went she had the thing she wanted, which was not a plan but a shape.

The spring tide on the twenty-second was nine days off. On that day the south channel would run at about three metres per second on the flood, and the ebb behind it would be longer and only a little weaker, and the window in which a body could move inside the gat without being carried onto the bar and held there was, by her own arithmetic, between eleven and thirteen minutes each side of slack.

Eleven minutes. On the flood, going in.

She drew it on the back of the log, on the clean page, and it was not a picture of a base, it was a picture of a throat: the bar at the mouth, the slot above it, the vertical wall on the west, the south wall with the seam and the door and the mooring eyes, the grate, the spine, the four anchors. She marked the slot with the depth at half-flood and the depth at slack, and she marked the hours, and she folded the page and put it in her pocket.

Tob came up at dusk and she showed it to him and he looked at it for a long time with his finger on the slot.

"This is a boat place," he said. "This slot. You could put a dinghy through this at slack and sit in it, and the wall would hide you from the door."

"Yes."

"And on the flood the water would come up through the slot and push you up against the wall and hold you there, and you would come out on the ebb if you timed it, and if you mistimed it the bar takes you."

"Yes."

He took his finger off the page.

"Isla," he said. "What exactly is it you want me to bring back."

She had thought about this on the crossing and had not made it soft, and she did not make it soft now.

"A plate number," she said. "A tank that has a number on it and the number is the same as the number on the skid you saw in the frames. A piece of paperwork if there is paperwork, and if there is no paperwork then a photograph of the tank and the rack and the light above them and the paint on the mooring eye that matches the paint on your hand. Anything that ties the yard that paints its gear grey-green to the dock inside the canyon. Anything with a date on it. And if there is a name on anything, the name."

"A photograph is not a wall with a dock in it."

"A photograph of a wall with a dock in it is a photograph of a wall with a dock in it," she said. "And I will take the photograph and the date and the number and I will put them in a sealed bag with the times on them, the same way I did with the jars, and I will hand them to the same analyst with the same form, and then it is not two people saying a thing about a place. It is a document."

Tob looked at the water, which was going the colour it went at dusk, chalk and oil and then nothing.

"You want the tank number because the tank number goes somewhere," he said.

"Everything that is loaded on that skid goes somewhere," Isla said. "A rack that leaves the dock with twenty-four tanks on it goes to a boat, and a boat goes to a buyer, and a buyer has a name, and a name is a man who can be found. I want the name."

"And if there's no name."

"Then I want a number that stands in front of one," she said. "Which is the same thing with an extra step."

He nodded slowly. Then he said, "The mask is in the shed," and went down the path in the dark, and she stayed with the log and the folded page and the last of the light, and watched the blue-green beacon come on out in the basin, two and then one, two and then one, patient as a clock, and thought about the fact that she had just asked a man to swim into a hole on a spring flood so that she could have a number.

The nine days were the longest of the four months, and she spent them the way she had spent the first three, which was in numbers.

The dampening window held. She logged it every night: the pulse at nine, the basin going flat, the vibration in the rock, the hum of the diesel below the pump, and out at the south wall the red lamp that had come the first night of the watch and had not come again, so that the submersible came and went without a light and was known only by the hydrophone track that dropped into the canyon and vanished at a hundred and eighty metres. Four nights of the nine she saw the track; on the other five the water was too loud and the vessel either did not run or ran on the ebb and was lost in it.

Twice in the nine days she walked to the village and did not go to the shed. She had a reason both times and the reason was true and the reason was also that she wanted to look at the house with the corrugated roof and the concrete path and the clean mask and see whether the light was on, and both times the light was off, and both times she walked back out along the gravel in the dark and told herself she was checking the tide.

On the seventh day Voss emailed her.

Is the gear still intact.

That was all, and she did not answer it, because it had no question in it that she could answer without writing down a thing she did not want written down. She wrote the time of the email in the log instead and let the silence stand.

On the eighth day she went out to the flat at low water with the tide table in the plastic sleeve and sat on the rock and read the numbers off the page and put them against the soundings and the drawings and came out, four separate times, at the same eleven minutes on the flood.

On the ninth morning the wind came up from the north-west and by noon it was against the ebb and the whole shelf was standing on end with spray, and the bar at the mouth of the gat would be shifting sand in a wind like that. With a spring tide and the wind onshore, the surge would push the race past its hour and the water would run where it had no business, and she stood at the frame and looked at the white water on the southern flat and made the arithmetic a fifth time and got eleven minutes, and the fifth time she did not believe it.

She went down to the shed.

"Not tonight," she said. "The wind. If the wind takes the tide the flood comes early and the slot fills before we are there and the ebb behind it is more than we can take."

Tob was on the bench with the mask in his hands and a cloth. He had been at it since the seventh day, and the lens had been done so many times that the cloth only moved to keep his hands busy, and the strap was new, and she had not asked him about the strap and did not ask him about it now.

"Tomorrow," he said.

"Tomorrow the wind drops. The table says eleven forty-one for slack at the bar and the flood behind it, and the window is open at twenty past eleven."

He set the mask down on the bench, and he got up, and he went to the wall and took a torch off its peg and tested it and put it back, and took a second torch and tested it and put it in the bag he had ready on the floor: mask, fins, a short knife, a spool of line, the torch, and a flat waterproof case the size of a paperback with a camera in it that Isla had given him and shown him twice, and he had made her show him a third time.

"You're not going in," he said.

"I'm not going in."

"You'll be on the rock at the frame with the hydrophone."

"I'll be on the rock at the frame with the hydrophone," she said, "and the spool is on your wrist and the line runs back out of the slot and I have the other end, and if the line goes slack I pull it in, and if the line goes tight twice I leave it alone because that means you are coming and I should not be pulling you onto the bar." She had written this on the page in the pocket, and she had made him read it, and he had read it.

Tob looked at the bag on the floor.

"Twenty-nine years," he said, "and I am going to know what is behind the wall."

"Yes," she said.

"That is not why I am going."

"I know why you are going," she said. "You are going because you were there and you saw the grate and you did not tell anyone, and you have cleaned that mask every year since, and you would like the last dive of your life to be the one where you finally went and got the number."

He looked at her for a moment.

"The last dive," he said.

"Tonight's the last night of the wind," Isla said. "Tomorrow's the dive."

The wind dropped in the night the way the table said it would, and the morning came flat and bright and cold, with the water in the south basin like a plate and the bar at the mouth of the gat sitting under two metres of clear green and a long white line of foam along the west wall where the current ran even on the neap.

Slack at the bar at eleven forty-one.

They were on the flat at ten past eleven with the frame between them and the hydrophone in the water and the spool of line peeled off and running down the slope into the slot, and Tob was in his suit with the mask on his forehead and the bag clipped to his belt, and the only thing he had said for the last hour was a single sentence, which was "The light goes on the wall and the line goes on the wrist and you do not come in."

At twenty-eight minutes to twelve she watched him go down the slope.

He went the way men went who had been good once: straight, without hurry, fins barely moving, one hand out to the rock, and the slack water over the bar took him and turned him and he let it, and went through the slot on the push of the flood the way she had read it, and the line ran off her hand in slow coils and the green water closed over the last of the fins and he was gone.

The line moved. It moved the way a line moves when a man is working, cautious, out and back, and Isla sat on the rock with her hand around the spool and the hydrophone lead in her other hand and listened to the shelf: the tide running in the outer channel, the small clicks of the reef, a fish going over the flat somewhere behind her, and under all of it, from far off down in the canyon, the low beat of the diesel.

For four minutes the line did what a line should do.

At the fifth minute it stopped.

She counted to sixty. She counted to sixty again. The line lay still in her hand, neither tight nor slack, the way line lay on the bottom when nobody was on the other end of it and nothing was picking it up, and the hydrophone, which had been a long soft noise of the current in the slot, changed the way a room changes when a door behind you opens.

She sat with it for eleven seconds, and she did not pull, because pulling was the one thing she had promised not to do.

Then the line came tight.

Not twice. Not the two tugs that meant coming. One long steady pull, hard and even, like a man standing on the bottom of a hole and holding the end of a rope and leaning back.

And it did not stop.

She leaned against it with both hands and her heels on the rock and she could feel it: a load, a dead weight, an even unarguable pull from down in the slot that had nothing to do with the tide and nothing to do with a man swimming, and she knew exactly what she was holding, because she had stood on a lot of decks and held a lot of lines and there was only one thing a line did when somebody at the other end had hold of it and somebody above them had hold of them.

Then it came slack, all at once, all of it, so fast that she went back onto her heels, and the slack ran down the slope into the dark and disappeared.

And the diesel, down in the canyon, changed its note.

She knelt on the rock with the empty spool in her hand and looked at the flat green water, and out over the basin the submersible came up off the wall without a light and turned and ran for the mouth of the canyon, and it went in and the sound of it went down and down to a hundred and eighty metres and was gone.

Isla did not move for a minute. Then she stood up, and picked up the hydrophone lead and pulled the hydrophone out and coiled the lead, and put the frame's loose stones back on the planks in the order Tob put them, and walked back across the flat in the sun with the empty spool in her hand and the wet line over her shoulder, and did not run, because running was a thing that would tell the whole shelf that something out there had gone wrong.

She sat in the shore station with the laptop open and the log on the desk and did the sums, because the sums were the only part of the day that would hold still.

Eleven minutes. He had gone in at thirty-two minutes to twelve and the line had gone still at the fifth minute, which put the still place at twenty-three minutes to twelve, which was eight minutes early, and she went back through the table and the wind and the state of the bar and found the eight minutes, and the eight minutes were her own. She had read the tide off a rock in a wind and had not carried the wind into the number, and the flood had come early through the slot the way water does through a hole when the sea behind it stands higher than the table says, and Tob Marek had gone in on the last eleven minutes of a window that had closed eight minutes before he got there.

She wrote it in the log in the flat data hand, with the times, and beneath it, error: eight minutes. Cause: not carried in the estimate. Own.

Then she closed the log and sat with her hands on the desk and looked at the wall.

Two things, she thought. There are two things, and you are going to have to choose one, and you have about forty hours to do it in.

The first thing was the mainland. She had the drive and the drawings and the receipt and the chain of custody on the jars, and she had a copy of everything in a waterproof case under the floor of the shed, and Voss was on the mainland with a phone and a name and a will to be arrested, and there was a mail boat on Tuesday and a patrol vessel she could reach on the radio, and if she went now and took what she had and made a statement, then the operation on the shelf would be broken by somebody else in some other month and Tob Marek would be in a holding facility in a canyon and the only man who had ever put a hand on the bar would be the only man who went to prison for a wall he had seen in ninety-four and reported to nobody.

The second thing was the gat.

And the second thing had the load on the tethers in it.

She pulled the drawings out of the drawer and spread them on the desk over the log and stood over them with her knuckles on the paper, and went back through it the way she had gone through it in Voss's kitchen, slowly, twice, three times, until the shape came up off the page, and the shape was this: four anchors, two on the canyon wall and two on the lip, four tethers at eighty tonnes each, a margin of about twelve, a base hanging at eleven metres in a slot of flat water that was flat only because a man on the mainland kept it flat, and beneath the base and beside it a gat that on an ordinary flood carried two point four and on the spring flood would carry three.

They had him. He was down there now, and they had him, and Sable had docked his submersible and put his crew on the wall and the base would be on its highest alert of the year, and every door in it would be shut.

And in nine days there was a tide that would run three metres per second through a hole that had never once in twenty-nine years seen more than two point four.

She put her finger on the mooring plan and moved it down the page to the anchor on the lip, and sat down in the chair and looked at it for a long time.

They have him, she thought. And I cannot go down the slot and get him out, and if I go to the mainland he sits in a box, and if I do nothing he sits in a box. And there is one thing in this whole business that is bigger than the crew and the door and the submersible and the man holding the line, and it is the thing I have been measuring for four months without knowing what it was for, which is that the water in that canyon is being held off the rock by a man's hand and four threads, and the threads have a margin of twelve tonnes on them, and in nine days there is a tide that will put ninety on one of them.

She sat back.

She was not going to use it to kill anyone. She had said that to Voss on a quay and she meant it, and it was still true, and it would stay true, because the point of the whole of it was that the reef was going to come out of this alive and a body count was a thing that came back out of the water and grew over the evidence like weed over a seam.

But a gate did not have to kill a man to close on him. A gat could close on a vessel the way the canyon had closed on the survey of ninety-four, and a vessel pinned on a bar with the current on its beam was a vessel that was not going anywhere and a crew that was not going anywhere, and a base whose tethers were being asked to hold ninety tonnes was a base whose doors would open for the sea and whose men would go up on the spine and whose radio would go out to anybody who was listening, and out on the shelf, in nine days, in the middle of the spring tide, there would be a woman sitting on the frame with a hydrophone and a laptop and a satellite link and a chain of custody.

She sat up and put both hands on the drawings.

The base was not a wall she could break. It was a thing held up by the water, and she had been measuring the water for four months, and the water was going to do the work for her, and the only thing she had to do in the whole of it was be in the right place at the right minute, with the evidence, when it did.

And that was the same thing as a rescue, if she got it exactly right. It was a rescue with a tide in it.

She reached for the laptop and pulled up the shelf table and put the spring tide on the screen with the twenty-second on it and the south channel at three metres per second and the flood behind it at half past eleven in the morning, and she opened her own current file from three months of nights on the rock and laid one against the other, and the shape she got was a shape she had already drawn.

The water is the weapon and the geometry is the map, she wrote in the log, in the flat data hand, and under it, Tob taken on the dock, approx. 1148. Line held, then slack. Submersible docked; base closed. Passage of the gate by the race: on the spring flood the load on the lip anchor goes to approx. 90 t against approx. 80 t of tether capacity. The arrays hold the base up. The tide will test the arrays. Therefore the tide will open the base, and the base at spring flow will put everything it has on the spine and the wall, and a submersible on a bar in a gat with three metres per second on its beam is a submersible that is not going anywhere.

She stopped, and read it back, and read it back again, and did the arithmetic under it once more with the wind in it this time, and then she put the pen down and sat in the chair in the small damp room that smelled of salt and diesel, and looked out of the window at the flat southern water and the place where the slot went down into the dark, and thought about a man who had waited twenty-nine years for the last dive of his life and had got it and had not come back.

She had two things to choose between and she had chosen, and the choice was not the safe one, and now there was a day to prepare, nine days to wait, one man to bring out, and no one on this shelf to tell.

She picked up the pen and added the last line.

No one will come before six in the morning. The buddy box is empty. I am alone on this, and I intend to stay alone on it, because the only way Tob Marek comes out of that canyon is from the water side, and I am the only person on this shelf who reads the water.

She closed the log and put it in the drawer under the drawings and turned off the lamp, and outside the window the beacon came on out in the basin, two and then one, two and then one, patient as a clock, and the tide began to make against the flat with the long hiss she had heard every night for four months and would hear for nine nights more.
