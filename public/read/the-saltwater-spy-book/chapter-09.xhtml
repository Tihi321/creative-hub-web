The Broadcast

The flood took her the way a hand takes a rope, without asking, and for the first forty seconds Isla Renn did not swim at all. She let it carry her, face down and arms back, past the bar and into the neck of the gat, and she counted the seconds off the way she had counted them off a hundred times on the frame, and at forty she put her head up and breathed and put her head down and began to work across the stream.

The water was colder than it had been on the neap. A spring flood in a gat pulls deep cold up off the bottom of the canyon and pushes it over the bar, and the cold sat against her face and her hands like a second skin, and it made the count harder because the count had been built in water she could feel and this water felt like something else. She put it out of her head. Cold was not a number. Cold was a thing she could carry or could not, and she was carrying it, and the wall was coming up on her left the way the drawings had promised.

The second thing she noticed was the sound.

On an ordinary day a gat is loud only at the race, and the race is a roar you hear from the shore and a hiss you hear up close. What she heard now was not a hiss and not a roar. It was a long low note under everything, like a pipe being leaned on, and it came through the water and through the bones behind her ears, and she knew that note. It was the note of the arrays taking a flow they had not been built for. Four baffled panels set in the rock, tuned to turn the water and pay the surplus past the lip with a kind of industry, and the sound of that was a steady hum. What she heard now was a hum that had a breaking in it, a thin high thread laid over the low note, and the thread was the sound of a plate that was not tight.

She had time to think: the cut ends are singing. Then the wall came up out of the green and she had to work.

The pocket was exactly where she had drawn it, and for a moment that was worse than being wrong, because being wrong would have meant turning back and being right meant going in. It was a fold in the western wall a little above the bar, where the rock turned back on itself and made a hand's width of shadow and a metre and a half of still water, and at the mouth of it the stream went past at three metres a second and a little over, the colour of smoke, carrying strings of weed and one pale shape that could have been a fish and could have been a piece of foam and did not matter either way.

She took the pocket the way she had planned to take it, which was feet first with the spool held flat against her chest and one hand hooked into the rock, and the stream took her and tried to peel her off the stone and she let it, and then it let her, and she was inside.

Inside, the water was over her head and quiet, and she stood on the rock with her shoulders against the fold and the kit bag hanging under her and the line running out of the mouth of the pocket and away up the gat toward the bar. She checked the line. It was taut but not loaded, laid across the stream the way she had laid it on the neap, and the spool was full and the crank was free and the case was dry, and the first part of the plan was done, and she had forty minutes or a little more before the flood peaked.

She had entered the water at eleven forty-two, a minute after the table said slack at the bar and a minute after her own reading said the sheet had gone over. The bar had a wall coming six minutes behind the sheet, and the wall was the thing that would take the panel, and the panel was the thing that would take the base. By her count the wall would reach the canyon lip at four minutes past twelve, and the base would know about it a minute or two after that, and the dock would begin to work. She gave herself until twelve twenty-five in the pocket and no longer. That was forty-three minutes. The margin was the same three minutes it had been when she did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had been done by hand in a shore station under a lamp, and now it was being done by hand in a hole in a wall, and the answer was the same, and the answer was that she had just enough.

She put her head against the cold rock and made herself watch the stream, because watching the stream was the only instrument she had, and what she was watching for was a change.

The change came at twelve oh one.

It came as a stiffening of the note. The low pipe under everything, the note the arrays made, rose by a step that was not much of a step and was enormous, and the thin high thread over it went from a break to a shriek and then stopped, and in the stopping the whole gat went quieter and the stream in the mouth of the pocket went faster, and Isla Renn, standing in the dark with her shoulders on the rock, said out loud into her mask, "There it is."

The seaward panel had gone.

There was no great report and no grinding and nothing any fisherman on the shelf could have heard over his engine. There was the loss of a hum and an acceleration of the water, and then a second note arriving underneath the first, a deep irregular thumping, and the thumping was the panel turning over and over in the canyon on its way down and out, and each thump was a piece of the base's foundation leaving the wall.

She began to count the thumps and lost them at seven, and then the water in the pocket changed again and she felt it: a slackening, faint, at the very edge of what a body could feel, and she knew what it meant before she had the sentence for it. The load had come off the arrays and onto the spine, and the trim was paying out on the tethers, and one of them, the one on the lip, was being asked for more than it had ever been asked before, and a structure being asked for something it cannot give decides within seconds whether it will hold or let go.

It held. She felt it hold, the same way she felt the note, as a settling under her feet, a long slow lean in the rock that moved the whole mouth of the pocket a hand's width and then stopped, and the hum came back up and the stream eased by a little and the base, off its trim and alive and hurt, went on standing on three good threads and one that was not going to be the same after this afternoon.

There is your open door, she thought. Now open it.

The dock lights came on at twelve oh four.

She saw them through the mouth of the pocket, a pale wash that lit the stream from below like a lamp held under a table, and then the thumping of engines, and then the submersible came up the canyon on the flood, blowing its tanks and running fast and shallow, and Isla pressed back into the fold of the rock and watched it go. It passed the mouth of the pocket four metres off, a fat white hull with its skids down and its lights on and two men visible in the wet cabin, and the man on the aft deck was lashing something down with the haste of a crew that had been told to be off the bar before twelve twenty, and the submersible went past her and up toward the neck of the gat and became a pale shape in the smoke of the stream.

One man and one vessel off the base. That left the rest of them in a hole whose foundation had just lost a panel, and it left Tob somewhere down there, and it left the door open, and the door was the thing she had come for.

She came out of the pocket on the flood, downstream of the spine, which was not a thing she had planned to do and was a thing the water made her do, because the stream off the pocket carried her past the mouth of it and down the wall whether she liked it or not, and she went with it and kept low and kept her hands on the rock and let the water put her where it wanted, and the water put her down the canyon wall four metres to a shelf of rock that ran out from the base's own foundation, and the shelf was a landing. There was a rail. Somebody had bolted a rail and a foothold to it in grey-green paint, and it was the kind of thing a crew used to get from a boat to a spine in a swell, and Isla reached the shelf and took the rail and pulled herself up and lay on the stone with the stream going past underneath and the base standing over her.

The spine was four metres above the water and the trim was wrong. She could see it, the way she could have seen a floor go steep in a room. The whole platform leaned toward the wall, and the wet flanks of the holding tanks caught the dock lights and ran with water, and there was a man on the spine with a lamp in one hand and a radio in the other, and he was looking down at the deck and shouting, and what he was shouting about was the leak.

She had not predicted the leak. A spine off its trim pays out in one direction and comes up in another, and a hatchery in a canyon is built to sit still, and when it does not sit still the insulated tanks, moulded as one-piece shells and held in cradles that spread the load, are not built to carry bending through their walls, and the man on the spine with the lamp was looking at twenty-four tanks that had just been leaned on and the water was coming out of one of them in a bright arc.

That was when she understood what she was going to do, and it was not the plan.

The plan said: enter the dock, find Tob, free him, transmit the evidence, leave on the ebb. The plan did not say what to do with twenty-four holding tanks that were going to empty themselves into a no-take reserve on the biggest spring of the year, and Isla Renn, lying on a shelf of rock with a rail in her hands, did the thing she had done every time the shelf had overruled her arithmetic. She read the situation again and she took the reading.

The tanks were leaking and the fluid was coming out of the spine and going into the canyon and would go down the canyon and through the gat and out onto the reef beyond the reserve, and whatever was in a holding tank was by definition an animal that belonged at two hundred metres and nothing at twelve. Twenty-four tanks of the wrong water in the wrong place was a worse event for the reef than the pump had been in twenty-nine years. The panel had opened the door. The door was going to let out the tank.

She made the reading and she made the decision in the same breath, and she got up onto the shelf rail and swung herself up over it and dropped onto the base's outer catwalk, and she stood up in the open in the middle of a structure she had spent three months watching through a camera, and she was seen almost at once.

The man with the lamp on the spine turned and saw a woman in a dark suit and a mask on his catwalk and did the only thing a man does when he sees the thing he was told would never happen, which was to open his mouth and shout for Sable.

Isla walked past him.

She had crossed the catwalk and put her hand on the ladder to the spine before he finished the shout, and she went up the ladder with the kit bag over her shoulder and the line on her wrist, and the spine deck was a wet grey rectangle under a grey sky she could see through the gap between the spine and the canyon wall, and at the far end of it a door stood open in a housing pod with a light burning inside, and in the middle of the deck, lashed to a stanchion with two turns of line and sitting on a crate with his hands behind him, was Tobin Marek.

He saw her come up the ladder. He did not say anything. He had the look of a man who had spent nine days doing arithmetic of his own in a hole, and the arithmetic had come out the same way hers had, and now the answer was standing in front of him on the deck of a sinking base, and he did not have it in him to be surprised.

"Which tanks," he said.

"All of them," she said. "They're split at the seams. There's fluid going over the side now."

"How long."

"Twenty minutes to the reef, and it will make the reserve about the time the peak breaks it."

Tob turned his head and looked at the housing pod at the end of the deck, and what he looked at was a hatch set in the pod's floor, and what he said next was the first time he had ever given her an order on the shelf.

"The valve manifold's under that hatch," he said. "There's a head tank that feeds all twenty-four and a single valve at the low point, and if you shut that valve the fluid in the tanks goes nowhere. Voss's drawings would have it. I saw it when they brought me in."

"Can you reach it."

"Not with my hands behind me."

She cut him loose. The line was Tob's own thin waxed line, the same diameter as the coil she had carried in the kit bag, and it came apart under the cutter in two seconds, and Tob Marek stood up on a leaning deck in a canyon in the middle of the biggest spring of the year and rubbed his wrists and looked at her.

"I'll do the valve," he said. "You do the rest."

And then a man came out of the housing pod with a hand torch in his fist and stood at the top of the ladder with the light on them, and behind the light, unhurried, in a dry coat, with a mug in one hand, came Warden Sable.

Isla had seen his face twice: once on a permit document and once in a photograph in a fisheries newsletter, and both times he had been a man in a hat at a distance, and the man on the deck was older and thinner than either of them and had the flat unhurried look of someone who had walked out of a doorway into weather and expected the weather to be fine, and it was the mug that told her the most about him, because the mug was steady. His crew's world was at fifteen degrees and leaking, and Warden Sable had walked out of a housing pod carrying a cup of coffee and had not spilled it.

"Dr. Renn," he said. "You're the current woman."

"I'm the current woman."

"You cut my panel."

"I cut two of its bolts. The tide cut the rest."

Sable looked past her at the open water past the gap in the canyon wall, where the stream was running high and brown and fast, and he took a sip of the coffee and did not look at Tob, and Isla understood that he had already made his decision about the afternoon. He was not going to fight anybody. He was going to stand on the deck of his base in the sun and drink his coffee and be the last man on the spine, because that was the one place in the world where Warden Sable could not be arrested.

"The submersible's away," he said. "She'll be three miles out by one. You've cost me an afternoon's work, and a platform, and I'll be honest with you, I'll miss it. Twenty-nine years. You don't get a second one of those."

"You had twenty-nine years of a no-take reserve," Isla said. "You pumped the reef's own larvae through a grate in its floor. You lit a beacon on the rock to do it. You put a wall over a canyon so the chart would look like water, and you put a man in a hole for nine days for reading the chart right, and there is not one thing about that which the shelf will forget."

"I didn't put a wall over anything," Sable said. "Voss did. That's his business and it's always been his business. I'm a tenant."

"You're the operator. It's in the file."

"What file."

And that was the moment Isla Renn had been carrying in the kit bag since ten past nine that morning on Tob Marek's wooden frame, and she took it out of the bag and held it up in one hand on the deck of the base, and it was a small grey waterproof case with a latched lid, and what it contained was a drive with two files on it that had already gone out on a satellite window to three addresses that morning.

"The file I transmitted from the shelf at ten past nine," she said. "To the Marine Authority, the Fisheries Board, and the Warden of the Cobalt Shelf Marine Reserve, which is a title with your name on it. It has the seven jars and the chain of custody. No—the chain of custody is on the mainland, and the receipt for it is in this case. The file has the jars' photographs, the four camera cards, the hydrophone tracks at a hundred and eighty metres, Voss's signed drawings with the tether loads on them, and three months of my log, and a statement with your name in it, and the date on the statement is the twentieth. It does not depend on whether anybody believes me today. It depends on whether the post works."

Sable's mug came down. It came down the way the stream had come down the pocket, which was not fast and not slow, and he looked at the case in her hand and then at her, and for the first time that afternoon Warden Sable had to do arithmetic.

"You've been reading Voss's drawings," he said. "You know what happens to this place if the arrays go."

"I know. One thread is at ninety and its capacity is eighty. The water is going to test that thread about eleven minutes from now and then again on the ebb, and I have not touched it, and I am not going to touch it. The arrays are gone on their own. The panel went on its own. Nothing happens on this shelf today that the water did not do."

"And these men."

"Your submersible is away. Your dock is open. Your part of it is already over." Isla kept her voice flat. "There is one thing that is not over, and it is not yours to decide."

She turned and pointed at the bright arc of water coming off the flank of the tanks, and it was fuller now, and the stream under the base was carrying it down the canyon in a pale braid.

"The reef is the reason," she said.

Sable looked at the leak and looked at Tob Marek, who was standing at the open hatch in the deck with his hand on the handle, waiting, and who had not moved since the valve had been named, and Sable looked at the woman holding the drive and did something that Isla had not predicted and that she understood completely.

He stood aside.

"I want it on the record that I opened the valve," he said.

"You won't be the one who opens it," Tob said, and went down the hatch.

Isla stood by the rail and watched the flood take the base.

It went the way she had drawn it and the way she had not: the third thread came up to its load and held, and then the ebb came on, and the ebb was the thing nobody had drawn, and when the water turned the base lost its footing on the wall. The trim was gone. The spine went down on the seaward side until the deck stood at fifteen degrees and the deck lights went out one by one up the length of the platform, and the submersible's empty garage below the spine filled and began to take on the wet of the canyon, and the dock's open door looked out on brown water and a gap of grey sky and then not even that.

The valve was shut. Tob came up the hatch at twelve nineteen with his hands wet and his face set and the leak done, and the tanks sat full and quiet, and the whole of the base's cultured cargo stayed where it had been grown. Twenty-four tanks, and in them maybe twenty-nine years of a reef's recruitment, and it was all going to be evidence now, and evidence was better than an empty canyon.

The stream ran at its peak and did not quite break the third thread. It came to ninety and held and came down again, and at the top of the flood the race came over the bar in a long grey wall and stood up against the rock and did not go anywhere a crewman could put a boat. Sable's two remaining men took the ladder down to the shelf and stood on the shelf with the rail in their hands and looked up at the deck and then out at the neck of the gat and back at the deck again, and by then it was not a question of leaving. It was a question of waiting.

At half past one the ebb began to eat the race out from under the gat and the water over the bar went from a wall to a sheet to a standing ripple, and a boat came up the south channel against the ebb and stood off the neck of the gat with a lamp on its deck, and then a second boat. They were mainland boats. Isla Renn saw them from the deck of the base with her hand on the rail and her kit bag on her shoulder and the drive back in its case and the receipts in it, and she did not wave and did not shout and did not do any of the things the woman in the shore station would have done three months before, because the boats were not there for her. The boats were there for the file.

The first uniform came aboard the spine at twenty past two.

He came over the rail in the last of the ebb and found a wet grey platform off its footing and a middle-aged man in a dry coat sitting on the crate at the foot of the ladder, and beside the man on the crate a woman in a dark suit who stood up when the uniform came over the rail and held out a small grey case.

"Dr. Isla Renn," she said. "This is the drive. There's a chain of custody on the jars on the mainland and the receipt for the chain of custody is in this case with it. The statement inside is dated the twentieth. The rest is the shelf's."

The uniform took the case and looked past her at the spine and the tanks and the open dock and the two men on the shelf, and then at the man on the crate, and he said the sentence Isla had been waiting three months to hear, and it was not a clever sentence and it did not need to be.

"Warden Sable," he said. "You're under arrest."
