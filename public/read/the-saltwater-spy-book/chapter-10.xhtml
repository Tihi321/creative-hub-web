The Reserve

The file came back to her in the second week of the following month, and it came back thick, and with it came a man from the authority who stood on her flat at first light with a waterproof case under his arm and a clipboard he held the way a man holds something that might bite.

Isla Renn had expected a receipt. What she got was a survey vessel standing off the shelf for three days and an officer who walked the southern basin with her without saying anything for the first twenty minutes, which she appreciated, and who then told her, in the flat voice of a man reading terms, that she should not be alive.

"You broke a reserve order," he said. "You cut bolts on a structure inside a no-take area, which is destruction of habitat. You entered that structure without authorisation. You transmitted material without prior notice. You did all three alone, on a spring tide, with no support, and I have read the sequence twice and my professional opinion is that you made every mistake except the two that would have killed you."

"I know."

"Then you also understand that the file is accepted because the file is correct, and not because the file is yours. If your readings had been wrong we would be charging you now, and the fact that they are right is the only thing standing between you and a long conversation in a room in the mainland town." He held out the case. "The survey is yours. Sign for it."

She signed on the flat with the case on her knee and the wind taking the paper corner, and then they stood there for three days and put her readings against a hull's readings, and the readings argued for a while and then stopped arguing.

It was her reading of the bar that decided the boundary. She had drawn the line by hand off the rock, in the ten days alone after Tob was taken, on the outer edge where the flood spends the last of its strength, and the authority's own survey had it forty metres wide, out past the reef. The officer sat with her on the flat for an hour with both sets in front of him and asked her where she had put the pencil and why, and she told him about the wall of water that stood up off the bar at peak and the slack pocket in the western wall that closed at full flood, and he listened and put his own number down and picked up hers.

"You will be wrong about something inside two years," he said, closing the clipboard. "Everybody is. What matters is that from now on you will be wrong in writing, and checked against water."

The reserve closed in the spring, and the order was long, and Isla read it twice at the shore station with the lamp on and the door shut. The part that mattered to her was on the third page. The southern basin was named a total no-take zone within the Cobalt Shelf Marine Reserve, which meant nothing alive could be taken from inside it by anyone for any reason, and which also meant no one could disturb what lived there. The panels of the base, the anchors and the spine were listed as removed hardware, taken to the mainland as evidence. The canyon was entered in the schedule as a survey site with a fixed cadence: six visits a year, three at spring, three at neap, each one recorded and filed.

The pump had stopped the afternoon the base went down its spine, and the grate in the basin floor had not pulled since, and the beacon on the grate had gone dark. She walked the flat at low water in the first week of the closure and stood over the grate with the sun on her back and nothing moving in it, and what she felt was not triumph. It was closer to the feeling of a door hung in a wall she had built.

Two threads were left open, and the authority closed one and left the other open on purpose.

The tanks came off the base when they cut the spine loose at the end of the week. Twenty-four holding tanks of cultured cargo were lifted onto a barge and taken to the mainland and held as evidence, and the animals in them were by then a question of law and biology at once, and the two did not agree. The compound that had made them worth keeping was the reason the ring had held them alive in a canyon for twenty-nine years, and the reason the authority could not simply put them back on the reef was that they had been grown on mesh and fed in tanks and were not, strictly, wild animals any more. They went to a marine laboratory under a court order and a clean chain of custody, and what happened to them after that was a question about the future of the reef. Isla put her name on the request to return them to the southern basin under survey if the laboratory certified them clean. The request was noted. It was not yet decided.

The submersible had gone and stayed gone. It had left the canyon at twelve twenty with two men aboard and was three miles out by one, and the boats that came in on the ebb took Sable and the two crewmen who had stayed, and the submersible had not been in the gat when they came and had not been on the shelf and had not been found in the weeks after. The authority put out a notice to the ports and the coast stations and the customs posts and the ferry companies, and the notice carried the hull's description and its paint and the two men's names, and no one had answered it. Sable had told the authority, in a room with a recorder on the table, that the hull was leased and the men were contract crew and he did not know where they had gone, and the authority wrote that down and did not believe him and had nothing to put against it.

Isla kept the notice in the front of the log. She had looked at the two names four times and knew neither of them, and running was what the water did with anything it was not asked to keep, and the file had their names in it, and the file would keep.

Voss confessed in the third week, and the confession took four hours, and what came to her by post was four pages of transcript with the authority's stamp on the corner.

She read it at the shore station in the evening, and she read it the way she read a survey, for the numbers. He had designed the arrays in ninety-six and built them in two stages and maintained them for the better part of three decades. The first stage he had built for the money, which was small and had been paid to a company he half owned and had used to keep a boat and a survey afloat. The second stage he had built because by then the first stage was a fact that could be told, and he could not tell it. He had drawn the panel geometry on the same table he had used for his thesis and signed the drawings with his own hand, and the drawings had gone into the file and the file had gone to Sable, and the shelf had been held down for twenty-nine years by four baffled plates and a signature.

The transcript was in his own words and it was flat, and the flatness was the thing. He did not plead and did not weep, and when the officer asked him why he had not stopped, he answered in a sentence Isla copied into the back of her log by hand because she did not want to lose it.

Because a reef has no memory and a man does. I could tell myself the shelf did not know. It did not. That was the whole of my defence and I knew it was no defence and I used it for twenty-nine years.

He was not jailed. The offences he could be charged with ran against a structure the ring owned and through a permit that had lapsed in ninety-two, and the law went where the structure was and not where the signature was. What was done to him was done in the profession, and it was done hard, and in the open. His paper was retracted in the journal that had carried it, in a notice that named the settlement tiles and the basin data and the pump, and for a while the retraction was the most-read notice in the field. His name was struck from the authorship and from the register of the shelf's research, and every paper that had cited his work now carried a note saying the citation rested on basin data run with the pump pulled. The note went on more than four hundred papers. The field read the note and drew its own conclusion, which is the conclusion a field always draws, which is that a man's work is his and his name is on it.

He came back to the shelf once, in the autumn, on the mail boat. He walked up the gravel path to the shore station and stood outside the door with his hands in his coat and did not come in. Isla was at the desk with the lamp on, and she saw him through the glass and opened the door and stood in it.

"I'm not asking you for anything," Voss said.

"Then why are you here."

"To look at it." He tipped his head at the water. "I built the thing that held it down and I have never looked at the flat that was over it. I would like to look once, and be told to go away, and then I will go away."

She stood in the door with her hand on the frame. The shelf was at the turn of the water, and the flat over the southern basin was running down and out, and the reef was beginning to breathe.

"Low water's at twenty past," she said. "Walk down at four. You can look at it from the flat and you can look at the grate and see that it is not pulling. Then you walk back up and get the boat. You are not coming in, and the data on this shelf is not yours, and if you put one hand on one rock of that flat I will put it in the log and it will go to the authority the same day."

"I understand."

"Then go down at four."

He went down at four and stood over the grate for a long time with the sun going down the western edge and the low water running under his boots. Isla watched him through the glass the way she had watched the basin through a camera for three months. At the end of it he walked back up the path and got the boat and did not look back and did not come again. She wrote the visit in the log that evening, because every visit to the flat went in the log, and the entry was four lines and all four of them were true.

Tob Marek did his part in the spring, and his part was the frame and the water and the eleven houses, and of the three of them it was the part that lasted.

He had given his survey notes and his memory of ninety-four on the mainland and had come back on the mail boat and gone straight out to the reef edge before he went into his house, and after that he was simply on the shelf the way a rock is on a flat, which was not a thing anyone had arranged and was not a thing anyone could take away. He built the new frame at the east edge and he built it right, and he had known the east edge was the place before she did and had not said so until she had read it off the swell herself. He took the posts down to the rock by hand and set them with a bar and a rope and no machine, because a machine on the reef edge at nesting season is a machine the birds remember, and he set the camera housing above the height the swell had reached on the worst day of the winter before and marked the two heights on the post in pencil so that the next person who set anything on the edge would see them.

He did it standing waist-deep in the shallows, which was his exception, and which had been his exception once before, and which was an exception he never discussed and never stopped making. Isla watched him from the flat with the tide table in the plastic sleeve and the log open on her knee, and she understood by then that the exception was the whole of the man's repayment, and that the last of it had been taken in a canyon on the twenty-second, when he had gone down the hatch in the spine's deck to the valve manifold and shut the valve with the base off its footing and the water coming in.

The baseline of the settlement tiles was another of his. The authority wanted a fixed set of sites measured every visit so the reef's recovery could be read against something that did not move, and Tob took the soundings of the eastern flat and the two flat patches on the northern edge by hand with a lead line and marked them with permanent pins set in the rock, and the pins became the baseline of the reserve schedule. He also did the thing the authority had not asked for, which was to walk the village with the tide table and the survey schedule and the new boundary and explain the no-take rules in plain words to every house on the leeward side. What he told them was not the language of the reserve order. He told them that a reef is a place you leave alone if you want it to go on being a reef, and that if you want something out of it you take it from outside the line, and that the line had been drawn by a woman who could read the water off the rock, and that they could read the water off the rock too if they wanted. Three of them did.

The nesting season came on, and with it the two night watches.

The authority had not asked for a night watch and did not pay for one. What the authority had done was put the reserve on probation and put a schedule against it and put the whole thing in writing. What the village did on its own was to take two nights a week out of the nesting season and sit on the eastern edge with a lamp and a camera and a radio and watch the birds come in. It was not enforcement and it was not surveying. It was the kind of thing a coast village does for itself, which is to keep an eye on the thing it lives next to because it has understood at last that the thing is worth keeping an eye on. Isla put the two watches in the schedule with the survey entries, because anything in the schedule went to the authority, and the authority wrote back to say that the watches were noted and were not a reserve function and would be recorded as community observation, and the notification sat on the shore station wall under the lamp.

Tob took one of the two watches. The other went to a house at the top of the row with a young family and a boy of eleven who had been given the chair on the point, and Isla found that out in the third week of the season, when she walked the edge at dusk and found the boy in the chair with the lamp and the camera and Tob sitting behind him on the rock with his back to the sea and his face to the village, being the second pair of eyes. That was the arrangement they had made, and neither of them had told her.

The lights on the water at night were the tide table and nothing else now. There was no blue-green beacon on the grate and had not been one since the twenty-second. The pulse in the water was the ordinary gurgle of a flood in the channel and the tick of the small animals on the wall. What the night watch looked for was a torch where a torch should not be, a boat inside the line, flat water disturbed where it should be still. They looked for a while and saw nothing, which was the point. In the fourth week one of the watches saw a light on the northern edge and radioed it in, and it was a fisherman from the next island coming round the point late with a hand lamp on his bow, and it was written in the log as a contact and not an incident, and the authority wrote back that the contact was recorded and the boundary had been checked, and the reserve was doing what a reserve does, which is to be looked at.

On the first evening of the season Isla sat on Tob's new frame at the east edge with the log open on her knee and the tide table under her hand and the hydrophone lead in the water with nothing on the end of it and everything on the end of it, and she wrote the first entry of the new season in the hand she had used for three months.

The entry was a list. Current at the bar, one point two nine metres per second, her reading, off the rock. Beacon, off. Grate, no pull, the flat over it cold and clear and running down on the low. Tanks, twenty-four, held on the mainland as evidence, request pending for return under survey if certified clean. Submersible, not found, notice standing to all ports. Voss, retracted, removed from the register, walked the flat once and went. Sable, arrested, and the file named other men above him whose names she did not yet have. Tob, in the water at the east edge, watch taken. Village, eleven houses, one boat shed, no street light, three asked for the tide table, two took the night watch.

She put the pencil down. The tide was at half ebb and the light was off the western edge and the flat over the southern basin was running down and out, and the reef was beginning to breathe the way a reef breathes at every turn of the water, which is without hurry and without memory and without any need at all for the woman on the frame to do anything but watch.
