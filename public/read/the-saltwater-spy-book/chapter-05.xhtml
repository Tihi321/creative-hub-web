The Engineered Signal

The cards came out of the housings at first light, four of them, cold enough to ache through her fingers, and she carried them up the flat in the pocket of her jacket with her hand closed over them, as if they were the only four things left in the world that had seen what the water had seen.

Tob walked beside her with the mallet over his shoulder and said nothing, which was his way of saying a great deal. He had slept four hours in the boat shed, on the bench, under a canvas coat, and he had been awake before her and had already walked the flat at low water, looking at the frame and the traps and the sand, and had come back to say only: "Everything's still there. Everything's still standing."

The shore station was a small concrete building on the edge of the reef with one room in it and a desk and a lamp and a laptop with a satellite link, and it smelled of salt and diesel and the cold of the rock it stood on. Isla put the kettle on the gas ring because her hands were shaking and because the kettle was something to do. Then she dried each card with a cloth, wiped the contacts, checked the seals on the housings for the fourth time, and slid them one after another into the reader.

Two thousand nine hundred and fourteen frames.

She went through them at speed, four to a screen, the way she went through everything, and the reef passed in front of her in the dark: a rock cod nosing the sand, a small green turtle coming in over the lip of the drop, polyps opening on the two coral heads, a cuttlefish hanging in the frame like a wet leaf with its arms folded. Then the frames went dark and the dark went on for a hundred and forty images, and then the pump started and the light came up on the grate and the count began.

"You want to eat something," Tob said from the doorway. He would not come inside while the laptop was on. He said the building smelled of the wrong kind of engine.

"After."

"After what."

"After I see it."

He came and stood outside the window with his back against the concrete, out of the light of the screen, and watched the flat, and she went on through the frames.

At 21:19 the water went flat and the pulse went long, long, short, and the trap at the head of the drop fired eleven times in twenty-two seconds, and Isla stopped the run and sat back and let the frames play one at a time.

The submersible came into the top of the frame from the upper left, low and black and unhurried, with a bow wave like the lip of a spade. There was no light on it anywhere above the waterline. The trap was working in the near infrared, and the infrared found what the eye would not have: the wet sheen of the hull, the thin bright line of water draining off the deck, the hump amidships with its two handrails, and along the centre of the deck, between the hump and the stern, a long low stack of shapes lashed down and covered with a tarpaulin.

The frames were grainy. The water between the lens and the hull was full of the night's plankton, particles moving like dust in a lit room, and the hull moved behind them and came out of the far side of the frame as a shadow on a wall.

"Eleven metres," Isla said. "Give or take half a metre. Beam about two and a half."

She said it to the room, which was what she did with measurements, and Tob heard her through the window and said, "Twelve, if it's got a skeg on it."

"Eleven and a half," she said, and stretched the exposure until the deck went from black to grey.

Under the tarpaulin there was a structure, and the structure had a shape, and the shape had edges. She ran the stretch further and pulled the contrast and pushed the gain and the noise came up with it like a swarm of flies, and under the dark there was a rack.

Three tiers. Each tier a frame of square tubing. And inside each tier, set side by side with the precision of things that have been made to fit, four boxes.

Twelve boxes on one skid, and behind the skid, under the stern of the tarpaulin, the corner of a second skid.

"Three tiers of four," she said. "And a second skid, so at least twenty-four."

She wrote it in the log, in the flat data hand.

2121. Vessel westbound over basin at approx. 30 m from drop trap. Length est. 11.5 m. Deck cargo under tarpaulin: skid-mounted rack, 3 tiers x 4, second skid partially visible. Est. 24+ units. Units boxed. No markings legible at this resolution.

Then she went back and looked at the boxes one at a time, the whole night's work for four boxes in six frames, and the shape of the thing came up out of the noise.

They were the size of cool boxes. They had hard corners, and along the top edge of each there was a line of small round holes, evenly spaced — vents, or drains, or both. On the long side of each box there was a panel, and the panel was not solid. It was mesh.

She put the cursor on the mesh and blew the frame up until each pixel was a thumbnail and the mesh filled the whole screen, and behind the mesh there was mass. Pale mass and orange mass and grey mass, and some of the orange had a shape to it that she knew, a lumpy shape with spikes on it, the shape of a thing that lives on a rock and passes water through itself and eats what it catches.

"You've found it," Tob said. He had come to the window and put his head into it, and the smell of diesel was not mentioned.

"I've found a box with something orange in it."

"You've found what's in the box."

"I've found a photograph of the outside of a box."

He looked at the screen the way he looked at water, not at the whole thing but at the edges of it, and he pointed with his chin at the bottom of the skid.

"Lashing," he said. "Four across the top, cross on the corners. That's not a boat knot. That's a deck knot."

"What do you mean?"

"That's the way you tie a load down on a ship, not the way you tie it down on a dinghy. That's a load somebody expected to move in weather."

She looked at the knots. He was right. She could not have said why, but he was right, and she wrote it down anyway, because when Tob said a thing about rope she wrote it down.

Then he said, "And look at the corner, there, on the second skid."

She looked. On the corner of the second skid, where the tube met the leg, there was a smear of grey-green.

"Paint," he said. "Same as on the mooring eye in the wall. Same colour, same job. That's a yard that paints its gear the same colour, and that yard painted the eye in that wall."

"Tob. It's a photograph of a smear."

"It's the same colour."

"That's not evidence. I can't take a colour to a court."

"No," he said. "But you can take it to me."

At slack low water the following morning they went out over the flat with two buckets, a trowel, sample jars, a hand lens, and her own camera, and they walked the shallow rock pools from the shore to the head of the drop.

The outflow slick was gone. The basin was cold and clear, and everything on the flat looked the way it always looked, which was the way it had looked for the three months she had been walking it: rock and sand and pools, a scatter of broken coral, a shell here, an urchin there, the little brown things that live in the mud and never get a name.

They found the first fragment in a notch in the rock on the seaward side of the flat, three metres down, wedged where a wave had pushed it and the rock had held it. It was the size of a thumbnail, pale grey, soft, and it was still wet.

"Sponge," Isla said.

"Any sponge. There's forty species on this flat."

"This one's not from this flat."

She put it in a jar of clean sea water, and she capped the jar, and she wrote on the label the date and the time and the depth and the coordinates and her own name and Tob's, and then she took eleven photographs of the jar and the jar's label and the place on the rock where the jar's contents had been.

"You're fussy," Tob said.

"I'm a witness," she said. "There's a difference, and the difference is paperwork."

They found six more fragments in the next hour, all of them on the seaward side of the flat, all of them below the line the slick had drawn, all of them pale and soft with a bloom of orange on the underside, and all of them too heavy to have floated and too light to have crawled.

Second low water after vessel: seven fragments recovered, leeward of outflow line, seaward side of flat. All fragments similar. Vessel's outflow has shed material on the rising tide.

She carried the bucket back up the flat like a woman carrying something alive, which she was.

The microscope was on the desk at the shore station, under the lamp, and she sat at it for four hours and did not eat.

A sponge, she wrote in the log, is not an animal with a shape the way a fish is an animal with a shape. It is a sack with holes, a pump with no heart, a colony of cells that have agreed to live together in a building of their own making. And the building has bricks. Every species of sponge builds its scaffolding out of spicules, tiny needles of silica or lime, made inside the cells and set in the jelly, and the needles have shapes, and the shapes belong to the family, the way a roof tile belongs to a country, and that is the oldest way there is of telling one sponge from another.

She teased the fragment apart on a slide with a needle, added two drops of water, and put it under the lens.

The spicules were there, thousands of them, and they were long, and they were straight, and they were sharpened at both ends, and mixed among them were shorter ones with a small head on one end like a nail. She recognised the family by the shapes and by the size, and she wrote the shapes in the log, and she wrote beside them the sentence she had written in the archive once, on a different shelf, in a different year.

Fragment: oxeas and styles; cortical spicules form a felt at the surface. Consistent with the dictyoceratid-adjacent clade of the deep shelf, at least. Will key further with a full specimen, which the permit forbids me to take.

Then she sat with the microscope for five minutes without moving, because at twenty times magnification, in the base of the fragment where the tissue was thickest and oldest, there was a straight line.

She changed the lens and looked again, and it was still there. A straight edge in a living thing, and behind it, in the place where the sponge's tissue gripped its rock, a corner of black mesh.

She went and got the kettle and did not drink the kettle's water, and then she came back and laid the fragment in a dish of sea water and worked at it with a needle for a quarter of an hour, and then she had it out whole, washed, in a clean dish under the lamp: a fragment of sponge the size of the first joint of her thumb, orange on the underside, grey on top, and set into its base, grown over and half hidden and unmistakably man-made, the corner of a piece of plastic netting, the kind used on the mesh side of a culture tray.

And through the netting, tied to the netting with a knot of monofilament, there was a length of line with a colour band on it. Blue. Faded. A tag.

"Tob," she said. "Come and look."

He would not come in. She took the dish out to him in the morning light and he bent over it with his hands on his knees, and he looked for a long time, and he did not need to know what a spicule was.

"That's grown round the net," he said.

"It's grown round the net."

"That's a thing that was tied down and got away."

"That's a thing that was grown, tied down, and cut."

He stood up.

"Can I say something."

"Yes."

"I spent three years looking at that wall from a boat," he said, "and the whole time I thought I was looking at a cave, and now you're telling me it's a farm."

"It's a farm," she said. "Somebody is farming the reef."

"No," he said. "Somebody is farming here. Which is the same sentence with the worst word moved to the front."

She went back through the frames that night with the fragment on the desk beside her in its dish, and this time she could see the boxes for what they were.

Twelve to a skid. Vents along the top and mesh along the sides, so that water moves through and air moves through and a live animal does not cook in its own waste. Slatted bases so the thing sits level on a shelf. Rounded corners so nothing scrapes. Shaded — she could see, in the way the infrared sat on them, that the top of each box was paler and smoother than the sides, a panel that reflected, an insulated lid.

These were not crates. These were holding tanks, and they were the best kind of holding tank there is, the kind designed by someone who understood that a deep-sea sponge brought up to warm water dies in twenty minutes and a deep-sea sponge held in its own temperature, in its own water, in the dark, will stay alive for a week.

Twelve boxes on a skid. Twenty-four on the deck, at least, and possibly thirty. Each box, at a guess, eighty litres. Each box, at a guess, a third full of animal.

"Six hundred litres of living sponge," she said out loud, in the empty room, at half past nine at night.

Then she did the arithmetic, because arithmetic was the one thing that could not be talked away.

The vessel had made the crossing at nine twenty-one in the evening and had gone into the canyon and been tracked to a hundred and eighty metres. The pump ran from nine o'clock until half past one. The door opened when the pump had pulled the basin down to still water. There had been a green light on the shelf, and the green light had been an answer, and an answer means a question, which means a schedule, which means the vessel was expected.

But you do not run a boat out of a garage in a reef for twenty-nine years for six hundred litres of anything. There are easier ways to move cargo and cheaper ways to hide it. You run it that way when the cargo cannot be landed anywhere, when the cargo has to stay alive and stay cold and stay in the dark the whole way, and when the place you take it from is the only place in the world where it grows.

She opened the satellite link and went looking for what grows.

It took her two hours to find the thread, and when she found it she sat very still for a long moment and then read it twice.

The compound was called ageliferin. It comes out of a sponge, out of the same family of sponges she had seen under the microscope, and it does two things. It kills bacteria. And it breaks up biofilm.

Biofilm was the word that mattered. Biofilm was the slime that builds on a wet surface and turns it into a city: the plaque on teeth, the scum in a pipe, the slick on a hull, the film that grows on a surgical implant or a catheter or a heart valve and turns a clean piece of engineering into a source of infection. Biofilm was one of the quiet problems of medicine and shipping both, and the compounds that dissolve it without killing everything nearby were not common and were not cheap.

And the sponge that makes it, on her shelf, in her reserve, in the dark water below the flat where she had eaten lunch for three months, was a sponge that grew at the rate of a fingernail a year and had to be collected one animal at a time.

Unless you farmed it.

Unless you built a box that keeps it alive, and a boat that keeps it dark, and a base sunk into a canyon that keeps it out of sight, and unless you had learned how to take a wild strain off a deep reef and grow it and feed it and make more of it, in the cold and the dark, at the rate the market wanted. And unless you had a reason to do all of it somewhere no one kept records, which is the whole reason anyone builds anything underwater.

There was an answer in that somewhere, and Isla sat with the lamp on and the fan turning and the water moving outside the window, and she did not have it, and she knew she did not have it, and that was the point.

The question had changed. On the first night it had been what is this place. On the second it had been is it alive and how does it work. Now she sat at a desk with a fragment in a dish and a photograph of a box with orange inside it, and the question was who, and the question was why, and behind both of them there was a third one that she was not ready to write down.

Somebody is taking the reef apart molecule by molecule and selling the pieces, and the pieces are worth more than the reef, and the reef is a hundred and sixty years from growing back.

She wrote it in the log anyway, in the flat data hand, because the log did not care what she was ready for.

Conclusion. Deck cargo is live animal transport: 24-30 modules, flow-through, insulated, shaded. Fragments recovered from outflow are sponges of the deep shelf clade, and one fragment has grown over a manufactured mesh substrate with a tagged monofilament line embedded in its base. The animals are cultured — attached to a substrate by hand, fed, grown, cut from the substrate and packed for shipment. The stock is a cultured strain of the local deep-shelf animal, maintained and multiplied in the base from a wild founder taken before 1996 and never returned to the reef. The base is not a poaching operation. It is a hatchery, a holding facility and a loading dock, operating inside a total no-take reserve, over a depth not shown on any chart, on a site that has been active for at least 29 years. Compound of interest: ageliferin (antibacterial, anti-biofilm). Commercial value of a live, transportable, cultured strain: high enough to justify 29 years.

She stopped with her hands over the keys.

Then she went back one more time, because there was a thing nagging at her, and the thing was the light.

The beacon on the grate was blue-green. She had the spectral data from the shore station camera, four nights of it, and she spread it out and the peak sat in a narrow band, which was not the band of a lamp. It was the band of a living thing that makes light with a chemistry instead of a bulb.

And the pulse was 0.8 hertz, forty-eight beats a minute, from nine o'clock until half past one, then low, then off, and the pattern had one long note and one short note in a repeating figure, two and then one, two and then one, the way a lighthouse says what it is and where it is to a boat that cannot see the shore.

The vessel had no lights. She had watched it come out of a hole in a wall and cross a reef in the dark. It had to be steered, and steered by what? By a light that pulsed in a code in the water where the driver could count it, and the code changed when the door opened, and the code said where the grate was, and the grate was where the water was, and the water was flat there because the pump was holding the tide off.

But the light did a second job, and Isla sat up when she saw it, because the second job was in the footage itself.

In the frames from the first night, before the boxes and before the boat, there had been fish on the sand: forty, fifty small fish standing nose into the current, and the numbers had grown with every night of footage, and she had read that as a feeding aggregation and moved on.

It was not a feeding aggregation. She went back over four nights of frames and counted them, and the pattern was plain. The fish came in after the light. The light went on and the small animals came to the light, out of the wall and out of the deep water over the drop, following the wavelength their bodies had been built to follow since before there were fish in the sea. And the pump pulled. And the pump pulled everything in the basin through a grate in the reef floor, and behind the grate there was a screen, and the screen was the filter, and what the filter caught was the thing the whole apparatus had been built to catch.

Larvae.

The farm was not only fed by its own stock. The farm was fed by the sea. The light was a lure, and the pump was a net, and every night of every spring the reef's own young — the drifting larvae of the deep shelf's slow-growing animals, the ones that spawn once and settle once and take a hundred years to make a thicket — came out of the dark and went down a grate in a rock and into a box, and were packed, and were carried out to sea to be grown, and sold, and made into medicine, and never came back.

Isla put down the pen and went out of the building and stood on the flat in the dark, which she was not supposed to do alone, and looked at the water.

The tide was making. The far channel was doing what it always did, hissing through the narrow place, and the reef was loud with a hundred thousand small things eating and being eaten, and out in the basin, at the base of the south wall, she could see it if she knew to look: a faint blue-green glow, steady, patient, saying come here, come here, come here forty-eight times a minute, to everything alive in the dark that had spent millions of years learning to obey a light.

She thought about what a beacon was. A beacon was a signal sent to be received, and a signal sent to be received is a signal that has been trusted. The reef had been asked a question every night for twenty-nine years and had answered it every night, because a larva cannot know that a light on a rock is not a light on a rock.

She stood there a long time.

Then she went in and wrote an email to Dr. Elias Voss and attached the photographs of the fragment and the mesh and the tag, and the frames of the skid, and one line of text.

Elias. Not sensor error. I have recovered material. I need an independent analyst and a chain of custody.

It was half past eleven at night. The reply came in nine minutes, which was faster than he had ever answered her for anything, ten minutes faster than the last time.

The phone rang four seconds later.

"Tell me exactly what you have in your possession," Voss said, and his voice was calm, and it was careful, and it was not the voice of a man being told about a sponge. "Is it alive? Is any of it alive? Have you told anyone else?"
