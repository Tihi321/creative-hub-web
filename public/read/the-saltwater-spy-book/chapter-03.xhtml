The Recluse

The village on the Cobalt Shelf was eleven houses and a boat shed, and it sat on the leeward side of the main island where the wind could not reach it in the summer and could reach it in the winter for weeks at a time. Isla had been there twice, once to buy batteries and once to borrow a wrench, and both times she had gone back to the shore station before dark because the village did not have a street light and the path back was a track of gravel and dead coral that turned to mud when it rained.

She went on the third morning after the night in the basin. She had not slept well. The field log was on the desk, and the chart was on the desk, and the accident report was on the desk, and she had read all three twice before she put them in a bag and walked down the track to the village. The morning was cold and still, the kind of still that comes before a change in weather, and the path was dry for the first time in a week. She walked it in her boots, the gravel crunching under her soles, the dead coral pale and brittle in the light, and she thought about what she was going to say. She had not prepared anything. She had not prepared anything because she did not know what she was going to find, and she did not know what she was going to find because she did not know what the accident report meant, and she did not know what the accident report meant because she had read it twice and the words were clear but the meaning was not.

The accident report said that Tobin Marek had left his buddy in a cave. It said that the current had taken the buddy. It said that Tobin Marek had gotten out and the buddy had not. It said that Tobin Marek had panicked. It said that Tobin Marek should have gone back. It said that Tobin Marek did not go back. It did not say what Tobin Marek saw in the cave. It did not say what Tobin Marek saw in the cave, and that was the thing that mattered, and that was the thing that was not in the report, and that was the thing that Isla had read twice and could not find.

She walked the path. The village came into view, the houses small and grey against the green of the hill, the boat shed at the end of the row, the roof corrugated and rusted at the edges. There was no one on the path. There was no one in the village. There was no dog, no car, no garden, no fence. There was a single figure at the end of the row, in the boat shed, and the figure was working, and the figure was Tobin Marek, and Isla walked towards him with the bag on her shoulder and the words she had not prepared.

Tobin Marek lived at the end of the row, in a single-storey house with a corrugated roof and a concrete path that went from the door to the boat shed. The path was clean. The house was clean. There was no garden, no fence, no dog, no car. There was a workbench under the boat shed roof, and on the workbench there was a row of tools hung by size, and beside them a coil of rope wound so tight and even that it looked like a solid object, and beside the rope a diving mask with the strap replaced and the glass clean and no scratches on it.

He was not in the house. He was in the boat shed, sitting on a crate with a piece of wood in his hands and a plane on his knee, working the wood flat with slow strokes that made a thin curl come off the surface and fall to the concrete. He looked up when she came in, and he looked at her the way a person looks at something that has come into a place it should not be.

"You're the biologist," he said.

"Yes."

"You're the one who was out on the reef last night."

She did not answer. He put the plane down and the wood down and sat with his hands on his knees, which were thick and scarred and had the look of joints that had been bent in cold water for a long time.

"I saw your dinghy," he said. "I saw the anchor light. I saw you come back at half past eleven. I saw you walk up the track."

"I didn't know you were awake."

"I'm always awake. I sleep when I can. I wake up when I can't."

She put the bag on the workbench and took out the field log and the chart and the accident report and laid them out in a row, the way she would have laid them out on the desk at the shore station, and she stood back and looked at them and then looked at him.

He picked up the accident report first. He read it, and he read it again, and then he put it down and looked at the chart, and then he looked at the field log, and then he looked at her.

"You wrote my name," he said.

"I found it in the archive."

"You found the survey."

"Yes."

"Twenty-nine years ago. Cable route. Two surveyors. I was surveyor two."

"Yes."

He put the report down. He picked up the plane and put it down again. He did not pick up the wood.

"The report says I left my buddy in a cave," he said. "It says I left him in a cave and the current took him and I got out and he didn't. It says I panicked. It says I should have gone back. It says I didn't go back." He turned the page over, as if there might be something on the back. "It doesn't say what I saw in the cave."

Isla waited.

"I saw a wall," he said. "A wall that wasn't a wall. A flat face in the rock, clean, no growth on it. I saw a seam in it. I saw a light inside the seam, red, small, blinking. I saw a mooring eye in the rock with paint on it. I saw all of that, and I told the surveyor, and the surveyor wrote it down, and the surveyor wrote it down wrong, and the surveyor wrote the soundings wrong, and the soundings came out smooth, and the chart came out smooth, and nobody went back." He stopped and looked at the tools. "And I went back three times, alone, over three years. I found the same wall, and the same seam, and the same light, and the same mooring eye, and the same paint. Every time. And I told nobody, because I was scared, and because I had already lost a man in that water, and because I thought if I went again and the thing was still there, I would have to go in. And I would have to go in, and I would not come out."

He said this in a flat voice, the way a person says a thing that has been true for a long time and has not changed. He did not look at her while he said it. He looked at the workbench, at the tools, at the rope, at the mask.

"I don't go in the water," he said. "I don't go in the water on that reef. Not since. Not for twenty-nine years. I fix boats. I fix engines. I plane wood. I sit in this shed and I plane wood and I don't go in the water."

Isla sat down on the crate opposite him. She put the chart flat on the concrete between them and put the field log on top of it and opened it to the last page, where she had written the data, and she put her finger on the line.

"Southern basin," she read. "Grate, two metres by three, six-centimetre bars, scoured, trimmed margins, panel light nought point eight hertz. Outflow seam in south wall, two by four, warm water plus one point eight. Mooring eye with paint. Diesel noise and pressure signature from inside."

She took her finger off the page.

"You saw the grate. I saw the grate. You saw the seam. I saw the seam. You saw the mooring eye. I saw the mooring eye. You saw the red light and I saw the red light, and you saw the paint and I saw the paint. You saw all of it twenty-nine years ago and I saw all of it last night, and the thing is the same, and the thing is still there, and the thing is still running."

He looked at the chart. He looked at the contour lines. He looked at the spot depths, two hundred metres apart, and he looked at the smooth curves that ran through the basin without bending, without crowding, without wandering.

"Those lines are wrong," he said.

"Yes."

"They're drawn through a gap. A gap in the soundings. A gap in the data. A gap in the rock."

"Yes."

"The gap is a canyon. I know the canyon. I know the shape of it. I know where it narrows and where it opens and where the current runs through it and where the current runs around, and where the current runs back into it when the tide turns." He said this to the chart, not to her. "I know the gat. The south gat, the narrow one, the one that runs from the basin out to the shelf. I know the depth of it. I know what it does on the flood and what it does on the ebb."

He stopped. He looked at his hands. He put the plane down.

"I don't go in the water," he said again.

"I know," Isla said. "I'm not asking you to go in the water. I'm asking you to tell me what you know. The caves and the gats and the currents and the places where the water can trap a person. That's all. What you know."

Tob looked at her for a long time. He looked at the chart. He looked at the field log. He looked at the accident report. He looked at the workbench, at the rope, at the mask.

"The mask is clean," he said. "I cleaned it. I clean it every year and I don't put it on."

"I know."

"I don't go in the water."

"I know."

He picked up the plane. He put the plane on the wood and did not plane it. He put the plane down and the wood down and put his hands on his knees.

And then he started, in the flat voice, on the number that had been sitting in him for twenty-nine years.

"The current in the gat on the south side runs at two metres per second on the flood," he said. "It runs at two metres per second on the ebb."

He stopped. He looked at his hands.

"That's not right," he said. He said it once, quietly, to the workbench. "That's not right."

He sat with it. He sat with it long enough that Isla, who had been taught to let a silence run, understood that this was the whole of what the man had to give on the first pass, and that pushing it would only make him say it again. She looked at the chart and let the quiet stand in the shed, and the wind moved the galvanised sheet on the roof once with the two-note sound it made in that village in any weather.

"It's not two," he said at last.

"No."

"It's not two on the ebb and it was never two on the flood. It's one and four tenths on the flood on a middling tide, and the soundings put it at nine metres in the throat, and nine metres and one point four is not a number I would have given a survey even on my worst day." He put both hands flat on the bench, and his voice was steady now and ordinary, the voice of a man reading a table. "The line in my notebook said one point four. The line in the fair copy said two. The fair copy was the surveyor's. I signed the fair copy anyway, because you sign it, because if you don't sign it you don't work, and the man making the fair copy worked for whoever it is that wanted the gat to look shallower and faster than it is."

Isla kept her voice level. "Why would anybody want the gat to look faster?"

"So that nobody swims it." He looked up at her for the first time in a while. "And so that nobody surveys it. Because a gat that runs at two and one that runs at one point four have one thing in common, which is that a man with a boat and a wife and a permit says no, and it stops there. It stopped me for twenty-nine years."

He got up off the crate and went to the workbench and moved the tools aside and pulled out from under it a roll of oilcloth, and unrolled the oilcloth on the bench, and inside the oilcloth was a survey notebook bound in green with an elastic strap rotted to nothing, and he opened it at the middle and turned it so she could read.

The pages were water-stained and the pencil had gone faint, but the hand was precise, and it was a survey in the old form: stations, bearings, soundings by lead line, tide times noted at the head of each page. The last section was the cable route, twenty-nine years old, and there was a page with a sketch on it, and the sketch was a plan of a gap in a wall.

"There," he said, and put his finger on the sketch. "That's the seam. That's the wall. Nine metres and a bit at the throat."

Isla read the numbers off the page and did not touch the book. "Do you have a copy of anything else? The fair copy?"

"No." He said it without regret. "The fair copy went in at the end of the survey and I never saw it again. This is the only one I brought off the island, and I brought it off and I kept it in a chest for twenty-nine years, and I have not opened it since I put it in the chest, and I am opening it now."

"Why now?"

He looked at her with the expression of a man who had just been asked why the tide comes in.

"Because you went out on the reef at night and came back at eleven and did not die," he said. "That is the whole reason. I have watched a lot of people walk that track. You are the first one I have watched come back up it after dark with a light on her head."

They sat in the boat shed for two hours. Isla read the field log aloud, line by line, and Tob sat with the green notebook on his knee and corrected her twice on depth and once on the bearing along the south wall, and each time his correction was quiet and it was right, and there was no argument in it.

At the end of the two hours Isla rolled the chart back up, and Tob took the notebook and put it on the workbench between them and did not put it away.

"I have more than one question," she said.

"I got that."

"The first one is the canyon. The chart has the basin smooth on the south side and the canyon not there at all. I need the canyon as it actually is. Where it narrows and where it opens and where the walls are vertical and where the bar is at the mouth."

He turned the green notebook to the back, where there were more pages than the survey, and drew with the pencil off the bench. He drew for a while, and what came up out of the pencil was a throat: a narrow mouth, a bar, a long run south-west, a wall that stood straight on the west side, and at the far end a widening into a basin he had never got to see properly because he had only ever seen it through a mask in bad light with his buddy ahead of him and his hand on the rock.

"Here," he said, and put the pencil down on the vertical wall. "That's the west face. This is the bar. This is the slot above the bar. On the flood the water comes over this bar and stands up against this wall and there is a pocket in the fold of it, there, that a small boat can sit in at slack, and I have seen it. On the ebb it reverses and the slot drains and the bar bares for a long minute."

Isla looked at the drawing and went through the tide table in her head and put her finger on the slot. "A person could get into the base from that pocket and no other way."

"A person could get into the base from that pocket and no other way," Tob said. "And I am not that person."

"I haven't asked you to be."

"You will."

"Maybe," she said. "Not today."

He put the pencil down and looked at her for a moment with the flat, careful look of the man who had spent twenty-nine years watching the track from the shed, and then he asked her a question of his own, which she had not expected.

"What's kept you on the shelf for three months when everybody else who comes here leaves after two weeks?" he said. "You don't have a boat and you don't have a crew and you sit in that concrete room with a lamp on and you put numbers in a book."

"I'm a witness," Isla said. "It's the only thing I have ever been any good at."

"Witness to what?"

"A current that goes against its own tide. A light that pulses on a reef floor where nothing pulses. A man in an office who answers a report in nineteen minutes and calls it sensor error." She picked up the log and put it back in the bag. "The witness part I can do. The other part is the part I came down this path for."

"What other part?"

"The part where somebody has to stand in the water."

She had not meant to say it that plainly. But Tob did not flinch. He sat with the notebook on his knee and looked past her out of the shed at the flat water at the end of the path.

"I have thought about that," he said. "For twenty-nine years. I have thought about standing in the water again, and I have thought about what it would take."

"And?"

"And the answer has never changed. It would take the water still going in when I am ready to go in, which it will not, because I am not going to be ready, which is a thing I have known about myself for a long time." He closed the notebook. "But I will tell you this. If it is ever something the water has to do anyway, and it is not a dive, and it is not under, and it is not alone, I will listen to the conversation. I am not saying yes. I am saying you have my ear."

"That's more than I had this morning," Isla said.

"That's more than you had this morning," he said. He stood up off the crate. "Come back tomorrow. The notebook stays here but you can read it here. Bring the tide table and I'll put the gat against it and you can find out what the water has to do and when."

She walked back up the track in the afternoon light with the bag on her shoulder, and the wind had come up the hill the way it did in the afternoons, and the dead coral crunched under her boots the whole way, and about halfway up she stopped and stood and looked back down at the row of houses and the boat shed and the concrete path and the clean mask on its peg, and she thought about the fact that she was alone, and she had already learned what to do with a fact, which was to write it down and go on.

She sat at the shore station desk that night with the lamp on and the door shut and the tide table open under one hand and the laptop under the other, and she did the arithmetic the way she had done it for three months, because the arithmetic was the only thing that could not be talked away, and the arithmetic said this: a gat whose real flow was one and four tenths was a different reef than a gat whose flow was two. It was a gentler reef, and it was a reef with a longer window, and it was a reef in which a person who had been told all their career that a place was impassable might actually be able to stand in the shallows at a slack tide and never once go under.

That was a small thing, in a way. It did not prove the base. The grate proved the base, and the light on the reef floor proved the base, and the wall with the seam and the mooring eye proved the base. But the false number in a fair copy, signed and filed and carried on the chart by every boat on the coast, was a different kind of proof. It was proof that somebody, twenty-nine years ago, had needed the gat to look worse than it was. And if somebody had needed that, then somebody had known, twenty-nine years ago, exactly what a person could and could not do in that water, and had gone to the trouble to make sure that no person ever tried.

To make a place unswimmable on paper is not to hide a thing. It is to hide a person from the thing. And the only reason to hide a person from a thing is that the thing is worth hiding.

She wrote the entry in the flat data hand, and it was four lines, and each line was a fact.

Tob Marek confirms wall, seam, mooring eye, red lamp, summer 1994 survey (cable route). Tobin Marek: surveyor two, green notebook, soundings 1.4 m/s local in gat throat; fair copy filed at 2.0 m/s. Fair copy is the copy on the chart. A false figure on a chart is a fact about a person, not about water. The reef is the reason.

She closed the log and turned off the lamp and sat for a while in the dark with the wind against the window and the tide making against the flat with the long hiss she had heard every night for three months, and she thought about a man who had cleaned a mask every year for twenty-nine years and never put it on, and about a man with a mug who had answered her in nineteen minutes, and about which of them she was going to have to ask for what, and when.

She already knew the shape of the answer. The next thing was to get Tob Marek into the shallows at a slack tide with the water still and no swell, which was not a dive and was not under and was not alone, and which was the only door in the man that she had found that opened.
