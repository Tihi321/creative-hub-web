The Tidal Surge

The nine days went by the way water goes through a hole, which is to say they did not go by evenly and they did not go by slowly in the middle, and Isla spent them on the reef edge at Tob's frame with the tide table in the plastic sleeve and the log open on the planks and the hydrophone lead trailing in the water.

She had told the village nothing. She had told the mainland nothing. On the third morning she walked to the top of the path and stood where the gravel turned to dead coral and looked down at the eleven houses and the boat shed and Tob's single-storey roof among them, and she turned around and went back to the flat without going down, because there was no sentence she could say to any of them that would not end with a man's boat being taken out by men who did not know the shelf, on days when the tide would not stand for it.

The neap went out of the water in the middle of the first week. She watched the range diminish on the pole at the flat and watched the race through the outer channel soften, and every morning she took the numbers off the pole and every evening she put them against the table, and the table did not once move her: the twenty-second was the spring, the flow would peak at three metres per second through the south channel and a little less in the gat above the bar, and the flood behind it would be one long push from a little after ten in the morning until well past one.

For a boat in the slot that was fatal. For a base hanging at eleven metres on four threads, it was a test with a number on it.

On the fifth day she went over the drawings again, and the thing that had been a shape in her head became a sequence: on the flood the arrays on the south wall would meet a flow they had never been asked to damp. The baffles would take it and turn it and bleed the surplus off past the lip. The pressure on the spine would come up against its trim, and the trim would answer by paying out on the tethers, and the tethers would take up whatever the trim gave them, and one of them, the one on the lip, would be asked for about ninety tonnes against a capacity of eighty. Voss had said it and she had read it and she had worked it twice, and both times the number came out the same: the margin on the lip tether would not be a margin that afternoon. It would be a deficit.

She did not need the tether to part. She needed it to be asked.

A tether asked for ninety tonnes did one of two things: it held, or it let go. If it held, the base would ride high and the doors would stay shut and the men inside would spend the afternoon explaining to Warden Sable why the floor had gone steep and the lights had flickered and the dampening field had run at forty per cent over its design. If it let go, the base would come off its trim and hang on three threads and drag its belly across the wall, and the men would be up on the spine with the water coming over it, and the submersible in its garage below would be a thing they would have to get out of the canyon before the ebb and the bar made the mouth of the gat a place a vessel could not pass.

Either way, the base would open. Either way, there would be a moment when the whole of it was a door that had been shut for twenty-nine years and had decided, for reasons of its own, to be a door that was open, and the only person on the shelf who would know when that moment was coming was a woman sitting on a wooden frame with a hydrophone.

The only person, and the only witness. That was the whole of what she had to trade. Everything on the mainland that could still be set moving — the drive, the jars, the drawings, Voss on a quay with his name on a statement — would move in its own time and on its own tide, and its own tide was a mail boat on Tuesday and a patrol vessel out of the north harbour and a week of statements, and Tob Marek would spend that week on the floor of a canyon. She could go now on what she had, or she could do the other thing, and she had already done the other thing in her head a hundred times and could not stop doing it.

She wrote the sequence in the log, and beneath it the arrival times and the times she would need to be in the water, and then she closed the log and looked at the flat southern water and knew what she had already known for a week.

She was going to have to go through the slot.

She did not go through the seam. She thought about the seam for two days and then she did not think about it again, because Tob had told her what it was and Tob had been right, and a woman in the slot in a spring tide with a closed door above her would have been a body on the bar in eleven minutes flat.

The way in was the way Tob had gone, and the way Tob had gone was the way she had drawn it: the slot above the bar, the western wall, the pocket in the wall that a small boat could tuck into at slack and that a swimmer could hold at a push. She worked the geometry off the chart gap and Voss's drawings until the pocket was a place rather than a line, a recess in the wall where the rock folded back on itself above the bar and the current let go of its grip because it had somewhere else to push.

At slack the pocket was a handhold. On the flood it would be the last quiet stone in the whole gat, and then it would be gone.

That was the part she could not make stand still for her: the eleven minutes Tob had been meant to have, and the eight minutes the wind had taken off the front of it. There was no wind forecast for the twenty-second on any page she could get, and the bar was sitting clean and flat, and the table said slack at the bar at eleven forty-one, and she had already found the error in her own reading and had written the eight minutes down in the log in her own hand, and she was not going to find it again by looking at the same rock.

So she built herself a second reading. Every morning at half-flood she went out onto the flat at the southern edge and stood in the ankle-deep water over the bar with a staff she had marked in centimetres and watched how the water came up onto a shelf of clean rock, and every evening she went out again at half-ebb, and she built a picture of the bar that was not a number in a table, because a table was made for shipping and a bar was made for water.

Five mornings. Five evenings. By the tenth reading she knew the way the flood came over the bar in two steps, the first a sheet and the second a wall, and she knew the wall came six minutes after the sheet and that the six minutes were worth more than any number in any table because they were hers, taken off the rock, with the wind in them.

On the morning of the sixth day she went down to the shed before first light, because the light would have made the walk a thing the village could see, and she stood in the doorway of the boat shed and looked at the wall where the tools hung by size, and at the peg where the mask had hung, which was empty.

She had known it would be. She had known it the second the line went slack. But knowing a thing and standing in the room where the thing had been set down are not the same, and she stood in that doorway for the length of a minute with the cold coming in off the water, and then she went in and took down what she needed.

A coil of the thin line off the shelf, the green waxed line Tob kept for the camera work.

Two shackles. A short length of chain, half a metre, the kind a man keeps in a boat shed because a boat shed always has a length of chain in it. The line winder, the flat spool on the wooden pallet with a crank on it, which Tob had used for a hundred survey casts and which would pay a line out evenly and, more to the point, would pull one back.

She carried all of it up the path in the dark in the kit bag and did not turn at the top of the village to look at the roof she had looked at twice before, because the looking had never once done anything but cost her an hour.

The sabotage was not a thing she had to invent. It was a thing she had to choose, and the choosing took her longer than the doing would.

The tethers were the base's whole life. Four threads from four anchors, eighty tonnes each, holding the spine at eleven metres and the trim answering to the load. If one tether was parted on the night before the spring, the base would come off its trim on three threads and the men inside would know within the hour that something under them was wrong, and they would close the dock and run the submersible out for a look, and they would find the parted end on the anchor, and the whole shelf would be alive with vessels and lamps by morning and there would be no spring tide for anybody to read.

If one tether was slackened, and slackened by enough to look like the sea's work and not a hand's work, the base would ride a little low and a little out of true and the trim would pay out on the others to compensate, and the men inside would spend a day chasing a fault they could not find, and on the twenty-second the load would come down on a tether that had been shortened overnight by a metre of slack nobody could account for.

She sat on the crate in the shore station with the drawings spread and worked the slack into the arithmetic, and the arithmetic gave her a number she did not like, because the number was a line that came in fouled on the third tether and a spine that went down four metres instead of two, and four metres was a difference that could put a man in the water, and the whole point of the plan from the first day on the quay had been that no man drowned because of it.

She started again.

The answer, when she found it, was not on the tethers at all. It was on the arrays.

Voss had built four baffled panels on the south wall and the canyon lip, and the baffles were the reason the water in the basin went flat every night, and they were also the reason the base had never once seen a current it had not been given. On the spring flood the baffles would take a flow they were never designed for. They would hold, Voss had said, because they had been built for the water the shelf actually had and the shelf's water would still be inside the design on any ordinary day, but a spring was not an ordinary day and the twenty-second was the biggest spring of the year, and a baffle panel with its end moorings cut would not damp the flow. It would take the flow, and turn on the two bolts that were left, and go further.

A baffle panel that had been cut free and gone with the stream would not only stop damping. It would turn in the race and go down the canyon like a door going down a stairwell, and it would pass beside the base and it would pass under the submersible if the submersible were at the dock, and it would take itself out through the mouth of the gat and be gone in the brown water off the shelf before any of them got their lamps on it.

And the men inside would not see a saboteur. They would see the sea take what the sea had given them, on the one day of the year the sea was strong enough to take it, and their first thought would be to get the vessel clear, and their second would be to get everybody up onto the spine, and the door would be a door that was open.

She worked it out in full before she let herself like it. The panel on the canyon lip, the seaward one, and the inner one behind it — the two whose anchors she had seen on the drawings and whose bolt pattern Voss had not thought to draw, because he had built them twenty-nine years ago and had never once expected anyone else to read them. She did not know how many bolts held each panel. She knew the panels were bolted, that the bolts were set in the rock, and that a panel whose end bolts were not there was a panel the water could take.

The only way to take a bolt off a panel at thirty metres, in a race that ran two and a half metres per second, on a day that was not the spring, was to put a hand and a pair of cutters on it, and all the hands on the shelf were either hers or the crew's or the three hundred tonnes of water running between them.

On the neap the sea itself would do what a diver could not. A neap flood through the gat is a gentler water and it stands clear at the bar for a slack long enough to work in, and on a neap a person who came down the west face from the flat with a line and a spool could reach the canyon lip and be back on the rock inside the hour.

The choice that she had been circling for six days sat on the paper and waited.

There was the safe thing, which was the mainland. The drive, the drawings, the receipt, the chain of custody on the jars, Voss on a quay with his name on a statement, the patrol vessel, the men and the authority and the wide lamp-loud machinery of the law. It would take a week and it would end Warden Sable's operation and it would end Voss's career and it would put Tob Marek in a holding tank in a canyon on trial for a wall he had seen in ninety-four and reported to nobody. There was a real chance, in that version, that the door would never be opened at all, because the moment the mainland moved on Sable he would take the submersible and run, and a man running from a canyon on the twenty-second of any month, with a spring under him, would not need Isla to wreck his base. He would do it himself.

There was the other thing, which was the one on the paper. The panel, the cut, the spring, the slot, the pocket, the frame, the hydrophone, the broadcast. It was a plan with a man's life in the middle of it, and the man was not one she had ever been given, and the plan only worked if she got it exactly right, and getting it exactly right meant a woman alone in the dark in a gat in a three-metre race with a line in one hand.

She had already chosen on the night the line went slack. She knew that. The reading of the drawings and the arithmetic of the tether and the discovery of the panels had not been the choosing. They had been the shape of a choice she had made in the shore station with Tob Marek's name written on a page, and she had made it because she had already worked out that the water was the only thing on the shelf that was bigger than the men who had taken him.

She wrote it in the log in the flat data hand.

Plan: panels on the canyon lip, seaward and inner. Cut on the neap, end bolts only, and let the flood take them on the spring. The panels leave the wall on the twenty-second and the base loses a dampening array and comes off trim and docks the submersible out and the door opens. I do not enter the base. I do not touch a tether. I go into the pocket on the wall before the flood and I stay there through the peak, and I come out on the ebb with the line and the camera, and I am off the flat before the water is. Under it: Duration in water: approx. 46 minutes on the flood, 30 on the ebb. Risk of the pocket being taken by the flow: moderate. If the pocket is taken, the wall holds the swimmer against the rock at 3 m/s; survival of the swimmer is not assured. Accepted. Own risk, not the reef's. The reef is the reason. If I am wrong, the reef stands, the operation stands, Tob Marek stands in a canyon, and the water does the work anyway on some other spring, and the only thing lost is the woman on the frame.

The neap was no good for anything except the cut, and she used it for the cut.

On the morning of the twentieth the flood ran at one point seven in the outer channel and there was no race in the gat worth the name, and Isla went down the slope from the flat with the kit bag on her back and the spool on her wrist and the line running back out of the slot to the frame, and she did not go through the slot because she did not have to, because the neap had put the canyon lip within reach of a diver if the diver went down the west face at slack.

She found the seaward panel first.

It was exactly where the drawings said, bolted to a shelf of rock that ran out from the wall above the bar, and it was a grid of four baffle plates set into a frame as long as a dinghy, and it was grey-green with the same grey-green that was on the mooring eye in the wall and the skid on the submersible, and the paint was flaking and new painting had gone over the flaking paint in patches, and the patches were the newest thing on the whole structure.

Voss had built it. Somebody was still painting it. Neither of them was going to get their hands on it again after today.

The bolts ran along the frame's land edge in a line. She put her hand along the frame and felt the paint and felt the lean of it and worked out which way the flood would push when it came, and it would come from the south and the east and it would push the panel up and inland against the land edge, and the load would be across the frame and the two bolts at the ends would take the turn of it that a lever always puts on the two ends of a lever, and if they gave, the panel would swing on the middle bolts and the middle bolts would follow it as a hinge follows a door.

She cut the end bolts.

The cutter was a hydraulics-and-hand type Tob had kept in the shed for cutting cable, and it went through the shaft of each bolt with a sound like a footstep on gravel, and Isla worked in the cold green water in the last of the neap flood with her mask down and her heart going the way it went when she was on the wrong side of a calculation, and she did the two end bolts of the seaward panel and left the middle ones, and then she came off it and let the current carry her down the wall four metres to where the inner panel sat against the base's own footing, and she did the same to its two end bolts, and the second time her hands were steadier than they had been, because the second time she knew what her hands could do in cold water.

She worked the inner bolts in eleven minutes and did not rush them and was not seen, and then the flood began to turn and she went back up the west face in eleven minutes of hard swimming and came out into the slot at the top of the bar with the kit bag on her back and the spool still on her wrist and the line still leading her home.

She had done it in forty minutes. She had planned eighty.

She sat on the flat with the wet kit bag beside her and her hands wrapped in the cloth of her own shirt and made herself breathe, and she went back over it once, coldly, the way she went over a reading. She had cut four bolts on two panels and had not touched a tether and had not touched the door. The seaward panel was hanging now on two bolts with the whole of the next flood behind it. The inner panel was the same. On the twenty-second the flood would come up the canyon at three metres per second and would find two panels that were hinged where they had never been hinged, and the panels would open into the race the way the panels of a gate open when the pin is drawn, and the rest would be water.

She had not been seen. She would have known by now. The diesel still had its note and the beacon still had its two and then one and the wall had no light on it, and she walked back across the flat in the cold sunlight with the spool in her hand and the wet line over her shoulder and did not run, and did not once look back at the slot.

The shore station had a satellite link that worked in a window, and the window the morning of the twenty-second was from ten past nine until half past nine, which put it a full two hours before slack at the bar and an hour before the panels were due to go.

She spent the two days before it writing the file, and she wrote it the way she wrote a log, in facts, in order, with the times on them.

She did not write the whole case. A case written before the case is complete is a case a lawyer will take apart. She wrote the documents that could not be taken apart: the seven jars and the chain of custody and the receipt; the four camera cards and their times; the hydrophone tracks off the canyon wall at a hundred and eighty metres, where the records showed a floor of eighty; Voss's own drawings, marked with the tether capacity and the design flow; and the log, hers, three months of it, with the eight minutes written in it in her own hand.

Then she wrote the covering document, and it was short, and it read as follows.

To the Marine Authority, the Fisheries Board, and the Warden of the Cobalt Shelf Marine Reserve. Enclosed are the documents relating to the structure in the submerged canyon of the southern basin. The structure is a hatchery, holding facility and transit dock for cultured deep-sea sponges grown for the pharmaceutical compound ageliferin, in operation since at least 1994, inside a total no-take reserve. The operator is Warden Sable. The engineer of the current-dampening arrays is Dr. Elias Voss. A member of my party, Mr Tobin Marek, was taken on the dock on the eleventh and is being held inside the structure. On the twenty-second the spring tide will test the arrays and come very near the design load on the mooring tethers, and on the twenty-second the structure will open its dock for the submersible to leave ahead of the race. Nothing about what happens on the twenty-second is to be taken as an accident. The water is the water. The structure is a man's structure, and it is right on the line where the shelf can no longer hold it, and the shelf is going to say so.

— Dr. Isla Renn, Cobalt Shelf, the twentieth.

She read it back and it was four paragraphs and every sentence in it was true, and she sealed the drive in the waterproof case and put the case in the kit bag and went out onto the flat to check the pole and the bar and the race and the sky.

The sky was going to be fine on the twenty-second, and the table said slack at the bar at eleven forty-one, and the panels would go about twenty minutes after, and the base would open about half an hour after that, and the submersible would be on the bar and the submersible would not be going anywhere.

She slept badly and woke early and went down to the frame with her hands full, and the morning of the twenty-second came up flat and grey and bright and cold, and the water in the southern basin was the colour of a knife blade.

At ten past nine she sat on Tob's wooden frame with her arm around the satellite housing and sent the file, with its times on it, to three addresses, and watched the little lights on the housing count off one, two, three. She did not transmit the part of it that she was going to do. She would not write that on any page that a court could read, and she would not write it on any page at all. What she sent was the structure and the record and the date. The rest of it was between her and the water.

Then she closed the laptop and put it in the waterproof case with the drive and the receipt and the second camera, and put the case in the kit bag, and did the last thing she had to do before she went in, which was sit down on the planks of Tob's frame and look at the flat water and make herself stop shaking.

She had done everything she could do from the land side. What was left was the water side.

She got up. She picked up the kit bag and put the line on her wrist and the spool on the line and the mask over her eyes, and she walked down the flat through the shin-deep water over the bar, and stepped off the edge of the bar into the slot as the flood took it, and the green closed over her the way it had closed over the last pair of fins on this shelf nine days before.
