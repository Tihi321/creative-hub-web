The First Watch

The mallet came down onto the head of the stake and the stake went into the sand of the shallows, and Tob Marek stood in the water to his knees and did not go under.

He had said it on the rock, before he stepped down, with his boots still dry and the mallet in his fist, the way he had said the thing about the current, the way he had said the thing about the mask, the way a person says a thing that has been true for a long time and has not changed.

"I don't go in the water on that reef. I don't go in the water on any reef. You know that."

"I know it."

"So this is the exception," he said. "One exception, and I'm telling you what it is. I'll stand in the shallows. On the flat. Where the rock is joined to the shore and the sand is joined to the rock."

"Waist deep."

"Waist deep and no more," he said. "I won't go under. I won't put my head in it."

"I know."

"I'll stand in the shallows," he said once more, and stopped saying it, and stepped off the rock into the water.

It came over his boots and up his shins and stopped at his hips. He stood with his feet set and the mallet raised, and the swell came up the flat in a long shallow push, a knee-high wave that came to his chest, and he stepped back up onto the rock and waited for it to go, and then stepped down again. Three times the swell came and three times he came out of it and stood on the stone and looked at the water until the water let him back in.

Then he drove the stake.

Isla had brought the gear down from the shore station in two trips, in the dinghy and then on foot across the flat at the last of the ebb, when the rock was bare and the sand shone and the shallow pools between the ridges were warm as a bath. Four camera traps, small boxes with lenses and infrared triggers that fired when something moved in the frame. Two hydrophones on a short aluminium pole. Cables, batteries, memory cards, housings sealed and greased. The laptop, the satellite link, the field log.

She opened the log on the flat rock and wrote the date and the time and the words, in the flat data hand her log had been written in for three months.

Station on reef edge, flat rock above southern basin. Four camera traps. Two hydrophones on pole at basin edge. Tob in the shallows, waist deep. No diving.

She read that last line twice. Six nights before she had dived the basin and come up with a cut hand and no instruments at all, and everything she owned that could see or hear was at the bottom of a canyon that was not on any chart. This time the gear would stand in the shallows and do the watching, and she would sit on the rock beside a man who had not put his head in the sea in twenty-nine years, and neither of them would go under.

The reef here was in a reserve. She had written the permit application herself, two pages of it, and the terms were short and total. No fishing, no taking, no removal of anything alive, no anchor dropped on coral, no disturbance to the animals that lived in the shallows. The strictest kind of protection the law allowed, and the only kind that worked, and everything inside the line belonged to itself, and nobody could take a shell out of it, and nobody could take a fish. Somebody had cut a hole in the wall of it and put a pump inside.

Tob worked the pole into the sand with the mallet, one blow at a time, and the pole went down a metre and stood straight, and the hydrophones hung on it a hand's width above the bottom. He carried the traps out through the shallows one at a time, in his arms like loaves, and set them where she told him, in the gaps between the coral heads, angled across the sand so that anything crossing the basin would cross the frame. He set the last one almost at the edge of the drop, where the flat stopped and the rock fell away into deeper water, and he stood in that water and did not look down into it.

"Three metres of line on this one," Isla said. "In case we have to pull it back."

"Three metres," he said. "A line has no lungs."

He nodded, and set the trap, and came out of the water onto the rock, and sat on the flat with his elbows on his knees and looked at the basin.

The night came down the way it always did here, sudden and complete, and the reef got loud.

There was the crackle of the snapping shrimp, thousands of them, a dry frying sound that never stopped. There were the grunts and the knocks of fish talking in a language she could hear and not understand. There was the long hiss of the northern gat as the flood began to make, the water hurrying through the narrow place between the islands and out over the shelf. Isla lay on the flat rock with the headphones on and the laptop beside her and the log open under a plastic sheet, and Tob sat against the rise with his back to the land and his eyes on the water, and the tide table said high water at twenty forty-seven and the flood would be building from a little before nine.

At nine o'clock exactly, the reef went quiet.

Not silent. Quieter. The crackle thinned out and the hiss of the channel stopped the way a kettle stops, and the water at the edge of the flat stopped going anywhere, and the small waves that had been slapping at the rock lay down and lay still. The anchor line of the dinghy, which had been bar-tight, sagged and hung. Tob sat up straight.

"I know that," he said.

The hydrophones heard it first: a click, thin as a fingernail on glass, and then the pulse, slow and measured and horribly regular. Isla counted it against the second hand of her watch and got forty-eight in a minute. Eight tenths of a hertz. She had heard the number before, in a logger on a shore station and in a file on a laptop, miles away with the wind at the window. In the water, in the dark, three metres from a pole in the sand, the sound was not a number at all. It was a pump. It was the deep slow working noise of a machine moving a great deal of water a small distance, again and again, forty-eight times a minute.

And under the pump, low and far off, there was a rumble that was not the sea. Diesel. Something between her and the sound, and the sound coming through it.

"Twenty-nine years," Tob said, "and it's the same."

The camera traps began to fire. The laptop clicked every time a frame came up the cable, and Isla watched the images build on the screen in the dark. The reef floor, grainy and pale, the two coral heads exactly as they had been four nights before, the polyps open, and between them the grate, and standing on the grate the circle of blue-green light, pulsing. Forty-eight times a minute. She put her hand flat on the rock and felt a faint vibration come up out of it, up through the stone and into her palm, and she took her hand off the rock and put it back and felt it again.

"Pull," she said. "You can feel the pull through the rock."

"I felt it the first time," Tob said. "That's the part nobody would believe."

They lay there for four hours. The flood built and peaked at a quarter to eleven, and the flat hummed, and the pulse never varied by so much as a beat, and the light on the reef floor never flickered, and at half past one the pump stopped. They heard it stop: the click, then nothing, and then a long, low, sighing note as the stolen water came back. The basin filled and turned, and the eddies that belonged to the reef came back all at once, whirlpools three metres across, and the flat was smacked by a wave that came out of nowhere and soaked them both to the chest.

The water climbed the rock and caught Tob's thighs and pulled at him like a hand, and he stood in it, and then he stepped up onto the bare stone and said, "Waist deep and no more," once, quietly, to the water.

Then the flow let go, and they packed the gear in the dark and walked in over the flat with the tide at their heels and the lamp off so as not to be seen from the water.

The log line took her a long time to write.

First watch. Pulse 0.8 Hz from 21:00, ends 01:30. Grate light steady, panel confirmed, temperature at basin edge plus 0.4 C above ambient. Pump signature recorded on both hydrophones. Vibration felt through rock. Diesel audible below pump. Data consistent with installed, manned, powered structure inside south wall of basin. Reserve status: total no-take. No permit covers this. Logging under protest.

The email went to Voss at eight the next morning, with four images, eleven minutes of audio and a temperature trace attached. He answered at nineteen past eight.

Isla. I have reviewed the material. The audio is consistent with your own hydrophone pole: a pole set in sand in shallow water will move with the swell and produce a periodic thump. The images are too poorly lit to identify anything. The temperature trace is within diurnal variation. I also want to raise an administrative matter. You are operating instruments inside a no-take reserve outside the scope of your permit. The reserve office has been informed. Please do not deploy further equipment there while this is under review.

She read it twice on the flat rock in the morning light, with Tob standing beside her, and then she read it a third time and handed him the screen.

"He calls it diurnal variation," Tob said. "He calls his own reef a thing that swings with the sun."

"He hasn't looked at the audio."

"He has looked at the audio. That's the trouble."

She did not ask him what he meant. She went back to the shore station and did the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was the one thing that could not be talked away. Water is eight hundred times denser than air. Power scales with the cube of velocity, so a flow cut from one point four to nought point six loses fifteen sixteenths of its energy, and a pump that takes fifteen sixteenths of the energy out of a tidal race is a pump with industrial muscle behind it, and industrial muscle needs fuel and maintenance and people, and diesel engines need air and they need exhaust, and air and exhaust have to leave a sealed space.

So there was a second hole somewhere, and she knew where it was, because she had hung in the water beside it six nights before with warm water coming out of it and a mooring eye rusting in it with grey-green paint on it.

That night the pole came out.

The tide turned at twenty past one and came back with all its stored strength, and the flat was swept by a wave that carried the base of the pole off the sand and threw the pole and both hydrophones and the trap at the edge of the drop over the lip and down the slope into deep water. By the time Isla got out there in the morning there was nothing left but one short piece of cable lying across the rock like a dead snake.

She stood on the flat with the cable in her hand and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that did not sound like a man arguing with a chart. Tob came out and looked at the empty sand and the empty lip of the drop, and then walked in and came out again with a hammer, a saw, eight wooden pegs and a heavy plank from his bench, and over the next two days, working always in the shallows with his waist in the water and his feet on the rock, he built her a frame. A low table of planks pegged into the reef floor with wooden stakes, weighted with stones from the beach, the pole bolted through the middle of it, the whole thing low enough that the swell went over the top and left it standing the way dead coral stands.

"It'll hold," he said. "It's built like a boat."

"How do you know?"

"Because I built it like a boat."

She set the four traps again, and the pole again, and on the sixth night she took the dinghy out alone over the head of the canyon and lowered a hydrophone and a temperature logger on a weighted line, and Tob watched from the rock with his arms folded and did not offer to come and did not tell her not to. The canyon was there, in the mouth of the chart, the gap in the soundings that ran out from the basin to the shelf edge, two hundred metres wide on paper and no detail at all inside it. The line went down through water that got colder and then stopped getting colder, and the hydrophone hung in the dark at forty metres and listened.

On the seventh night, the beacon stopped.

It was a quarter past nine and the pulse had been running for fifteen minutes when a second light came up on the reef floor, deeper in the frame, further from the grate, on the south side, at the base of the wall where the sand met the rock. A red lamp, small, the size of a thumbnail, blinking twice and going dark, blinking twice and going dark. Isla had seen it before, deep in a seam in a rock face, from two metres away, with her hand bleeding in the water.

"That's the door," Tob said. "That's the door and somebody's opening it."

The pulse changed. Long, long, short, and then long, long, short, and then it stopped, and the water in the basin went flat and still and the reef went quiet, and the two of them lay on the flat rock in the dark side by side, and neither of them said the word that both of them were thinking, because it was not a word you said out loud on a rock in the dark in the middle of the sea.

The wall opened.

She had walked the length of that wall with a torch and found nothing but grey stone and a lip of new concrete, and now a seam in it widened into a mouth and warm water came off it in a slick that smoothed the ripples for forty metres, and out of the mouth, low and slow and with no light showing above the surface, came a hull.

It was grey-black and looked black, long and low, with a hump amidships like the hump of a wheelhouse, and no name on it and no flag and no lights above the waterline, and it came out of the inside of the reef with the confidence of a thing that had come out of there many times before. Water ran off the hump in a sheet. The warm water of its passage went over Isla's hands where they lay on the rock, warm as a bath, in the middle of a cold sea at night.

It crossed the basin at a walking pace, and it passed within thirty metres of the flat, and neither of them breathed.

Then it turned south, and the hump of its wake went down the length of the basin in a long slow line, and at eleven minutes past one in the morning, out past the edge of the flat, over the head of the canyon, the water bulged once and settled, and the line of the wake broke into a circle, and the hydrophone on the canyon line took the sound down into her headphones and into her ears. The note of a screw, and under it the note of a motor, and then both notes going down and down in pitch as the thing went deeper into the rock throat and the water closed over it.

She watched the depth trace on the screen and read the numbers out loud in the dark without meaning to.

"Forty. Sixty. Ninety. One hundred and twenty."

The chart said the canyon had a floor at eighty metres. The numbers went on going down, one hundred and fifty, one hundred and eighty, and then the sound thinned out and the note was gone and there was nothing in her ears but the sea again, the shrimp, the fish, the hiss of the channel.

The mouth in the south wall closed. The warm slick drifted away across the basin and thinned and vanished. The grate light came back, steady, blue-green, pulsing, forty-eight beats a minute, and the pump started up, and the water in the basin lay flat and stayed flat.

Tob sat up on the rock. He picked up the mallet, which he had carried out with him for no reason either of them could name, and put it down again. He looked at the basin for a long time.

"Twenty-nine years," he said. "I thought it went out to the shelf."

"It did. It went down through the canyon and out to the shelf, and it will come back the same way before morning, and no one will see it because no one looks at water."

"It's a boat, then."

"It's a boat."

"It has a crew."

"It has a crew."

"And they've got a garage cut into a reef inside a reserve where nobody's allowed to take a shell," Tob said. "And they've got a door that opens and a pump that pulls the tide out of the basin so the door can be used, and they've been doing it a long time, and nobody has ever noticed."

He stood up. He looked south, past the flat, past the head of the canyon, out to where the shelf dropped away into open sea, three miles off, black and empty. Then a green light showed out there, low on the water, once. It did not blink. It came up, held for the length of two breaths, and went out.

"You see that," Tob said.

"I saw it."

"That's not a star."

"No."

"It's an answer."

Isla sat with the log open on the rock in the dark and wrote the last line of the first watch in the flat data hand she had used for three months on this shelf, the hand that had never once written a thing she did not have, in numbers or in images or in her own damaged ears.

0146. Structure is manned. Submersible left the wall at the south basin, crossed basin, entered the canyon. Tracked by hydrophone to 180 m; chart shows 80 m. Vessel is met on the shelf. Reserve is compromised. Log the permit, the email, the time, and the green light. Someone is home.

She closed the log. The pump beat on in the dark, forty-eight times a minute, and the basin lay flat and quiet as a paved yard, and she and Tob walked in over the flat rock ahead of the tide with the mallet and the laptop and four memory cards, and behind them the reef went on doing what it had been doing for twenty-nine years, which was keeping a secret in plain water.
