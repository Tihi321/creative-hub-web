The Mentor’s Betrayal

The phone was still warm in her hand when she put it down, and the room was very quiet, and the fan turned, and outside the window the tide was making against the flat with the long hiss she had heard every night for three months.

Tell me exactly what you have in your possession.

Nine minutes. Four seconds.

She had sent him the frames of the skid and the photograph of the fragment and the mesh and the tag, and the line of text that said not sensor error, and he had answered before she had put the kettle on, and he had asked three questions and none of them had been where.

She took the log off the desk and opened it at the back, where the margins were, and wrote the time of the email and the time of the reply and the time of the call, and under them, in the flat data hand, the three questions.

Is it alive. Have you told anyone else.

Then she went outside and stood on the flat in the dark and looked at the water, and the blue-green light was still going in the basin, two and then one, two and then one, patient as a clock, and she thought about what kind of man asks whether a sponge is alive before he asks where the sponge was taken from.

The mail boat came on the Thursday, which gave her four days, and she spent the first of them in the archive at the shelf station, because the archive was the one thing she had not yet read properly and the one thing that could still surprise her.

It was a cupboard and a laptop and a scanner that worked when it felt like it. The shelf station's records went back to the cable route surveys of the early nineties and a little further, and most of it was grey and dull and exactly what it claimed to be: permit applications, inspection logs, three decades of patrol reports with the same four sentences in them, weather sheets, tide tables, a run of the recruitment paper's own correspondence from 2001 when Voss had been running settlement tiles in the basin and the university had been paying for it.

She read the twelve most recent patrol reports in the cupboard first. They were a form with boxes and a hand that changed with the officer, and every one of them had the same route on it. The northern gat, the eastern edge, the western anchorage, the run home. Not one of them went into the southern basin. Not once in twelve reports. She went back another year and read twelve more and then twelve more, and the southern basin did not appear in a patrol route anywhere in the file. She sat with that for a while and then wrote it down, because it was the first thing in four days that made the whole shape of it click.

A reserve that is patrolled everywhere except one bay stays quiet everywhere but that one bay. A pump does not need a wall. A pump needs a route no one walks.

Then she went looking for the paper.

It was in a grey folder at the back of the cupboard with the rest of the shelf's publication correspondence, and it was a hard copy, which was lucky, because the online journal's site had been through two redesigns and the supplements were gone. Voss had written it in 2001, on larval retention in the southern shelf, and Isla had cited it twice in her doctoral chapter and had opened one of her own teaching lectures with it, and she read it now the way she read a survey, for the numbers.

It was a good paper. It was better than she had remembered. The model was careful, the field data was dense for a basin of that size, and the recruitment argument was sealed with a single set of settlement tiles recovered over twenty-one months, tiles that had been laid flat on the reef floor at four sites and read for recruits. She found the methods section and read it twice, and then she found the site table, and there it was.

Four sites. Three of them on the shelf outside the basin mouth. The fourth on the reef floor inside the basin, on the sand between the two coral heads, at the head of the southern slope, from the first week of September 1996 to the last week of May 1998.

Twenty-one months of tiles. In the basin. On the sand between the two coral heads.

She sat with that for a long minute. She knew that patch of sand now. She had lain over it with a torch and watched a film of it crawl across a grate. She knew what the flow did there when the arrays were running, and she knew what the tiles would have collected while they were sitting on it with the pump pulled for a transect every night from nine until half past one.

She got the folder and went back through the correspondence.

It took her an hour to find the line, and when she found it she read it out loud in the empty room, once, because it had to be said.

It was a letter from the shelf's own station, from a previous officer, dated the twenty-second of March 1997, to the university's field office, and it said, in a paragraph in the middle of a page of tide and mooring matters: I would also note, for the record, that the station has no vessels available for the southern basin patrol on the nights of the 14th to the 28th of each month, as the duty hulls are chartered to Dr Voss's project for its basin transects. Please ensure your own schedules do not rely on our launch on those dates.

The nights of the fourteenth to the twenty-eighth. Every month. For the two years they had been running the tiles.

She copied the line into the log with the date and the file reference and the officer's name, and under it she wrote three sentences in the flat data hand, and they were the only three sentences she needed.

The basin was closed to patrol for the transect nights. The transect was the paper. Therefore the paper is the reason the patrol came nowhere near the grate for two years. Check the tile depth against the reef floor; check the tile dates against the array installation.

She got the tide table for 1996 and 1997 out of the cupboard and then she went and stood outside for a moment with the wind on her face and did the arithmetic in her head, and it held. The tiles had been laid in September 1996. The station's previous officer logged the closed patrol nights from the following March. The arrays were installed, by Voss's own account, in 1996.

The paper had not been written from a basin that happened to be quiet. The paper had been written from a basin that had been made quiet, by the man who wrote the paper, and every citation behind it had been standing on that quietness for twenty-five years.

She did not call him. She did not send him anything. She put the folder back in the cupboard and locked the cupboard and went out to Tob's frame and sat on it with the log on her knee and made herself breathe, because the anger was not useful yet and she knew from experience that a thing had to be written before it could be used.

She wrote it that evening, in the flat data hand.

Archive, shelf station. Patrol files, twelve reports, one year, no basin entry; same across three years. Publication file, Voss 2001. Methods: settlement tiles, four sites, three outside the basin, one inside, on the sand between the coral heads, Sept 1996-May 1998. Station letter, 22 March 1997, previous duty officer: basin closed to patrol on the 14th-28th of each month, duty hulls chartered to Voss's basin transects. Arrays installed 1996. Tiles pulled 1998. Paper 2001. The patrol gap and the tile period and the array period are the same gap. The paper is not about the reef. The paper is about the pump. Voss did not only maintain the arrays. Voss used them, and filed the result, and the field has been citing it ever since.

She read it back and it was enough to take to the man, and it was also, she understood, exactly the thing he had been keeping back from her.

The mail boat came on the Thursday, which gave her three days.

She spent them doing the thing she did best and the thing that made her useless company, which was putting numbers in order. She did not call Voss back. She did not want to be on the phone with him, where he could hear the room and the tide behind her and work out which building she was standing in; a man who asked three questions in four seconds was a man who listened for the edges. She wrote him an email instead, plain, one paragraph, saying she had material in custody at the shelf station, that she had not spoken to anyone, and that she would bring it to the mainland on the Thursday boat and wanted an independent analyst present when the seals were broken.

He replied in eleven minutes. He said he would meet her at the harbour. He said not to open the jars on the boat. He said, in the passive voice he used for things he did not want to have said, that it would be preferable if the material were not discussed with the crew.

She read that line four times and then wrote it in the log with the time beside it.

Voss: 'it would be preferable if the material were not discussed with the crew.' Object of the sentence: the material. Agent: omitted.

The mail boat was eleven metres of steel with a wheelhouse you could stand up in and a bench along the rail where the crates went, and it ran the forty-one miles from the shelf to the mainland in three hours on a good day and never in less, because the channel between the two outer islands had a tidal race in it you did not argue with. She sat on the bench with her kit bag between her feet and her hand on the strap and she did not talk to the crew, which was easy, because the crew were two brothers from the village who had known Tob Marek since they were boys and who spent the crossing telling her about the weather and asking her, twice, whether the old man was eating.

She watched the wake and rehearsed the thing she would not say first.

The harbour at the mainland was a stone quay with a crane on it and a shed with a corrugated roof, and the boat came in against the wall at ten past two in the afternoon and she stepped off with her kit bag and her cooler over her shoulder and there he was.

Voss was sixty-one and looked fifty, small and neat in a canvas jacket that had been expensive once, with the grey hair cut short and the glasses on a cord, and he smiled the way he always smiled, which was pleasant and slightly absent, the smile of a man who had already had the useful part of the conversation in his head and was waiting for you to finish it.

"Isla," he said. "You look tired."

"I am tired."

"Let me take that." He put his hand out for the cooler.

She did not give it to him. "Analyst first," she said. "You said you'd have someone."

"In the shed," he said, and turned, and walked, and she walked a step and a half behind him with the cooler on her shoulder and her eye on the back of his neck.

The analyst was a woman in her thirties with a laptop and a chain-of-custody form and a lanyard that said she worked for a contract lab on the mainland, and she was competent and quick and she took the seven jars and logged them and sealed them and photographed the seals, and Isla signed and the analyst signed and Voss signed last, and when it was done the analyst put the jars in a case and the case in the boot of her car and drove away north.

Which left the two of them standing in the shed with the crane chains ticking in the wind, and the smell of rope and old diesel, and the door open onto the quay.

"You've done this properly," Voss said.

"I'm a witness."

"Yes." He looked at the open door and then at her, and the pleasant smile was still there and had come unhooked at one corner. "Walk with me."

They walked the quay to the end, where the wall was green and the swells came up and slapped it and ran back.

"When you wrote the first time," he said, "about the current going soft in the southern basin, I told you it was sensor error."

"You did."

"I did that because I did not want you in the southern basin."

"I know," Isla said. "I worked that out on the second night."

"Yes," he said. "I'm sorry it took me this long to say it."

"So say the rest."

And he looked out at the water, at the three miles of grey between the quay and the first headland, and he put his hands in his jacket pockets, and he said the sentence she had been walking toward for four days.

"I designed the dampening system," he said. "The field in the south basin. The reason your meter came back wrong. That was me."

The swell came up and slapped the wall and ran back, and a gull went over, and Isla did not say anything, because she had learned in nine years of supervising students and three months of supervising herself that the useful thing after a sentence like that is silence.

"It isn't a dam," Voss said. "There are three arrays of low-profile cowlings set into the rock on the south wall and the canyon lip, each with a baffled outlet. They bleed a controlled volume of water out of the main race and put it back where I want it. The result is that for a window of about four and a half hours on the flood and the ebb you can flatten the flow in the basin by sixty per cent while the water outside the canyon still runs at one point four."

"Sixty per cent," she said.

"Sixty-three on the flood." He said it precisely, the way she would have said it, and she understood with a cold drop in the middle of her chest that he had designed it well, and that he was proud of it, and that the pride had outlived the reason.

"How long."

"Twenty-nine years," he said. "The first array went in with the base, in ninety-six. The third array I fitted in two thousand and eleven."

"So you didn't just design it," she said. "You went back."

He looked at the water.

"I go back once a year," he said. "Under the survey permit. To check the geometry. If an array goes out of true the flow in the basin comes back and the moorings take the load, and if the moorings take the load they fail, and if they fail a hundred and eighty metres of canyon comes down on the base."

"On the base," she said. "You mean on the people in it."

"Yes."

She put her hands on the wet stone of the wall, and they were steady, which was interesting, because the rest of her was not. She had come off the boat planning to say one thing, and it was not going to be that thing, not yet, because a man laying his cards down was a man still holding the deck.

"There's a name missing from that," she said. "You've been talking for ninety seconds and you have not said who you are working for."

And Voss took his hands out of his pockets and folded his arms, and for the first time he looked old.

"His name is Sable," he said. "Warden Sable. He runs the operation, and he has run it since before ninety-six, and he came to me in ninety-five, when I was thirty, with a proposal and a problem and a very good survey of the shelf. The problem was the current in the southern basin. The proposal was a research contract. I was thirty and I had published nothing anyone had read and I had a loan and a marriage that was already over, and I said yes."

"Does the sentence end there?"

"No," he said. "The sentence is twenty-nine years long and every year it has cost a little more to stop."

"That's not coercion," Isla said. "That's a choice you kept making."

"No," he said. "The coercion is different. The coercion is on a shelf in the back of that cupboard over there." He tipped his head at the shed behind them. "You have just spent four days reading it, and I would like you to tell me what you found, because I have found it myself about once a year for a quarter of a century and I would like to hear it in someone else's voice."

Isla looked at him for a moment.

"Two thousand and one," she said. "Larval retention. Settlement tiles in the southern basin over twenty-one months, September ninety-six to May ninety-eight. Four sites. One of them inside the basin, on the sand between the coral heads, which is the patch of sand I have been filming a grate through for a fortnight."

"Yes."

"And in the station archive there is a letter from the previous duty officer dated March ninety-seven that says the basin was closed to patrol on the fourteenth to the twenty-eighth of every month because the duty hulls were chartered to your project's transects."

He did not move.

"The patrol gap and the tile period and the array period are the same gap," she said. "So the paper is not a paper about the reef. The paper is a paper about a basin that had been made flat by a pump you fitted, running every night of the tiles' lives, and you wrote it as if the flatness were the sea, and it has been in print since, and everything citing it is wrong behind it, and that is the thing that keeps you in the chair and not on the next boat out. That's what you were going to keep back from me. I found it myself in your own archive in a cupboard, and I want you to know that before you tell me the next thing, because the next thing is the only reason I sat on a mail boat for three hours to hear it."

Voss let the swallow come and go over the wall and did not answer for a long moment.

"Who else knows," Isla said. "And where is the money going to."

"Sable's buyers are pharmaceutical contract houses on two continents," he said. "I have never met one. I have never wanted to. And who else knows is a shorter list than you would think: Sable, his crew, and the yard that builds his gear. And me. And now you."

"And the light," she said. "The beacon. The bioluminescent signal on the grate."

"That was not my design and I did not know it was there," Voss said, and for the first time in a quarter of an hour his voice moved off its careful line. "I found out what it was from your own photographs. The pump I knew. The lure I did not. He added it in two thousand and four, and he did not tell me, and when I say it I want you to understand exactly what I mean, which is that the man who claims he is holding my research over me is keeping his own architecture back from his own engineer, and that the day I stop being useful is a day I do not want to be standing in a canyon."

There was a silence on the quay.

Then Isla said, "Tob Marek."

Voss did not blink, which was itself an answer.

"Tob Marek surveyed the cable route in ninety-four," she said. "He saw the wall and the seam and the light and the grate, and he went back three times, and he never told anyone. And in ninety-four a man died on that survey. And the chart everybody carries has smooth contour lines through a gap in the soundings, and the gap is the canyon, and the canyon holds the base. Somebody drew those lines."

"The survey was ninety-four, not seventy-four," Voss said. "Whatever Tob Marek remembers, that is when he remembers it, and I would like you to be careful with the date, because the date is the whole of it. In ninety-four I was twenty-eight and I was not on that survey. I was given the soundings afterwards, and I was given them by the man who ran the survey, and that man was Sable's surveyor, and those lines were drawn before I ever saw the sheet. I did not draw them. I inherited them. And when I saw the gap and understood what it was for, I did the thing I have done every year since, which is that I did nothing."

Isla stood with the wind on her face and looked at him.

"You want me to believe you're the second-worst man in this," she said. "That's what this is. You want me to take the coercion and hold it up and find it lighter than the alternative."

"Yes," Voss said. "That is exactly what I want."

"And what do you want me to do."

He said the next thing simply, and it was the first thing he had said since she stepped off the boat that she believed all the way down.

"I want to be arrested," he said. "Not tomorrow, and not by a patrol boat that will phone ahead, and not by email. I want the evidence to be real, and I want the chain of custody to be clean, and I want my name on the statement, because the only thing I have that Sable does not is that he needs me to keep the water flat, and I am going to stop doing it. If I am the reason the canyon is quiet, then I am the only lever you have, and I want it applied properly and once."

The swell came up and slapped the wall and ran back.

"It doesn't work," Isla said.

"What."

"Your lever. I've seen the arrays from inside the basin and I've seen your numbers, and you said it yourself: if an array goes out of true, the current comes back, and if the current comes back, a hundred and eighty metres of canyon comes down on the base and the moorings fail."

"Yes."

"Then if you stop being useful, you don't arrest him. You kill whoever is in there, and you destroy the only evidence that was ever on the seafloor, and you give Sable a wreck to blame on a natural event, and you go to prison for it. That's not a lever. That's a confession with a body count attached."

Voss was silent for a long moment.

"Then what do you propose," he said.

"I propose you do nothing at all for now," Isla said, "and you give me the geometry."

"The geometry is on a drive in my study."

"Then that is where we are going," she said, "because I am not going to stand on a quay and be told that the man who has been lying to me for three months is the only one who knows how the water moves, and then walk away and let him keep the map."

He drove her to his flat in the town, and the flat was on the second floor and faced the wrong way, nothing but roofs and a car park, and she sat at his kitchen table and watched him bring up the drawings, and for three hours she read the water off his screens.

It was good work. That was the part that made her angriest and kept her quiet, because it was better than anything she had ever seen from him in a paper: three arrays, each baffled, each with an intake tuned to the local cross-section, each with an outlet that dumped its water tangentially into the main race so that it did not fight the flow but added to it, and behind it all a control philosophy laid out like a diagram of a lung.

She copied every file onto her own drive with the timestamps intact, and she made him initial a printout of each drawing with the date and his name, and then she sat back and looked at the last page, which was the mooring plan.

Four anchors. Two on the canyon wall, two on the lip. A floating spine slung between them at eleven metres, and the base hanging under the spine on short tethers, tuned to sit in the slack water the arrays made, with a margin on each tether that was, she saw, about the width of her thumb.

"Tether capacity," she said.

"Eighty tonnes each," Voss said. "Margin of roughly twelve."

"Twelve tonnes per tether at the design flow."

"At the design flow."

"And if the arrays stop," she said, "the flow in the canyon goes to two point four on the spring flood, and the load on those tethers goes to—"

"About ninety," Voss said. "On one anchor. That is why it has never been tested."

She sat with her pen over the page and let the whole shape sit in her head, and it was the first time since the boat that she had felt like herself.

The arrays did not just hide the base. They held it up. The man had built a thing that was hiding the sea from itself and holding a building over his own head on four threads, and the collar of the whole design was one man's hand on one panel, and it was exactly the kind of clever, brittle, twenty-nine-year-old mistake that a young scientist with thirty years of well-chosen numbers in front of her was going to be able to read.

Which meant she could not use the lever.

But she had measured the reef.

She had three months of current data off the shelf station and four nights of footage and a spreadsheet she had built herself to keep the tidbits straight, and she had the tide table for the shelf, and she had the spring tides in it marked in red because the coral spawn was near, and she had, sitting on the drive in her pocket, the geometry of a hole in the rock where the water had been held flat for twenty-nine years by a man who was now going to help her take that flatness apart.

She stood up.

"Keep the arrays running," she said. "Do not touch them, do not go and inspect them, do not send anyone. If Sable's people ask, everything is normal."

"And?"

"And on the twenty-second of next month there is a spring tide on this shelf that runs the south channel at about three metres per second," Isla said. "Your design flow is two point four. I have the profile and I have the table, and the gat below the base is a narrow hole with a vertical wall on the west side and a bar at the mouth, and I would like you to look at it with me, because you built the thing that hangs inside it, and I did not, and I want to know if I am reading it right."

For a moment he did not move. Then he came around the table and leaned over the screen with his hands on the wood, and they looked at the canyon together the way she had looked at the basin from the rock, and that was the last hour they ever spent as teacher and student.

When she left he stood in the doorway with the light behind him and said one more thing.

"Isla. If this works, I lose everything. If it doesn't, I lose everything and you lose the reef."

"Yes," she said.

"You don't have to sound pleased about it."

"I'm not pleased," she said. "I'm prepared."

She walked down the stairs with the drive in her pocket and the printouts under her arm, and at the bottom she stopped and looked back up the stairwell, and he was still in the doorway, small and neat and bent at the shoulder, and he had the pleasant absent smile off his face at last, and he looked like a man who had just found out what his twenty-nine years had come to.

The mail boat did not go back until the morning.

She slept two hours in a room above the harbour and was awake before the gulls and walked the quay at first light with her kit bag on her shoulder and the drive zipped inside her jacket, and she stood at the end of the wall where she had stood the day before with the swell coming up and slapping the green stone and running back, and she did one more thing with the morning.

She took the printout of the mooring plan out of her bag and looked at the anchors and the tethers and the numbers Voss had initialled, and then she took the tide table out of the plastic sleeve and lay it against the mooring plan on the wet stone, and she read the two of them together the way she had read the paper against the patrol reports, and the answer came out the same as it had on the kitchen table and the same as it had in her own log, which was that the water in that canyon was being held off the rock by a man's hand and four threads, and the threads had a margin of twelve tonnes on them, and on the twenty-second of the month there was a tide that would put ninety on one of them.

She wrote the last line of the visit in the log on the bench in the shed, in the flat data hand, and it was short, and every word of it was a fact.

Spring tide 22nd. Tidal race through the gat approx. 3 m/s against design 2.4. Tether load on the seaward lip anchor goes to approx. 90 t against approx. 80 t capacity. The arrays are holding the base up. Therefore the tide is the only thing on this shelf that can open it, and the geometry is the map, and Voss's drawings are the key to the map. Tob Marek is the only man left on the shelf who has put a hand on the bar. Mail boat back today.

She put the drive in the inside pocket and zipped it, and she sat in the dark of the shed with the wind in the chains above her and the water slapping the wall outside and the first light coming up the harbour, and she thought about a man in a boat shed who had spent twenty-nine years looking at a wall and never telling anyone, and about an old man on a quay who had spent twenty-nine years holding a building up with numbers, and about which of them she was about to ask to go back in the water.

She already knew the answer, and the answer was both of them, and one of them was going to refuse.
