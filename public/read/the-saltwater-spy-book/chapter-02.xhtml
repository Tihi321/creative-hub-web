The Silent Cave

The tide table was the first thing Isla checked that evening, and it was the tide table that told her she was not chasing a ghost.

High water at the northern gat: 22:47. The flood would be pushing from a little before nine, gathering strength through the night, the strongest flow of the whole day arriving in the same hours the profiler said the water had stopped moving. She sat in the shore station with the sheet of paper under the lamp and worked it through the way she had worked through every problem on the Cobalt Shelf for three months. Water does not stop. Water is pushed, and then it goes. If the flow in the southern basin fell from one and four tenths to six tenths while the tide was making, the water was going somewhere else, and something else was making it go. And if the flood moved the most water at the hour the anomaly fell, then the anomaly was there in the tide, hidden in it, wearing its clothes.

She wrote it in the field log in her flat data hand. Southern basin. Flow reduction coincides with building flood. Anomaly is not tidal. Anomaly overrides tidal. Source is powered.

Then she packed. The list was on the desk, and she worked down it the way her dive instructor had taught her in her first year, when she had still believed that lists were for other people. Two hydrophones and the tripod frame. The underwater camera trap, with new batteries and a clean card and the housing sealed and greased. Two dive cylinders, one for the dive and one filled in case. A cutting tool, a spare mask, a hood, gloves with the fingertips cut away because she could not work the clips with the fingers covered. A torch, and a second torch, which was the old one that flickered, because the good one had flooded in February and there was no dive shop on the Cobalt Shelf, only a man in the village who sold bait and batteries and opinions. Last, the surface line: thirty metres of six-millimetre line on a plastic reel, tied to the dinghy, paid out as she swam, the line that would bring her home.

She filled in the dive plan on the standard sheet, coordinates and time down and time up and depth, and then she looked at the last box, which asked for the name of the buddy. She left it empty. She put the sheet on the desk and weighed the corner down with the log book, so that if anyone came into the building before six in the morning they would know where to look. Nobody would come into the building before six in the morning. She put the sheet there anyway.

The outboard started on the third pull, as it always did. The first two pulls were, she had decided some weeks ago, a formality.

There was no moon. The reef was a darker line drawn on the dark water, and the channel was a long hiss, the sound the current made as it came through the gap between the islands and hurried out over the shelf. Isla ran the dinghy slowly, standing at the tiller with her knees bent, watching the water in front of the bow for the pale rings that meant shallow coral and a ruined propeller. The wind was from the north-east, about four knots, cold on her cheek, and the air smelled of salt and of the reef itself, that low animal smell of a place where a great many small things are eating each other very slowly.

She anchored on the sand patch at the eastern edge of the basin, in eleven metres of water, and paid out the anchor line until the dinghy sat easy with the reef to the west and the deep blue of the shelf to the east. She turned the radio on and left it on channel sixteen with the volume up, which was a way of pretending there was somebody listening. Then she lowered the tripod frame over the side on its own line, hand over hand, and set it down on the reef floor six metres from the place where the light had been, angled so the camera trap looked sideways across the sand rather than down into it. She did not want her instruments on the doorstep. She wanted them across the road, where they could watch without being noticed, which meant she was already thinking about the signal as a house.

She put on the headphones and sat still.

The reef at night is not quiet. It is a busy, crackling, gossipy place, and anyone who says the sea is silent has never listened to it with a microphone in the water. There was the shrimp noise, the endless dry crackle of snapping shrimp, like fat in a pan, like rain on a tin roof. There were the grunts and knocks of fish talking to each other in a language she could hear and not understand. There was the tide itself, a low wide rushing under the boat, and her own heartbeat, and the small tick of the hull as the cold gathered in it.

At nine o'clock exactly, the water changed.

It began as a click, thin as a fingernail on glass, and then the pulse came. Slow, measured, impossibly regular. She counted it against the second hand of her watch and got forty-eight in a minute. Eight tenths of a hertz, the same number the shore logger had given her, four nights running, on a laptop in a concrete room. In the water, in the dark, at a hundred metres from the dinghy, it was not a number at all. It was a pump. It was the sound a big positive displacement machine makes when it is working slowly and with enormous strength, the sound of something moving a great deal of water a small distance, again and again, forty-eight times a minute.

And at the same moment, the reef went quiet.

Not silent. Quieter. The hiss of the channel thinned out and stopped, the way the wind drops when a door is closed somewhere else in a house. The eddies at the constriction, the small churn that had been running ever since she had arrived on the Cobalt Shelf, went away. The anchor line, which had been bar-tight, sagged. The dinghy swung on it, drifting slowly, and then sat still on water that had stopped going anywhere.

Isla sat with the headphones on and looked at the slack line and understood the shape of it. The tide was making. The whole weight of the ocean was leaning against the northern gat, and in the southern basin the water had gone calm as a swimming pool, because somebody was pulling the water out and putting it somewhere else.

She wrote the time and the pulse rate in the log and then she dived.

The cold took her at the surface, the way it always did, a tight band around the ribs that lasted for the first minute and then let go. She went down the anchor line with the torch off, because the water was black and there was no point in switching on a light before she needed it, and she went slowly, equalising gently, the bubbles running up past her face in a stream of small silver rings.

At six metres the water was dark green. At nine metres it was the colour of ink, and she switched on the torch.

The beam found the sand, and the sand was clean, pale, ribbed with the marks of a current that should have been moving and was not. It found the two coral heads she had photographed, sitting exactly as they had sat in the frames, their polyps open, their colours flat and brown in the torchlight. It found the small creatures of the reef floor going about their night business: a cleaner shrimp working at a grouper's gill, a hermit crab in a shell with a wart of weed on it, a flatfish that lifted one eye and considered her and then became a patch of sand again.

The light was not a glow on the sand.

It was a lens.

She came in closer, and the beam picked out a circle of glass the size of her fist, set flush into a flat grey surface. The glass was dark, and behind it something glowed, slow and blue-green, pulsing at the same patient forty-eight beats a minute. There was a rubber seal around the glass. There was a countersunk screw, and beside it another, and another, running in a neat line along the edge of the panel.

The panel was a grate. Two metres by three, six centimetres of gap between the bars, the bars rounded and scoured bright at the edges where water had been running over them for a long time. And the water was running. A thin film of sand was crawling across the bars, grains tumbling and then flicking through and down, and a small starfish that had made the mistake of sitting on the panel was sliding too, one arm at a time, in no particular hurry, towards the gaps.

Around the panel, on the rock, there was nothing growing.

Isla hung in the water and looked at that and felt the cold go through her, and not from the sea. On the Cobalt Shelf, every hard surface within reach of daylight was furred with life within weeks. She had watched a stainless steel bolt, one bolt, put in place for an instrument mount, become a garden in twenty-six days. This rock, in this place, with all this light and food and water moving past it, was bare. Not scraped clean once. Kept clean. Somebody had been scrubbing, or painting, or both, and the growth around the panel's edges grew right up to a border as straight as a ruler.

She reached out and put her gloved hand flat on the rock beside the grate, and the water pushed her hand sideways, gently, and she looked at where it was pushing her and let go of the rock.

Then the pull took her.

It was not a current. It was a tug, a steady and increasing one, from the middle, the way you feel when a big vehicle passes you in a street. She drifted half a metre, one and a half, her fins swinging out behind her, and the sand on the reef floor lifted and floated past her in a slow blizzard, going to the grate, all of it going to the grate, and she put her hand back on the coral head and held it while she thought.

She had read that the intake screens on a desalination plant are built so that water comes through them slowly, so that fish are not pinned against the mesh and killed. This was faster than that, and there was no mesh at all, only bars, and the fish around the basin were not being pinned because the fish around the basin had left. She saw one now, a young grouper, hanging on the reef forty metres away with its face into the flow and its tail working hard, holding its position in the only piece of moving water left in the basin. It was doing what everything with a brain does when it finds a bad current. It was standing still and waiting for it to stop.

Water is eight hundred times denser than air. That was the number she had used in every model she had ever built, and down here it was not a number, it was a hand laid flat on her back. A flow of fifteen centimetres a second across six square metres of screen is not a lot of water moving; it is a great deal of force, applied steadily, and the force goes on and on.

Isla got out of there the way she had been taught to get out of a rip. Not against the pull. Sideways, and low, close to the rock where the flow was slower, using her hands. She went across the reef floor like a crab, three metres, five, and the tug faded, and then she was out of it and her heart was going hard and her breath was loud in her ears.

She stayed there a moment on the bottom, holding the surface line, breathing. Her left hand was stinging. She looked at it in the torchlight: the glove was cut, a neat slice across the palm, and blood was coming out of it in a slow red cloud that started, as blood does, to travel sideways towards the grate. She closed her fist and held it and watched the red ribbons go, and thought, with the kind of flat calm that comes when you have been frightened and come out of it, that this was exactly the problem with the Cobalt Shelf. Everything here went the way the water went.

The water could not go nowhere. That was the thing that pulled her forward when she might have gone up. Whatever the pump moved had to come out of the other end, somewhere, and the other end would be a jet, and a jet leaves a scar in the water that a person with the right eyes can read from a long way off.

She turned and swam west, along the base of the rock wall.

She found the wall in nine minutes, and she nearly swam straight past it.

It was below the visible reef, on the southern side of the basin, where the sand stopped and the rock dropped away into a slope of rubble and old coral. She followed the slope down to eleven metres and then twelve, deeper than the anchor, further than she had planned, and there the torchlight found a face of rock that ran from the sand up into the dark, ten metres of it, smooth and bare, with the flat grey look of something that had been poured instead of grown.

She stopped. She hung there in the water and looked at ten metres of nothing, which on this reef was the strangest thing she had ever seen.

Eighty metres away, the reef wall was furred and ruffled, sponges and soft corals and weed and the little bright specks of things that live on things, all of it swaying in the flow. Here there was grey, and grey, and grey. The growth came in from the sides and stopped at a line as clean as the edge of a page. She ran her torch along that line and found the whole face was trimmed: rock cut back in a straight plane, top to bottom. And where the face met the sand, someone had cut a channel, a shallow gutter the width of her two hands, running along the base of the wall like the drainage under a house.

Halfway along it, she found a mooring eye.

It was iron, or steel, older than she was, and set into the rock with a bar of concrete around it. It had been painted. The paint was the grey-green of a workboat's hull, and the anti-fouling in it had kept the barnacles off, and the paint itself was worn in one place where a rope had been led through the eye, over and over, for years.

Then the water went warm.

It came over the top of her hood, over the back of her neck, and she actually flinched, because a sudden change of temperature in the sea at night is a wrongness you feel in your whole body before you can name it. She looked at her gauge. It told her the temperature had risen by nearly two degrees in the space of a few metres of water. Ahead of her, in the light of the torch, the beam went soft and wavering like the air on a hot road.

She came on, slowly, and found the mouth.

A seam in the rock face, two metres wide and taller than she could see, and inside it, black. Not the black of depth. The black of a covered doorway, of a room. There was a frame around it: a squared-off lip of concrete on either side, and the lip was clean and sharp and new, and a strand of kelp was caught on the corner of it, waving, and around the kelp a couple of small fish were feeding quietly in water that was coming out of the gap, out of the dark, out of the inside of the reef, and travelling down along the base of the wall and away across the basin towards the deep.

The dye trail of it. The outflow.

A red light, low and small, blinked twice deep in the seam and then stayed dark.

Isla hung in the warm water outside the gap and did the arithmetic, because the arithmetic was the only part of this she could hold on to. In, at the grate, at fifteen centimetres a second and more, across six square metres of screen. Out, here, through four square metres of gap in a rock face half a kilometre from anything. All of it inside the reef. All of it through a channel in the middle of a wall that the charts said was solid, thick, unbroken rock.

Very far inside the dark, something with a diesel engine and a metal hull moved, and she felt it in the water before she heard it, a soft thump of pressure against her chest, and then a rumble that came through the bones behind her ears.

She looked at her gauge again and saw her time, and the cold, and the cut hand, and understood that she had been down too long and had done nothing she had planned to do, and that this was the moment when a careful diver turns around and goes home. She turned around. She paid out along the surface line, hand over hand, with the warm water falling away behind her and the cold coming back at her ribs like an argument.

She was halfway back when the tide reached her.

It came as a shove, from the side and above, and then it came as a spin. The pump had stopped, or something had changed, and the flow that had been stolen away from the basin for four hours came rushing back through the constriction with all its stored strength behind it, and the eddies that belonged to the reef formed again in an instant, violent whirlpools three metres across, the reef's own water taking back what had been taken from it. The anchor line went bar-tight and began to saw up and down. Isla was lifted, turned, rolled, and had the good sense not to fight it. She kept her fist around the line. She let the water throw her and she pulled herself along the line through it, hand over cut hand, and the second torch, the old one, was torn off her jacket by an eddy and went spinning away into the dark and she did not go after it, and somewhere behind her in the churn her tripod frame, her two hydrophones, and her camera trap and the four nights of footage on its card went over the edge of the slope and into the canyon.

She came up by the dinghy with her mask half full and her hand bleeding and her legs shaking, and she got a hand on the pontoon on the second try and lay over it like a wet coat.

The reef was roaring again. The hiss of the channel was back, the reef was alive and loud and busy, and the water underneath her was black and moving and none of it was going the way it had been going twenty minutes before.

She lay across the pontoon and laughed, once, quietly, at nothing in particular, at the fact that all of it had been right there, all of it, under nine metres of ordinary water, for a year, for ten years, for as long as the mooring eye had been rusting.

Then she hauled herself in, and started the outboard, and the first two pulls were a formality.

She wrote the log at half past eleven, sitting on the floor of the shore station with a towel round her shoulders and her hand wrapped in a field dressing, because the chair was too far away and she could not be bothered to stand up again.

Southern basin. Grate, 2 x 3 m, six-centimetre bars, scoured, trimmed margins, panel light 0.8 Hz. Intake estimated flow high. Pull felt at 3 m. Outflow seam in south wall, approx. 2 x 4 m, warm water +1.8 C, mooring eye with paint, diesel noise and pressure signature from inside. Structure is built. Structure is manned. Structure is inside the reef.

She sat and read it back. It looked like somebody else's log. It looked like the kind of log entry that gets a person quietly moved to a desk job, and she thought about that, and then she thought about what she had actually seen, and it seemed to her that the wonder of it was bigger than the risk of it. Somebody had cut a hole in a rock wall and hung a pump in the sea and put a lamp on the floor, and the reef had closed over it, and nobody had noticed because nobody looks at water the way water deserves to be looked at.

Then she unfolded the chart.

It was the standard survey sheet, folded in eighths, coffee-stained at the corner, and she laid it flat on the floor and put the log book on one corner and the lamp on the other. The southern basin sat in the middle of it, ringed with contour lines. The contours ran in smooth parallel curves from the northern gat right across the basin, and they did not waver, and they did not kink, and the spot depths between them were spaced two hundred metres apart.

They were too smooth. Contours drawn around real soundings look like contour lines anywhere: they bend, they crowd, they wander, they bulge where the rock bulges. These ran straight, the way lines run when a surveyor has a gap and has drawn through it with a ruler. She got the magnifier out of the drawer and looked at the tiny print along the edge of the sheet, and there it was, in the corner, in letters four millimetres high.

Soundings by lead line, 1994. Southern basin: data incomplete.

A canyon two metres wide, in a basin sounded by a lead line every two hundred metres, would stay off a chart until the end of the world.

She rolled the chart up, and then, because she was cold and awake and not going to sleep, she opened the survey archive on the laptop and pulled down the last piece of detailed work anyone had ever done on that wall. It had taken the satellite link eleven minutes. It was the dive survey from the cable route, twenty-nine years old, scanned badly, the pages grey and the numbers sloping, the names typed into a form at the top of every page.

Surveyor one. Surveyor two.

She read the second name twice. Then she opened the accident report that the same archive had stapled to the back of it, and read it to the end, and sat with it for a while with the towel round her shoulders and the dressing dark on her hand, listening to the reef outside the window moving the way it was supposed to move.

Then she took the pen and wrote at the bottom of the field log, under the last line of data, in letters big enough to read from the doorway.

Tobin Marek.
