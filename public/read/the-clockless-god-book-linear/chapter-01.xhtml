Two Hundred Clocks at Nine

Roux & Fils, Horlogerie, rue des Gravilliers. Monday 23 March, just after nine.

The narrow shop is lined floor to ceiling with clocks: cuckoos, carriage clocks, walnut regulators and a row of cat-shaped kitchen clocks. Not one of them ticks, and every face reads nine.

The brass bell over the door hangs dead, its clapper wedged with a twist of paper.

On screen: Dec (brown leather jacket, satchel, puzzled, neutral), Roux (brown work coat, loupe on its chain, staring at the wall, sad)

DEC (neutral) Monsieur Roux? C'est moi. L'Irlandais. For the gears.

ROUX (sad) It's Sunday.

DEC (worried) It's Monday. Just gone nine. I said Monday, didn't I?

DEC (worried) The escapement wheels, the brass bits. They sent me a list.

ROUX (thinking) Who are you?

DEC (neutral) Dec. Declan Hartley. I was here Thursday.

DEC (neutral) You called me a thief for offering forty euro for a mainspring.

ROUX (neutral) You are a thief.

He says it with relief, as if he has found one thing where he left it. Then the relief goes.

ROUX (sad) I closed on Saturday. I went to bed. I came down to wind them, and they are all at nine.

The regulators, the carriage clocks, the ship's clock and the cheap plastic one over the workshop door all say nine.

DEC (worried) Right. Okay. That's a lot of nine.

ROUX (scared) Is it Sunday?

DEC (sad) It's Monday the twenty-third of March. Have you had anything? A drink of water?

ROUX (angry) I had my coffee. Everything tastes of cloves.

ROUX (angry) Cloves and slate. Like licking a church roof.

He puts out his tongue. It is a dull, smeared grey, like pencil lead.

Dec fetches water from the workshop tap and waits while the old man drinks.

ROUX (angry) Somebody has been in here. They have touched my clocks. And it is Sunday.

DEC (neutral) Monday.

ROUX (angry) Don't correct me, thief.

DEC (happy) That's more like it.

Dec opens the trunk of the oak longcase by the counter and shines his phone torch inside.

A sliver of brass no bigger than a matchstick is pushed between the crutch and the pendulum rod, holding it off to one side.

On the bare oak inside the door, someone has drawn in neat white chalk a circle with twelve little marks, and at the top, where twelve should be, IX.

DEC (scared) Monsieur Roux, did you draw something in your clock?

ROUX (angry) I don't draw. I mend.

DEC (worried) Lovely. Just checking.

He photographs the chalk without touching it and shuts the little door with two fingers.

On his way to the workshop he reaches up and plucks the twist of paper out of the bell's clapper. He can't leave a bell like that.

The back workshop of Roux & Fils. Nearly half past nine.

A long bench runs under a strip light, with trays of springs and wheels laid out like jewels. At the far end is the door to the courtyard.

In it sits a cheap brass-plated cylinder lock on fresh, bright screws. Fine scratches run into the keyway like the tracks of tiny skis.

On screen: Dec (crouching by the door, thinking), Roux (in the workshop doorway, glass in hand, angry)

DEC (thinking) Ah, now. That's not right at all.

ROUX (angry) What? What's not right?

DEC (neutral) When did you put a new lock on your back door?

ROUX (angry) I didn't! That door has my father's lock!

DEC (surprised) Well, it hasn't now. It has a thing out of a blister pack, and somebody's been at it.

DEC (thinking) These scratches are a pick, and a bad one. Raked in and out like leaves.

DEC (thinking) A professional leaves you nothing.

DEC (thinking) But it's locked from inside, with a thumb-turn. So nobody left this way.

DEC (thinking) They only want it to look like somebody came in.

He unrolls his canvas roll: twelve picks and two tension wrenches, each in its own pocket, like cutlery.

He has the hook in the keyway and his tongue between his teeth when the front bell rings out, loud and clear.

PICHON (neutral) Do not move, monsieur.

PICHON (neutral) And please do not finish whatever you are doing to that door.

On screen: Dec (on his knees, picks in hand, scared), Pichon (beige raincoat, glasses on a cord, napkin in his collar, neutral), Roux (behind him, waving the glass, angry)

PICHON (neutral) Commissaire Pichon. Police judiciaire. And you are?

DEC (worried) Dec. Declan Hartley. This isn't what it looks like.

ROUX (angry) That is the thief! He wanted my mainspring for forty euros!

PICHON (neutral) Monsieur Hartley. At nine eleven, a neighbour reports a break-in.

PICHON (neutral) At nine thirty-one, I find you at the back door with burglary tools.

PICHON (sad) I was at breakfast. I would like to go back to breakfast.

PICHON (sad) Please make it easy for me.

DEC (worried) I'm a locksmith. I build escape rooms.

DEC (worried) A medieval clockmaker's cell, for the Fondation Vaulmont. I was buying gears!

DEC (worried) Monsieur Roux! Tell him I was buying gears!

ROUX (thinking) He is a thief. But he didn't stop my clocks.

ROUX (thinking) He can't even get the price of a mainspring right.

DEC (happy) Thank you.

PICHON (neutral) Stand up, please. Slowly. Leave the little knives on the floor.

An officer turns Dec gently to face the wall.

DEC (worried) One thing. The back door's locked from inside, and picked from outside to fake a break-in.

DEC (worried) There's a brass sliver in his pendulum, and a chalk clock with nine at the top.

DEC (worried) And his tongue's gone grey.

There is silence behind him. When Pichon speaks again, the tiredness has gone out of his voice.

PICHON (thinking) A clock with nine at the top.

DEC (neutral) In the longcase. Inside the door.

Footsteps cross the shop, and the little door of the longcase opens. Then there is nothing but two hundred stopped clocks and a van reversing in the street.

The door closes, softly.

PICHON (neutral) Monsieur Hartley, you are under arrest.

DEC (surprised) For what? I've just told you!

PICHON (neutral) Exactly. You have told me a great deal more than you should know.

He takes the napkin from his collar, folds it into four, and pockets it.

PICHON (neutral) Come. We will talk about it over lunch.

Atelier Morel, rue Vieille-du-Temple. Earlier that morning, before eight.

The studio sits on the top floor under a grey skylight, and the radiator knocks.

On the big easel stands the Allegory of the Sundial: a woman in blue beside a stone sundial, pointing at the sky. A third of the sky is clean, cold blue. The rest is three centuries of brown varnish.

On screen: Inès (glasses pushed up, apron, gloved hands, thinking)

INÈS (neutral) You had better not have been retouched in the eighteen hundreds.

INÈS (neutral) I don't have time for it.

The sundial is odd. Its shadow falls on a smooth blank band where there are no hours.

Slow shoes come up the last flight of stairs.

On screen: Inès (at the easel, neutral), Morel (grey cashmere thin at the elbows, wringing his hands, worried)

MOREL (worried) How far have you got?

INÈS (neutral) Good morning, Jean-Paul. A third of the sky.

MOREL (worried) Only a third. En somme, Lassalle telephoned again.

MOREL (worried) The client wishes it finished by the end of the month.

INÈS (angry) We said the fifteenth of April.

MOREL (worried) Yes, voilà, but the client has an event. On the first. A sort of exhibition.

INÈS (angry) I won't take a scalpel to a seventeenth-century sky because somebody is having a party.

INÈS (thinking) Who is the client?

MOREL (neutral) Lassalle doesn't say. A private collection.

He leans too close to the canvas. His eyes skip the clean sky and go straight to the sundial, and stay there.

MOREL (scared) And there's nothing unusual? Underneath? Overpaint, that sort of thing?

INÈS (thinking) Why would there be?

MOREL (worried) No reason. A painting of this age, there's always something. Voilà.

MOREL (worried) I'll be in the office. If there's anything at all.

He shuts himself in his glass-walled office and sits before a dark screen with his hands flat on the desk.

Atelier Morel. Half past eight, blind drawn.

In the dark, the UV lamp hums, and the old varnish glows milky green across the whole canvas.

Only the sundial's blank band is different. A faint dark ring shows there, with twelve little marks around its rim.

On screen: Inès (bent over the canvas with a loupe, surprised)

She swaps the UV for a raking lamp, angled almost flat, and ridges of buried paint throw tiny shadows.

A painted-out clock face rises from the stone like Braille, with XII at the bottom, III on the left, and at the top, where twelve belongs, IX.

Below it, along the base of the sundial, run painted-out Roman capitals: MVTVO DATVM. Then comes a gap, scraped to the ground with a blade, and then HORA NONA.

INÈS (thinking) Mutuo datum. Given as a loan.

INÈS (thinking) Hora nona. The ninth hour.

INÈS (worried) And one word somebody did not want read.

She photographs it from every side, forty-one shots, and logs each one in the notebook in her apron pocket.

Through the glass wall, Morel sits exactly as before. He has not asked why the blind is down.

At nine thirty-five, the radio above the sink starts talking about a shop three streets north.

"An elderly clockmaker was found this morning, confused, in his shop in the rue des Gravilliers. All his clocks had been stopped."

"Listeners may remember last October's case of a retired judge, the so-called Stopped Hour..."

Inès turns it off. Everyone in the Marais knows Anselme Roux. He once told her mother that her wristwatch was an insult to the wrist.

She pulls off her gloves, grabs her navy trench coat, and winds the mustard scarf twice round her neck.

MOREL (worried) Inès? Inès, where...?

INÈS (neutral) Coffee.

Rue des Gravilliers, outside Roux & Fils. Ten o'clock.

A police car stands half on the pavement. A small Paris crowd has gathered: two women with shopping trolleys, a man with an old dachshund, and an old lady in a fur hat telling everyone she telephoned the police herself.

Through the dark green shop window, every clock shows the same time.

The door opens and the bell clangs. A tall man ducks a beat too late and hits his head on it.

On screen: Pichon (beige raincoat, tired, neutral), Dec (hands behind his back, hair in four directions, worried), Inès (mustard scarf, arms folded, neutral)

DEC (worried) No, I understand, I'd arrest me too. On paper I look desperate.

He spots Inès and stops dead, so the officers have to stop too, and gives her a smile of enormous hopeful charm.

DEC (happy) Madame! Could you telephone to my client?

DEC (happy) Je suis en retard pour mon client. J'ai un rendez-vous dans onze heures.

INÈS (neutral) You're not late yet. You mean je vais être en retard.

INÈS (neutral) And dans onze heures means eleven hours from now. You mean à onze heures.

INÈS (neutral) Your client will be waiting until tonight.

DEC (surprised) Right. Thanks. That's actually very helpful. I'll know for next time.

INÈS (thinking) Will there be a next time?

DEC (worried) Ah, God, I hope not. Will there?

PICHON (sad) Walk, Monsieur Hartley.

As they fold him into the back seat, something drops from his jacket pocket and rolls, ringing, across the cobbles to the toe of Inès's grey boot.

The car bumps off the pavement. Dec turns in the rear window and looks back at her, half apology, half plea.

On screen: Inès (alone on the pavement, holding something small, thinking)

In her palm lies a brass gear the size of a two-euro coin, new-cut, its teeth too sharp, still warm from his pocket.

Stamped on one face, very small, is a circle with twelve ticks, and at the top, IX.

INÈS (surprised) Istenem.

She puts it in her coat pocket.

INÈS (thinking) Now. Where does the police judiciaire have its lunch?
