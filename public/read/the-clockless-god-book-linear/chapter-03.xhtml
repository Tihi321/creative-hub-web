The Ninth Niche

Roux & Fils, Horlogerie. Tuesday, four in the afternoon.

Two hundred clocks tap against the plaster. The wind-up cats swivel their glass eyes back and forth.

The longcase pendulum cuts a steady arc. Someone scraped the chalk drawing off its glass door. The handless watch rests on the counter. Roux stares at the floorboards instead.

On screen: Roux (brown work coat, eyebrows lowered, angry), Dec (on a stool, satchel on his knee, neutral), Inès (on a stool, trench coat, neutral)

ROUX (angry) A full day gone.

ROUX (angry) Eighty-one years and I track them all. I keep a ledger, if you please.

ROUX (thinking) You’re the one who poured me water when the pipes froze.

DEC (happy) I plead guilty. It was lukewarm tap water though.

ROUX (thinking) What about her?

INÈS (neutral) Inès Varga. You called my mother’s watch an insult to anatomy in twenty oh six.

ROUX (neutral) It absolutely did. Take a seat. Watch your fingers on the cuckoo. He has a temper.

DEC (neutral) Anselme. Anybody come knocking before Sunday asking for work you refused?

ROUX (thinking) A horloger’s trade is refusing. Two commissions this winter alone.

ROUX (thinking) One gentleman paid a visit. Then just the boy in grey kept showing up. Arms full of cardboard tubes.

INÈS (thinking) Which gentleman?

ROUX (thinking) Tailored charcoal. Hair combed straight back. Spoke with that smooth, expensive tone.

ROUX (thinking) He had one of those timepieces hanging from his waistcoat.

He tips his chin at the watch without looking at it.

ROUX (neutral) I thought, that’s a fine watch to have no hands.

ROUX (thinking) The first job was a copy of a fourteenth-century letter lock. Nine wheels.

ROUX (thinking) You turn each wheel to a letter. When the nine spell the word, the bar drops.

DEC (thinking) What was the word?

ROUX (angry) I don’t know! The drawings had numbers, no letters.

ROUX (angry) The boy brought the final position in a sealed envelope and stood over me while I cut it.

ROUX (angry) He said it was a matter of faith. Faith! In my shop!

INÈS (neutral) And the second commission?

Roux’s hands go still on the counter.

ROUX (angry) The second one I refused. They wanted the public clocks changed.

ROUX (angry) Station timepieces, hospital clocks, the receivers in the traffic lights.

ROUX (thinking) Every single one listens to Allouis, that big transmitter near Bourges.

ROUX (thinking) It broadcasts the official time on a long wave frequency around the clock.

ROUX (neutral) Stations, hospitals, traffic signals, your mother’s kitchen timer.

ROUX (neutral) All tuned to one source, like monks listening to an abbot.

ROUX (angry) They wanted them tuned to a different frequency. The boy said it was for an exhibition.

ROUX (angry) I threw him out. Ten days ago.

The cuckoo over the workshop door bursts out and shouts four times. Dec jumps.

DEC (worried) And on Sunday someone steals a day off you. Because you said no.

Roux says nothing. He doesn’t need to.

He pulls a cardboard tube from under the bench and spreads five sheets on the counter. He weights the corners with a carriage clock, a jar of screws and his loupe, and after a moment, with the handless watch.

The sheets are faint brown copies of old drawings: nine wheels on a spindle, with alphabets round their rims and a notch inside each. In every corner is a circle with IX at the top.

INÈS (thinking) Frater Anselmus Vey me fecit, MCCCXLIX.

INÈS (thinking) Brother Anselme Vey made me. Thirteen forty-nine.

ROUX (neutral) The plague year. Everyone was dying, and this one made locks.

ROUX (neutral) The word never appeared on the papers. The boy said Vey kept it in his personal book.

DEC (thinking) The watch. Those nine rings inside the lid.

DEC (thinking) It’s a diagram of this lock.

ROUX (neutral) Naturally. It’s their calling card, like the scallop on a pilgrim’s hat.

ROUX (neutral) They all carry the lock design. None of them carries the actual word.

ROUX (happy) See, thief? You’re not so stupid.

ROUX (sad) Take them. Take the tube. Every time I open it I taste cloves.

INÈS (thinking) Anselme. Show me your tongue.

With great dignity, he does. It is still grey, but fainter now, like the shadow of a thumbprint on a window.

INÈS (worried) I’ve seen that shade before. On an apothecary’s sign I restored in Lyon.

INÈS (worried) A patient lying flat, tongue out, stained grey. Spiced wine, cloves, and a plant with white trumpet flowers.

INÈS (worried) The rhyme said: give this to a man and he’ll owe you a day.

ROUX (surprised) White trumpets. Datura. My grandmother grew it for the rats.

ROUX (surprised) She swore the seeds would make me forget my own name.

The anger drains from his face. What is left is an old man in a work coat who sat in his own shop for a whole day without knowing it.

ROUX (sad) Somebody gave me that? In my coffee?

ROUX (sad) And drew in my longcase. My father’s clock.

Dec puts a hand on his shoulder. It feels as light as a bird’s.

DEC (sad) We’ll find out who. I promise.

ROUX (happy) Thief. You promise like a thief.

Rue des Gravilliers. Dusk.

The street lamps flicker on and people are heading home. Somewhere a scooter revs, ridden by someone who loves it more than he loves his neighbours.

On screen: Dec (carrier bag with the tube, thinking), Inès (unfolding a scrap of paper under the lamp, thinking)

DEC (worried) Roux said no, and they stopped him. Lassalle’s in the river.

DEC (worried) And there’s a party at the Panthéon on eclipse day, where nothing can move.

DEC (worried) I don’t know what any of it means.

INÈS (neutral) Neither do I. But I know where this is.

The note from Lassalle’s secret drawer reads: Barrière d’Enfer. La neuvième niche.

DEC (scared) Underground. With the bones.

INÈS (neutral) Six million of them. The ninth niche won’t be in the part with the gift shop.

She finds a number in her phone and stares at it as if it were a wasp on her hand.

INÈS (worried) My mother’s hairdresser has a cousin who goes down there at night.

INÈS (worried) A philosophy student. They call her La Taupe.

DEC (thinking) The Mole. Is she good?

INÈS (neutral) Very good, my mother’s hairdresser says. Also completely insane.

A disused railway tunnel, fourteenth arrondissement. Wednesday, eleven at night.

Water drips from a brick bridge. Beyond a fence with a gap in it waits the black mouth of a tunnel no train has used in eighty years.

On screen: La Taupe (shaved head, headlamp, chest waders, taped-up backpack, unimpressed), Inès (pulling on waders, worried), Dec (hopping into waders, worried)

LA TAUPE (neutral) He’s too tall. Some places it’s one metre forty.

LA TAUPE (neutral) The tall ones always complain.

LA TAUPE (thinking) Your mother’s hairdresser says you’re afraid of everything.

INÈS (angry) Not everything. Heights and dogs.

LA TAUPE (happy) Underground there are no heights and no dogs. Only water and the dead.

LA TAUPE (happy) You’re fine with the dead?

INÈS (neutral) I restore paintings from the sixteen hundreds. Everyone in them is dead.

DEC (worried) Your mother’s hairdresser has a very complete family.

La Taupe lies on her back by a hole at the foot of the wall and slides in feet first, like a letter into a postbox. Inès goes next, before she can think about it.

The quarry galleries under Paris. Past midnight.

The gallery is as tall as a church nave. Pale yellow limestone sweats in the headlamp beams, and black water ripples round their boots. A plaque on the wall reads: Rue Saint-Jacques, 1781.

On screen: La Taupe (lamp beam swinging, talking fast, happy), Inès (looking up, thinking), Dec (crawling out of a passage, scared)

LA TAUPE (happy) That’s the street above us. The king’s engineers named every gallery for the road on top.

LA TAUPE (happy) Up there someone is eating a kebab.

Dec stands up and cracks his head on the ceiling.

DEC (angry) Ow. No, it’s grand. I’m not complaining.

LA TAUPE (neutral) He’s complaining.

INÈS (neutral) He’s Irish. It’s how they say thank you.

They walk for an hour while La Taupe talks about Spinoza and drainage. Then the bones begin.

Long bones are stacked like firewood, ends out, with a row of skulls set into the stack, all facing the same way.

LA TAUPE (worried) Overflow from the old cemeteries. This is the Barrière d’Enfer section.

LA TAUPE (worried) The tollgate is right above us.

INÈS (scared) They look like they’re waiting for a bus.

LA TAUPE (thinking) Un. Deux. Trois... Huit. Neuf.

Arched hollows are built into the bones like little chapels. The ninth is empty, just a stone sill and a back wall of sweating limestone.

Dec lays his palm on the back wall and moves it slowly, as Inès feels a canvas for a change in the paint. He stops, and pushes.

Something deep in the rock goes clunk, like a big key in a big door, and the back of the niche swings inward.

LA TAUPE (surprised) Oh, putain.

The chapel under the Barrière d’Enfer. Moments later.

It is a round room cut from the rock, its floor swept dry. Nine tall empty niches circle the walls, each with a little stone step.

Over a limestone altar, carved deep and painted a dull silver, hangs a clock face a metre across. It has no hands, and IX at the top.

On screen: Inès (bent over the altar, pencil in hand, scared), Dec (at her shoulder, worried), La Taupe (by the door, worried)

On the altar lies a ledger bound in leather the colour of dried blood. Inès turns the pages with her pencil tip.

INÈS (worried) The old pages are hours paid. Then new ones, all in one neat hand.

INÈS (worried) Débiteurs. Debtors.

Delorme, Jacques, magistrat honoraire. Octobre. Une heure.

Aubry, Marthe, libraire. Janvier. Une heure.

Girardin, Abbé Luc, Saint-Médard. Février. Une heure.

Roux, Anselme, horloger. Mars. Une heure.

Lassalle, Gérard, notaire. Mars. Instead of an hour, there is one small word: Remis.

INÈS (scared) Remitted. Delivered. Or paid off.

INÈS (thinking) There’s a note at the bottom. By the rule of the Debt Clock. Brother Anselme Vey.

INÈS (thinking) Priory of Combe-Noire, Jura.

INÈS (surprised) Vey. Vey kept it in his book.

LA TAUPE (scared) Turn off your lamps. Now.

Far off in the galleries come many slow footsteps in water, and low voices rising and falling. They are not talking. They are chanting.

The three of them press into the deepest niche behind the altar, in the dark. Inès can feel Dec’s heart through his leather jacket.

The door swings open on candlelight.

On screen: Inès (hiding in the dark, scared), Dec (hiding beside her, scared)

Eighteen figures in grey hooded robes enter two by two, carrying candles. Eight pairs take their places on the steps of eight niches, and the ninth pair goes to the altar.

The niche with three idiots in waders in it is left empty.

The chanting stops, and pages turn.

VOICE (neutral) Lassalle has been remitted.

It is a man’s voice, cultivated and unhurried, the voice of someone people have always leaned closer to hear. It pours like warm oil.

VOICE (neutral) Brothers. Our restorer is close to the word. Our clocks are ready.

VOICE (neutral) In seven days the moon will cover the sun.

VOICE (neutral) At the hour of None, the voice from Allouis will speak for us.

VOICE (neutral) The city will hear its debt called in. And the Seventh will collect.

VOICE (happy) Mutuo datum. Reddetur.

Eighteen voices answer, low: Reddetur. It shall be repaid.

At the words "our restorer", Inès feels Dec’s hand find hers in the dark, and she grips it hard enough to hurt them both.

The candles leave pair by pair. The last pair stops in the doorway, and one of them is far larger than the other: a bald head, shoulders filling the arch, a little blue cuckoo clock tattooed on his neck.

The stone door closes. On the other side, a heavy iron bolt slides home.

The chapel, then a drain under the fourteenth. Two in the morning.

One headlamp clicks on. Dec throws his whole long body at the stone door, and it does not move a whisper.

On screen: La Taupe (sitting on the floor, scared), Dec (kneeling, lock picks unrolled, thinking), Inès (holding the lamp, worried)

LA TAUPE (scared) We’re locked in a cult’s bedroom under the fourteenth arrondissement.

LA TAUPE (scared) And nobody knows we’re here.

DEC (neutral) Ah, now. Locked is my department.

Behind the altar, low in the wall, a grille of rusted wrought iron covers a black opening the size of a dog kennel. Cold air and the sound of running water come through it. On the grille hangs a big square box lock, rusted almost solid.

Dec lays out his picks like the cutlery for Sunday dinner. His hands shake, and by the second pick they have stopped.

DEC (thinking) Old lever lock. Two levers. Not opened since Napoleon was a boy.

DEC (thinking) She’s going to be a right...

The lock makes a sound like a kicked gate.

DEC (happy) There she is. Good girl.

The grille screams open on a round brick tunnel full of fast black water.

LA TAUPE (happy) Drainage! Eighteenth-century! Mon Dieu, it’s beautiful.

LA TAUPE (neutral) Follow me exactly. Exactly.

They are wet to the neck by the time they climb out of a manhole into a courtyard behind a school, under an orange street lamp.

On screen: Dec (soaked, mud across his forehead, laughing, happy), Inès (soaked, glasses steamed, laughing, happy), La Taupe (sitting on a bin, laughing, happy)

For no reason at all, the three of them laugh until a window lights up above them. Inès’s laugh turns into something else, and then back.

She wipes her glasses on the inside of her collar, the only dry thing she has.

INÈS (neutral) I’m going to the Jura.

DEC (happy) Right. Well. I’m going with you.

INÈS (thinking) Why?

DEC (neutral) Professional reasons. Vey’s lock.

DEC (neutral) If his clock sits in the Jura, his lock sits in the Jura, and I want to see it.

DEC (happy) Purely professionally.

INÈS (neutral) Purely professionally.

DEC (happy) Totally.

From the bin, La Taupe mutters something about Spinoza that Inès chooses not to hear.
