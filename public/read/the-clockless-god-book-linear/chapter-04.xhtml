The Honeymoon That Isn't

The train from Paris to Basel. Thursday 26 March, morning.

Green fields smear past the window. Two paper cups of restaurant-car coffee sit on the table, the colour of ditch water.

On screen: Dec (leather jacket, turning his cup round and round, sad), Inès (scarf still on, empty notebook open, neutral)

DEC (sad) Da was a locksmith in Galway forty years. Shop on Dominick Street.

DEC (happy) Van with Hartley and Son painted on the side before there was a son.

DEC (happy) Saturdays he'd park me on an upturned bucket by whatever door and talk me through it.

DEC (thinking) He used to say every lock's a story about what somebody's afraid of.

DEC (sad) He died in twenty-one. Turns out I'm a decent locksmith and a feckin' terrible businessman. Closed the shop, sold the van.

DEC (neutral) So. Escape rooms. You build a story about being afraid, and people pay to get out of it.

Inès watches him over her glasses, pen frozen.

DEC (worried) Sorry. I talk. It's a thing.

INÈS (neutral) I know. Go on.

Basel station, then the train to Delémont. Late morning.

Twelve minutes to change under a big departures board. Dec holds a pretzel the size of his head.

Three carriages down, a thin young man in a long grey coat steps off the same train, glances at the board, and stares hard at nothing.

On screen: Dec (pretzel halfway to his mouth, surprised), Inès (heavy bag of books, neutral), Fabien (the young man in grey: pale, short fair hair, long grey coat, silver watch chain, neutral)

DEC (worried) Inès. Don't look round.

She looks round, straight at him. The young man in grey is suddenly fascinated by a vending machine.

INÈS (thinking) Him?

DEC (thinking) The chain on his waistcoat. Bet you the rest of this pretzel there's a watch on it with no hands.

INÈS (neutral) I don't want the rest of your pretzel. Platform four. Walk normally. Not like a stepladder.

On the Delémont train, the young man sits at the far end of their carriage behind a Swiss newspaper.

On screen: Dec (peering over Inès's open notebook, thinking), Inès (reading, neutral)

Inès reaches across without looking up and turns the notebook the right way up.

DEC (thinking) He's been on the same page since Basel. Nobody reads Swiss pharma shares for half an hour.

DEC (worried) That's not a share-prices face. That's a man memorising what you look like.

INÈS (neutral) Dec, you're the least discreet thing in Switzerland. You're less discreet than the mountains.

INÈS (worried) Morel was glad I was going. He asked for a week. Didn't ask about the painting. Relieved. And frightened.

DEC (thinking) "Our restorer is close to the word." That's what warm-oil said in the chapel. Our restorer. Like you're theirs.

INÈS (angry) I'm not theirs.

DEC (neutral) I know you're not.

INÈS (sad) I'm nobody's.

Her phone buzzes. She reads it, goes still, and turns the screen to him.

INÈS (worried) Pichon. At 07:51 every clock in Gare d'Austerlitz showed nine for nine minutes. The traffic lights on the boulevard de l'Hôpital too.

INÈS (worried) SNCF says a technical fault. He says nothing yet, no evidence, and a very good lunch booked.

INÈS (neutral) And: please tell me you're in Lyon with your mother.

DEC (surprised) He gave you his number?

INÈS (neutral) I took it off his card while he was eating. He didn't notice.

DEC (neutral) He noticed. He's a commissaire. He let you.

She types with one precise finger: I am with my mother. Send.

DEC (worried) We're in Switzerland.

INÈS (neutral) He doesn't need to know that. Yet.

At the far end of the carriage the young man turns a page. The same page.

Les Bréviaires, the platform, then the inn Le Balancier. Afternoon.

A little red train climbs out of the woods onto a high white plateau, black spruce scattered like ink blots under a sky the colour of an old spoon.

A dozen farmhouses, an onion-spired church, workshops with lights on. Up the narrowing valley, on the hill, a black square tower and a broken wall.

On screen: Dec (in trainers, snow to his shins, scared), Inès (grey ankle boots, neutral)

DEC (scared) Ah, God.

INÈS (neutral) I told you to bring boots.

DEC (angry) I don't own boots. I'm from Galway. We don't have weather. We have rain.

The young man in grey steps down and walks past close enough to touch. As his coat swings, Dec sees the round edge of a watch at the end of the chain.

He walks up the single village street, leaving long neat prints, turns in at a gate, and vanishes.

Inside Le Balancier it is hot and dark and smells of woodsmoke, melted cheese and wet dog. A very old Bernese mountain dog sleeps on the hearth. Inès gives it a wide berth anyway.

On screen: Madame Jobin (edelweiss apron, key ring, beaming, happy), Inès (neutral), Dec (dripping, worried)

INÈS (neutral) Two rooms. Please.

MADAME JOBIN (happy) Of course, of course! On your honeymoon? Everyone comes to the plateau on honeymoon. It's the quiet.

INÈS (angry) No.

DEC (worried) Ah, well, it's not...

They say it together. Madame Jobin's smile grows warmer.

MADAME JOBIN (happy) Rooms three and four. There's a door between. It locks. From both sides.

She winks at Dec. Inès takes key three and goes straight upstairs. Dec's ears go hot. The dog opens one eye, looks at him with enormous pity, and shuts it.

Room three, Le Balancier. A quarter to ten at night.

A knock on the old pine door between the rooms, from her side. Dec draws the little hand-forged bolt he has been admiring for an hour so as not to think about anything else.

On screen: Inès (long grey jumper, hair sticking up at the back, thinking), Dec (in socks, worried)

INÈS (neutral) Turn off your light. Come and look.

Her room is dark. Below, the village lies blue and silent under snow. Far up the valley, where the black tower stood against the afternoon, a small light moves.

It stops, goes on, slips behind the broken wall. Then it climbs, slowly, as if carried up a stair inside the tower, and comes to rest near the top.

INÈS (worried) Combe-Noire. The priory. Madame Jobin says nobody goes up there in winter. The snow's two metres deep on the track.

DEC (scared) Somebody's going up there.

They stand side by side in the dark, not touching. The light burns small and steady at the top of the tower.

At nine o'clock exactly by Dec's phone, it goes out.

Room three. Friday, dawn.

The church bell across the road strikes six, very close. Inès counts, and breathes out when it stops at six.

On screen: Inès (two pairs of socks, laptop glowing dim, thinking)

On the screen, photograph twenty-three, raking light from the left, enlarged until the scraped gap fills it: MVTVO DATVM, the gap, HORA NONA.

The word is gone, but the chalky ground beneath it is not. A painter lettering a motto marks his spacing first, with little ticks.

INÈS (thinking) There you are.

She counts three times. Nine ticks. At the start, the ghost of a downstroke and a curve: P, or R, or B. At the end, a short foot: E, or L.

INÈS (thinking) Nine letters. A word for what happens to a loan at the ninth hour.

She writes in her notebook: R/P/B _ E/L.

The Musée de l'Heure, the old schoolhouse. Friday morning.

A sign on the door: Ouvert quand c'est ouvert. Inside, everything ticks: painted farmhouse clocks, watchmakers' benches left as if for lunch a century ago, pocket watches in rows like eggs, a whole wall of cuckoo clocks.

Dec stops in front of the cuckoo clocks with deep personal suspicion.

On screen: Clothilde (grey habit and short veil under a red ski jacket, coffee pot in hand, unlit cigarette, happy), Inès (neutral), Dec (eyeing the cuckoos, thinking)

CLOTHILDE (happy) The couple at Madame Jobin's. The honeymoon that isn't. Coffee?

INÈS (angry) We're not...

CLOTHILDE (happy) Of course not. Nobody is, at first. Sit. You're here about the priory.

INÈS (thinking) How do you know?

CLOTHILDE (neutral) Nobody comes in March except for the priory.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) And the young man in grey who rented the forester's house, a Monsieur Ruel, Fabien, asked me yesterday if two people from Paris had come asking.

CLOTHILDE (angry) I said no. It was true, then. I don't like him. He reads timetables for pleasure.

Inès puts the handless silver watch on the table between the cups.

The old nun sets down her cigarette and folds her hands well away from it. The fun goes out of her face.

CLOTHILDE (sad) Ah. Then you'd better have the whole of it.

CLOTHILDE (thinking) Combe-Noire was a little priory. Twelve monks, a mill, sheep. In thirteen forty-nine the plague came up from Basel.

CLOTHILDE (thinking) One monk was a clockmaker, a new and strange thing to be. Anselme Vey.

DEC (surprised) The lock.

CLOTHILDE (worried) The lock, the clock, everything. He preached that God was a lender. He had lent us time, every hour of every life.

CLOTHILDE (worried) And now He was calling in the loan. Only those who paid ahead would be spared. In work, in obedience. Later in money.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) Vey built a clock to keep the account. The Debt Clock. Nine hours on its dial, because the ninth was the hour of reckoning.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) Whoever holds the clock holds the time of everyone who can hear it.

Inès thinks of Roux, and every radio clock in France listening to one voice. Beside her, Dec keeps his eyes on his cup.

INÈS (worried) And those who didn't pay?

CLOTHILDE (angry) The Grey Communion. Datura in wine, cloves to hide the taste. And ash from the burnt accounts of those who died owing.

CLOTHILDE (angry) That's what turns the tongue grey.

CLOTHILDE (sad) A man drank it and lost a day. The monks said the hour was taken as payment. He believed it. And then he paid.

All along the wall the cuckoo clocks strike ten, one after another. Nobody speaks until they finish.

CLOTHILDE (happy) In sixteen ten the Visitation sisters came up with soldiers and a bishop's letter. They walled up the clock and broke the stair.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) The Brotherhood went down to the cities and became bankers. Much the same thing, with better shoes.

Later, the coffee pot refilled twice, Dec wanders off down the room.

On screen: Clothilde (thinking), Inès (notebook open, thinking)

CLOTHILDE (thinking) They say Vey repented at the end. Wrote one last line, a confession, and they scraped it out.

CLOTHILDE (sad) It was in his ledger. My sisters burnt half of it. The other half they never found.

Inès shows her the notebook: the sundial, the nine at the top, mutuo datum, hora nona, the scraped word.

CLOTHILDE (thinking) Hora nona. In a monastery that isn't nine o'clock. Monks counted from dawn.

CLOTHILDE (worried) None, the ninth hour, is three in the afternoon. When the brothers rang the reckoning.

Inès's hand goes to the cream card in her pocket. Panthéon. Le premier avril. Quinze heures précises.

INÈS (scared) Three in the afternoon. The ninth hour.

CLOTHILDE (worried) They've bought the priory, you know. Last autumn. A foundation from Paris, a name like a wine. Valmont. Vaumont.

INÈS (worried) Vaulmont.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) That's it. They changed every lock they could, with a locksmith from Basel. Not this one. It's in the wall, and the wall is listed.

She puts an iron key on the table, black, as long as her hand, its bit cut like a little castle.

CLOTHILDE (happy) The abbess's key. To the tower. It's been in my drawer forty years and I'm tired of it.

At the far end of the room, Dec stands very still before a small glass case.

On screen: Dec (hands in pockets, reverent, thinking), Inès (holding the key, neutral), Clothilde (coffee pot, sad)

On faded red velvet lies an iron-bound reliquary box. Set into its front, a brass lock of nine thin lettered wheels, worn almost smooth by hands six hundred years dead.

DEC (thinking) It's his. Vey's. Little brother of the one Roux made. Look how thin he cut those wheels. By hand, by candlelight, in a plague.

DEC (surprised) And it's open. The shackle's out.

INÈS (thinking) Then the letters are still set. Can you read the word?

DEC (sad) Somebody's spun them, all nine, after. You can see where the wear stops. It only tells you he was very good.

CLOTHILDE (sad) The sisters found it in his cell, open. My predecessor said it was the only honest thing in the priory. A lock that had given up.

Through the tall window, beyond the village and the ridge of spruce, the black tower of Combe-Noire stands against a pewter sky, where last night a light climbed the stair and went out at nine.
