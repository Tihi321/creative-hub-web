VII de IX

The gallery of the dome. 14:56.

The dome is still in its blue eclipse dark. Dec's torch lies on the stone, pointed up at the brass doors, so at least the light stays still.

Below the balustrade, the nave is a murmuring sea of little phone lights. The loudspeakers are silent.

On screen: Vaulmont (three metres off, silver hair a little disordered from the climb, small dark pistol held like a pair of reading glasses, neutral), Dec (back to the cabinet, hands behind him, scared)

VAULMONT (neutral) Monsieur Hartley. Please don't touch that.

VAULMONT (happy) It's a very good lock. Monsieur Roux is a genius, and a very rude one.

VAULMONT (happy) You could stand there with your little picks until Easter.

DEC (worried) Yeah. I noticed.

VAULMONT (neutral) In four minutes the debt will be called. After that you go home and build your rooms.

VAULMONT (neutral) We are not monsters. We simply keep accounts.

Dec stays where he is.

DEC (worried) Can I tell you something about locks?

Behind his back, his left hand finds the first wheel. It is cold, fine and perfect, and it is already on R. Inès got that far.

DEC (thinking) My da used to say every lock's a story.

DEC (thinking) A story about what somebody's afraid of.

He turns the second wheel four clicks, counting from B. He went over every offset with her thirty times at the bench.

DEC (neutral) This one's a monk in a plague, terrified of his own bill.

DEC (neutral) So he puts in the word he wants, and doesn't think he'll get.

The third wheel turns, and then the fourth.

DEC (angry) And you lot kept the combination six hundred years like a relic. Nine holy numbers.

DEC (angry) Not one of you ever counted round the letters.

VAULMONT (worried) What are you doing?

DEC (neutral) Opening it. With the word.

The fifth wheel turns, and the sixth.

VAULMONT (angry) There is no word. There are numbers. The Rule...

DEC (neutral) Seventh. Eighth.

His fingers have stopped shaking, and he has no idea when that happened.

DEC (angry) A notary drowned. An old man's day stolen.

DEC (angry) All for a word that was in your lock the whole time.

DEC (sad) It's not pay, Monsieur de Vaulmont.

He turns the ninth wheel to E.

DEC (neutral) It's forgive.

There is a small, deep, perfect click. The brass doors swing open an inch, and the hum and the green light come out at him.

Inside stand a neat rack of black boxes with little green lights and a car battery the size of a suitcase. On the middle box, in white paint pen and in the notebook's slanted hand, someone has written ALS162.

Beside it is a big red rotary switch, like the isolator on a boiler. He turns it hard, all the way round, until it clunks.

The hum stops. The green lights go out down the rack, one by one, and silence fills the dome.

Vaulmont looks past him at the dead rack, then at the nine wheels on the brass door, still spelling R E M I T T E R E.

His lips move over the letters like a child's over a word it has never seen.

Through the great doors, the traffic lights down the rue Soufflot stop flashing red and go green, one after another.

In the nave, the vigil clock lurches, and its hands sweep round by themselves to 14:58. That is two minutes short of None.

A hundred people who were promised a miracle murmur as the clocks tidy themselves away.

Then a woman's voice comes out of the loudspeakers, quick and precise and a little out of breath.

The stage in the nave, under the dome. 14:59.

In the beam of the television light, the grey cloth lies on the floor.

The Allegory of the Sundial stands in the white glare, with its saffron sleeve, its clean blue sky and its sundial.

On screen: Inès (glasses crooked, scarf half unwound, phone held up to the camera lens, neutral)

INÈS (neutral) My name is Inès Varga. I'm the restorer of this painting.

INÈS (neutral) I'm going to show you something.

Her phone fills every television screen in France: photograph twenty-three, in raking light, with the gap in the Latin line and the nine ghost ticks.

INÈS (neutral) These are the last words of the Brotherhood's founder.

INÈS (neutral) Somebody scraped one of them out with a knife.

INÈS (thinking) I've put it back. Mutuo datum remittere hora nona.

INÈS (thinking) What was given as a loan, forgive at the ninth hour.

INÈS (angry) The clocks were not a miracle.

INÈS (angry) There's a transmitter in the dome, built by the Fondation Vaulmont. Someone has just turned it off.

INÈS (angry) Your wine was meant to have datura in it. It was swapped.

INÈS (angry) The real wine is in a crate in the south transept.

The silence is so complete that sixty metres up, Dec hears the pendulum go by.

Then the minister begins to laugh, helplessly, an empty pewter cup in his hand. Beside him, the prefect of police goes the colour of a church candle.

The red light on the camera stays on.

The gallery of the dome. Three o'clock.

The green glow is gone. There is only Dec's torch on the stone, and the dome slowly growing pale.

On screen: Vaulmont (pistol lowered, the calm still on his face like a coat left on a hook, sad), Dec (at the balustrade, hoarse, worried)

VAULMONT (sad) Forgive. For six hundred years.

DEC (sad) Yeah. Funny old thing.

The pale grey eyes rest on Dec's face with the enormous patience of a man who has always had time, considering a bill.

Then he turns the pistol round and holds it out, butt first. Dec takes it like a dead fish.

VAULMONT (neutral) I'll go down by the stair.

VAULMONT (neutral) You'll forgive me if I don't wait for you.

The nave. Three minutes past three.

The light comes back as a long silver dawn, and the painted saints step out of the dark one by one. In the square outside, the crowd cheers, foolish and relieved.

Everyone stands in a ring around the brass ring on the floor.

On screen: Pichon (out of breath, raincoat flapping, napkin still tucked in his collar with a small brown stain, neutral), Vaulmont (holding out his wrists together like a ticket at a barrier, neutral), Dec (at the edge of the ring, pistol hanging from his hand, surprised)

Pichon looks at the wrists. Then, as if it were a disagreeable task after a very good meal, he takes out his handcuffs.

PICHON (neutral) Auguste de Vaulmont. I am arresting you for the murder of Gérard Lassalle.

PICHON (neutral) And for poisoning Anselme Roux, Judge Delorme, Madame Aubry, the Abbé Girardin and Jean-Paul Morel.

PICHON (neutral) All of whom are alive, and very angry.

PICHON (happy) And for many other things I shall think of over my dessert.

PICHON (happy) I'm told you like to keep accounts. So do I.

The cuffs click. The pendulum goes past them with its soft, deep sigh. It never stopped, and it never even hesitated.

The crowd parts like water round a stone. Kessler comes out of the south stair, stooping, his hands empty, and holds his great wrists out to the youngest officer, who goes white. Kessler says nothing at all.

MOUSSA (happy) He's trying to take the crate! The thin one! He's...

From the south transept comes a crash, and then a yelp.

MOUSSA (happy) Monsieur l'agent, I'm sitting on him. Do you want him?

MOUSSA (happy) I'm sitting on him.

A young officer takes the pistol out of Dec's fingers, gently, like scissors from a child. Dec's hand stays curled round the shape of it.

On screen: Inès (beside the painting on the little grey stage, scarf hanging to her knee, very still, neutral), Dec (across the nave, worried)

He lifts his empty hand a little, uncertainly.

After a moment, so slightly that nobody else in the Panthéon could see, she lifts hers.

Roux & Fils, Horlogerie. Thursday 2 April, nine in the morning.

Two hundred clocks hang on every wall, and every one of them has stopped.

Madame Ferré from the bakery and an old lady in a fur hat, who came in uninvited and will not leave, sit on stools by the counter.

On screen: Roux (at the top of a stepladder, brown coat, winding key raised, angry), Inès (on a stool, neutral), Dec (on a stool beside her, happy)

ROUX (angry) Yesterday every station in Paris stopped at nine. And my clocks did not stop. Not one!

ROUX (angry) My clocks don't listen to Allouis. They listen to me.

ROUX (neutral) So this morning I stopped them all by hand, at nine.

ROUX (neutral) To see how it felt to do it to myself.

INÈS (thinking) And how does it feel?

ROUX (angry) Stupid. Watch.

He touches the walnut regulator's pendulum, then the next clock, and the next, all down the shelves. Last of all he pushes the longcase, as gently as a child on a swing.

The ticking comes up from every side like rain starting on a roof, none of it together.

The cuckoo over the workshop door shoots out, says cuckoo once as if clearing its throat, and goes back in.

ROUX (happy) There. That's the only miracle I've ever approved of.

The old lady in the fur hat bursts into tears. Madame Ferré gives her a croissant.

A very serious restaurant in the sixth. Half past twelve.

The tablecloths are white, and the eleven dishes on the menu have not changed since 1952.

The blanquette de veau comes in a sauce so smooth that Inès could lay gold leaf on it.

On screen: Pichon (napkin tucked firmly in, thirty hours awake, neutral), Inès (pushing a mushroom round her plate, neutral), Dec (forgetting to eat, surprised)

PICHON (neutral) Fabien Ruel has confessed to everything, with pride, in the form of a sermon.

PICHON (thinking) He believes he will be forgiven. He was very struck by the word on the lock.

PICHON (neutral) Your young waiter is pressing no charges for the bite.

DEC (surprised) He bit Moussa?

PICHON (happy) Moussa was sitting on him.

PICHON (neutral) Kessler has said nothing. His hands match the marks on Lassalle's arms.

PICHON (neutral) He asked for one thing only. That someone check the netting in the dome for birds.

Inès pushes the mushroom slowly round the sauce until she trusts her face again.

PICHON (sad) Morel woke at six this morning and wept. He'll be well.

PICHON (neutral) He would like to see you, Madame Varga, when you can bear it.

INÈS (surprised) It might not be long.

DEC (worried) And Vaulmont?

Pichon puts down his fork and lays a small evidence bag on the cloth between the bread and the salt.

Inside is a plain silver pocket watch with a white enamel face, twelve numerals, and no hands.

PICHON (angry) Released at four this morning. Two million euros bail.

PICHON (angry) Paid at three fifty-one, by a Liechtenstein foundation that did not exist on Tuesday.

PICHON (neutral) A car was waiting. No plane, no train. He has simply gone.

PICHON (neutral) And he did not ask for his watch back.

Inès opens the back through the plastic with her thumbnail, just as she did on the Pont de Sully.

Inside the lid, a single line is engraved in a plain old script: VII de IX.

INÈS (thinking) The seventh of nine.

DEC (worried) So there are eight more.

PICHON (neutral) There are eight more. I'll be keeping this, as a reminder.

PICHON (neutral) I have a very bad memory.

Inès takes Lassalle's watch out of her bag, the one from the notary's secret drawer, and lays it beside the evidence bag.

INÈS (sad) Then keep Monsieur Lassalle's with it.

INÈS (sad) It belongs with the evidence, not in my bag.

Pichon puts both watches in his coat pocket, as carefully as a good bottle.

PICHON (angry) Now. Cheese, then tarte Tatin, then nineteen hours of sleep.

PICHON (angry) And neither of you will be in the next case of anything. You hear me?

DEC (happy) We hear you.

Pichon, who does not believe him, calls for the cheese.

Atelier Morel. Thursday, five o'clock.

Low gold light falls through the skylight. The Allegory is back on its easel with a strip of police tape across the frame. She will not paint the word back in, because she means to leave the gap honest.

At four, on the stairs, Mère Clothilde rang, crackling with snow and plum brandy. The commune has unbricked the dial, the old twelve-hour wheel is back, and it struck four, not thirteen. She sent a kiss for the not-husband, on the forehead.

On screen: Inès (in her apron before the painting, back to the room, thinking), Dec (on his knees at the door with a toolbox, the old lock half out, humming, happy)

DEC (happy) This is a terrible lock. It's a crime against doors.

DEC (happy) I'm putting in a five-lever mortice with a...

INÈS (neutral) Dec.

DEC (neutral) Hm?

INÈS (neutral) We're not partners.

The screwdriver stops.

DEC (sad) No.

INÈS (neutral) But this building has fourteen doors, and every lock is terrible.

INÈS (neutral) And I'll need somebody who can get into places.

She turns round at last.

INÈS (neutral) It's a job. That's all. It pays very badly.

DEC (thinking) How badly?

INÈS (neutral) Very.

DEC (thinking) Will there be tea?

INÈS (happy) There might be tea.

He grins the whole ridiculous Galway grin.

DEC (happy) Ah, go on, then. Purely professionally.

INÈS (happy) Professionally.

Four floors down, the street bell rings. It is a courier in a helmet, who will not come up and will not say who sent him.

He leaves a small square parcel in brown paper, addressed in neat grey ink: Mme I. Varga. M. D. Hartley. Atelier Morel.

Inès puts on her white cotton gloves and slits the paper with her scalpel.

In a nest of grey tissue lies an old enamel clock face the size of a saucer, with twelve marks and no hands. At the top is a black and careful IX.

Under it, in the same grey ink, is one word.

HUIT.

INÈS (surprised) Eight.

Across the rooftops of the Marais a church bell begins to strike six. She counts, and beside her, under his breath and in English, so does he.

They both stop at six, and look at each other.

DEC (thinking) Well. That's not a partnership. That's an invitation.

She puts the clock face back in its tissue, takes off her gloves, and pushes her glasses up with one knuckle.

INÈS (neutral) Get your coat.
