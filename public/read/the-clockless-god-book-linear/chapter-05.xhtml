Thirteen Strokes

The track to Combe-Noire. Friday, one in the afternoon.

A white groove runs up the valley between walls of black spruce. There is no sound but breathing, the creak of snowshoes, and now and then the soft thump of snow letting go of a branch.

On screen: Dec (snowshoes, borrowed boots too big, snow down his collar, worried), Inès (navy coat, mustard scarf, snowshoes, neutral)

DEC (worried) Snowshoes. Tennis rackets you strap on so you fall over more slowly.

INÈS (neutral) That's the fourth time you've said it. And you're wearing a dead geologist's boots.

DEC (happy) He's not dead. He went home in his socks.

DEC (happy) And I've not complained once.

INÈS (thinking) Fabien took the red train down to Delémont with a briefcase. Madame Jobin's nephew saw him.

INÈS (thinking) We have the afternoon.

They come out of the trees onto an open shelf of hillside. A broken grey wall lies iced with snow, and in its corner stands the tower, square and black, with a row of slits near the top like the gills of a fish.

High on its face hangs a great iron dial three metres across, with one iron hand pointing straight up.

INÈS (scared) Count the hours. There are only nine.

At the foot of the tower is an oak door studded with nails, its lock plate the size of a dinner plate.

DEC (happy) The one lock the foundation's man from Basel wasn't allowed to touch.

DEC (happy) Let's see if the commune protects its heritage.

The abbess's key burns cold through his glove. He rocks it, feeling for the levers, and deep in the door something groans like an old man getting out of a chair, and turns.

Inside the tower, the chamber of the Debt Clock. Afternoon.

The tower is hollow, a stone chimney lit grey from the slits. The old spiral stair is a row of broken teeth in the wall, and a brand-new steel ladder zigzags up past it, its bolts still bright.

At the top, through a trap in a timber floor, the clock fills the room. It is a cage of black iron bars the size of a garden shed, with huge hand-cut wheels rising into the gloom and a stone weight on a rusted chain dropping through the floor.

An iron box is riveted to the front of the cage. Set in its door is a brass cylinder of nine thin wheels, gone almost brown.

On screen: Dec (glove off, hand on the brass, surprised), Inès (at the trap, pale from the climb, worried)

DEC (surprised) It's the one in the drawings. The actual one.

DEC (surprised) Inès. Six hundred and seventy-five years.

INÈS (thinking) Can you open it without the word?

DEC (thinking) It's old and hand-filed, made in a hurry because the world was ending. The gates are never quite tight.

DEC (thinking) Pull the shackle a hair, turn a wheel slowly, and feel the gate go past the bar. Maybe.

DEC (thinking) Don't talk to me.

He shuts his eyes and turns the first wheel a letter at a time. Nothing happens, and nothing again, and then there is a give, less than a hair.

DEC (thinking) One.

At the ninth wheel comes a sound he feels more than hears. The bar drops, and the box door swings open an inch.

INÈS (thinking) What word did you open it with?

DEC (thinking) None. Q, F, Z, A, nonsense.

DEC (thinking) Somebody had the wheels off and put them back in a different order, long ago, after Vey died. I opened it by the gates.

Inside lie three things.

The first is a huge red-leather book, its back half burnt to a black crust. Inès turns the brown pages with her pencil.

INÈS (thinking) Vey's ledger. Horae solutae. The people of the valley, and their hours paid.

INÈS (surprised) Dec. The last page before the burn. His writing goes big and shaky.

INÈS (surprised) And the last word runs off into the char.

REMIT...

INÈS (worried) It's Latin. Remittere. To send back. To forgive a debt.

INÈS (worried) It's the start of a word, with R at the start. It fits the gap in the painting.

The other two things are new. One is a fat black notebook in neat, slanted blue biro, headed and underlined twice: ALS162. Essais de substitution locale.

DEC (worried) ALS162. Allouis. Roux's voice, the time signal all the clocks listen to.

INÈS (scared) Local substitution. Replacing it.

The last is a silver anti-static bag holding a green circuit board with a socket for an aerial. In white paint pen it says: ALS162 / PROTO 3 / 162 kHz / 9 min.

The notebook keeps a list of tests.

12/02. Bois de Vincennes. 400 m. 9 min. OK.

05/03. Quai de la Rapée. 1,2 km. 9 min. OK.

26/03. Gare d'Austerlitz + bd de l'Hôpital. 2,5 km. 07h51. 9 min. OK. Aucune réaction.

DEC (scared) That's Pichon's text. Yesterday morning, the station clocks.

DEC (scared) That was them. A test.

On the next page there is one line, written more carefully than the rest: Panthéon, galerie du dôme. 1er avril. 14h51 à 15h00. Neuf minutes. Rayon prévu: 5 km.

INÈS (scared) Five kilometres from the Panthéon. The whole centre of Paris.

INÈS (scared) Stations, traffic lights, hospital clocks. At three o'clock, in an eclipse, in the dark.

DEC (angry) And on the telly a man in a nice suit saying, look, the clocks have stopped, God's calling in the debt.

DEC (angry) It's not a miracle. It's a radio.

INÈS (worried) Photograph all of it. Every page. Mine's on four per cent.

He photographs page after page while she holds them flat with bare, freezing fingertips. Below them, the oak door groans open.

Feet come up the ladder. One pair is light and quick. The other is very slow and very heavy, so the whole ladder hums.

INÈS (scared) Phones away. Inside your clothes, not your pockets.

He shoves both phones down his jumper against his skin, and gasps.

On screen: Fabien (grey coat, snow on his shoulders, smooth calm face, neutral), Inès (notebook in hand, scared), Dec (scared)

FABIEN (neutral) Every hour is lent. Every hour lent is owed.

FABIEN (neutral) You're in a private building, Madame Varga. Monsieur Hartley.

DEC (worried) We had a key.

FABIEN (neutral) Of course you did.

On screen: Fabien (neutral), Kessler (stooping under the beams, huge, bald, tattoo moving as he swallows, neutral), Inès (scared)

Up through the trap, slowly, like something surfacing from deep water, comes the bald man. Fabien calls him Brother Kessler.

Kessler holds out his palm to Inès. He does not threaten her. He simply waits, as patient as a parent at the edge of a sandpit.

She puts the notebook in his hand.

FABIEN (neutral) And the board, please. And the book.

Dec hands over the board. Kessler lifts the ledger flat on both enormous palms, as if it were a sleeping animal, and carries it down.

Fabien shuts the box and spins the nine wheels with one sweep, zzzip, like shuffling cards. From the ladder, with only his head showing, he looks back.

FABIEN (neutral) Minus twenty tonight on the plateau, they say.

FABIEN (neutral) The Clockless One lends us every hour. Tonight, perhaps, you'll learn the price of one.

The trap bangs down. Underneath it comes the bright modern snap of a padlock on a hasp. Far below, the oak door groans, and the abbess's key turns from the outside.

DEC (sad) Well. That's my key gone.

The clock chamber. Night, minus twenty.

Snow blows through the slits in a fine dry powder that does not melt. The iron cage is a black shape against a darker one, and the one phone torch is switched off to save the battery.

On screen: Inès (huddled against the wall, knees in her coat, glasses frosted, scared), Dec (beside her, shoulder to shoulder, shivering, worried)

He has tried the trap for half an hour: with picks, with two picks tied together with a bootlace, and with a strip of brass off the clock frame. The padlock is underneath, out of reach.

DEC (sad) Give me a keyhole and a bit of time and I'll have anything.

DEC (sad) They've not given me a keyhole. They've given me a floor.

Inès's fingers will not bend. She feels heavy and warm and sleepy, and some part of her knows that is bad.

INÈS (scared) Talk to me. You always talk. Don't let me go to sleep.

He waves his hands in the dark and tells her a long story about a door in Salthill, a Mrs Kilbride, a cat called Pádraig and a misunderstanding about a sandwich.

DEC (neutral) Your turn. Tell me something true.

She is too cold and too tired to decide not to.

INÈS (sad) My father lost days, at the end. Alzheimer's. Zoltán.

INÈS (sad) He drove a bus in Lyon for thirty years and knew every street.

INÈS (sad) He taught me to swear in Hungarian and to look at things. Ne csak nézd, lásd. Don't just look. See.

INÈS (sad) He'd wake up thinking it was a Tuesday in nineteen ninety and get dressed for work.

INÈS (sad) Every morning I told him. Every morning it was new, and he was so frightened.

INÈS (angry) And these people do that on purpose. To an old clockmaker, a judge, a priest.

INÈS (angry) They take a day out of someone like a coin out of a fountain. And they make it a religion.

INÈS (sad) That's why I came.

DEC (sad) I'm sorry. About your dad.

DEC (sad) Mine dropped dead in the van outside a chipper on Shop Street, with the engine running.

DEC (sad) I was in Dublin. A proper job, a lanyard. I thought the shop was small.

DEC (sad) I went back and ran the shop into the ground in a year.

DEC (sad) Then I came to Paris, because there was nothing left in Galway with my name on it.

DEC (sad) Locking people in rooms. Because I couldn't get out of one.

INÈS (neutral) You're not a man who can't get out of a room.

INÈS (neutral) I watched you open that lock for forty minutes, without the word.

INÈS (happy) You're the most stubborn person I've ever met. And I've met my mother.

His shoulder stops shivering against hers for a moment. The quiet afterwards is the good kind.

DEC (neutral) We'll not mention any of this. After.

INÈS (neutral) Never.

The clock chamber. Half past ten at night.

Above them, through a square hole in the timber ceiling, a big green-black bell hangs in the dark beside the clock's iron hammer.

On screen: Inès (getting up on stiff legs, surprised), Dec (torch pointing up, worried)

INÈS (surprised) The bell. Dec, point the torch up.

Cast in the bronze round its lip are the words HORAM PVLSAT QVI TRAHIT.

Inès laughs out loud, a short cracked sound.

DEC (worried) What? What does it say?

INÈS (happy) The hour is struck by whoever winds. Whoever draws up the weight rings the bell.

INÈS (happy) Dec. Can you make this go?

On the floor under the square end of a winding shaft lies an iron crank, where some clock-keeper left it four hundred years ago. Slowly, like the sun coming up, Dec grins.

DEC (happy) Ah, now. Ah, now.

On screen: Dec (hands over hers on the crank, straining, happy), Inès (steamed glasses, sweat freezing in her hair, angry)

It takes both of them. Every turn draws the great stone weight up its chain a link at a time, clack, clack, with a ratchet like a huge slow heart. After a hundred turns they are no longer cold.

DEC (thinking) That's the striking weight. If I hold the lever, she keeps striking.

INÈS (scared) Then don't let go.

He reaches into the cage up to the shoulder, finds the lever and looks at her. She nods, and he pulls.

The hammer lifts, and falls.

BONG. It is a blow more than a sound, and she feels it in her teeth, her ribs and her frozen feet. Snow falls from the beams.

Seven. Eight. Nine. The sound goes out through the slits and down the valley, over the black trees and every sleeping roof.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

Dec lets go, and the silence afterwards rings.

Inès sits down against the cage, shaking. Her fingers find paper wedged under the bottom bar of the frame, shaken loose by the winding.

On screen: Inès (holding a charred parchment leaf, thinking), Dec (holding the torch, worried)

It is a page of Vey's ledger, its edge burnt black. Nine small seals run down it: a key, a wheel, a closed eye, scales, a bell, a ladder, an open hand, a mouth and a crown. Beside each is a numeral from I to IX, and a title.

INÈS (thinking) I. Custos Horae Ultimae. The Keeper of the Last Hour.

INÈS (scared) And beside the open hand, VII. Exactor. The Collector.

DEC (scared) The Seventh will collect.

INÈS (worried) These aren't people. They're offices, like a government.

INÈS (worried) Somebody holds each one. Now. Today.

Far down the valley an angry, buzzing engine is coming, and with it, sirens.

The clock chamber, then a fire brigade Land Rover. Twenty past eleven.

Bolt-cutters as long as an arm snap the padlock, and yellow helmets crowd the ladder. The whole volunteer fire brigade of Les Bréviaires has come, Madame Jobin's nephew among them, delighted. The bell has not rung since sixteen ten.

On screen: Clothilde (red ski jacket over her grey habit, snow on her short veil, thermos in hand, happy), Inès (wrapped in a foil blanket, sad), Dec (wrapped in foil, crank still on the shaft, happy)

CLOTHILDE (happy) Well. You've woken the dead. I suppose that's something.

CLOTHILDE (neutral) Drink. Don't argue.

It is coffee and plum brandy in equal parts. It goes down like a lit match and arrives, slowly, in their feet.

Later, in the back of the Land Rover with the heater roaring, Dec pulls his phone from inside his jumper. It is on six per cent. He goes through the photographs in silence.

On screen: Dec (phone lit in the dark, surprised), Inès (half asleep against the window in foil, neutral)

He stops on the very first one, the notebook's flyleaf. It has blank ruled lines, and in the top corner, in small neat slanted blue biro, a name, written as people write their names in case they leave a notebook on a train.

J.-P. Morel.

He holds the phone out to her without a word.

She knows that hand from four years of invoices and condition reports and little yellow notes on her bench. Inès, client called again, voilà, could you possibly...

INÈS (sad) Our restorer is close to the word.

INÈS (sad) Not me. Him.

She turns to the window and watches the snow go past, and she is silent all the way down to the village.
