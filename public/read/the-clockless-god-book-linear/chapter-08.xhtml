Totality

The Panthéon. Wednesday 1 April, seven in the morning.

A security guard in a black fleece studies a contractor's pass, then the man wearing it.

Behind the man stands a trolley of three wooden crates stencilled FRAGILE and CECI N'EST PAS UNE BOMBE. Seemed funny in the workshop in Montreuil.

On screen: Dec (all-zones pass round his neck, pushing the trolley, happy)

DEC (happy) Escape room. For the guests. Pour s'amuser.

DEC (worried) It's a joke. Magritte? Ceci n'est pas une pipe?

DEC (worried) Art, see.

The guard looks at the stencil, says it is a crate, and waves him through. Nobody has ever wanted to argue about Magritte at seven in the morning.

Inside, pale columns stand thick as oaks, and the dome rises so high its painted saints shrink to thumbnails.

From the crown a single steel cable drops, thin as pencil lead, to a brass sphere swinging over a brass ring. It passes with a soft deep rush of air, goes away, comes back.

Caterers carry in tables and a television crew lays cable. Behind a low grey stage with a lectern stands an easel draped in grey cloth.

On screen: Kessler (at the foot of the roped south stair to the dome, sign reading ACCÈS INTERDIT, still as a piece of the building, neutral), Dec (across the nave, gripping his trolley, worried)

Kessler looks at Dec, and Dec looks back. The pendulum swings past between them, and comes back.

The Clockmaker's Cell, north-east chapel of the crypt. A quarter past eleven.

The room is three metres by four, stone-effect walls, a bench of brass gears, a parchment window and a door with a real iron lock.

On the wall hangs a clock with nine hours on its face. Was in the brief.

On screen: Dec (flat on his stomach, arm down a hatch in the floor, thinking)

DEC (thinking) Nine hours on the dial. Thought that was the client being quirky.

Through the maintenance hatch he built in February, he reaches down past the new timber to the old iron grille.

Its lock is as rusted as it looked from below, and takes him nine minutes. He counts them, because he counts everything in nines now.

The bolt slides back. He leaves the grille closed but unlocked, and puts the hatch back over it.

DEC (neutral) A way out. Nobody'll need it.

DEC (neutral) But it's there.

The catering chapel off the south transept. Two in the afternoon.

Behind a screen stand steel urns of ordinary vin chaud, and the whole chapel smells of cloves.

At the far end of the trestle table, on their own, wait four tall pewter jugs of a wine that is almost brown, with a grey sheen like the bloom on a plum.

Beside them are a hundred little pewter cups engraved with a clock, IX at the top, and a card: Le vin de la Veillée.

On screen: Fabien (in a caterer's jacket that does not suit him, back to the room, neutral), Dec (in the doorway with a clipboard, scared)

DEC (scared) That narrow back. I'd know it anywhere.

He turns away before Fabien can turn, and walks straight into a tall waiter with a tray.

On screen: Moussa (tall and gangly, shaved head, badge reading MOUSSA, tray under his arm, surprised), Dec (white as paper, worried)

MOUSSA (surprised) Pardon, monsieur. Ça va? You're white.

DEC (neutral) I'm Irish. We're always white.

DEC (worried) The brown wine. When does it go out?

MOUSSA (thinking) Half past two. Only to the guests on the list.

MOUSSA (neutral) Staff drink it, we're sacked.

MOUSSA (worried) This morning he put something in the jugs from a paper bag. Grey, like ash.

MOUSSA (worried) What is it?

Dec thinks of six lies and is too tired for any of them.

DEC (worried) A drug. Makes you lose a day.

DEC (worried) The minister drinks it, remembers what he's told, and says it on the telly.

DEC (scared) I need to swap the jugs before half two, and keep one full for the police.

DEC (scared) Can't do it alone.

MOUSSA (neutral) How much do you think I'm paid for this?

DEC (sad) Not enough.

MOUSSA (angry) Eleven euros forty. And he shouted at Fatou this morning about the napkins.

MOUSSA (neutral) What do you need?

At 14:22 a crate stencilled CECI N'EST PAS UNE BOMBE goes over in the passage, with great force and theatrical surprise.

Four hundred brass gears pour across the stone and under the table, ringing like every coin in the world dropped at once.

Every waiter goes down on hands and knees, and Fabien goes down with them, white with fury.

Dec has forty seconds, and he has built whole puzzles out of forty seconds.

The pewter jugs go into his empty crate. In their place stand four jugs that Moussa filled from the urns and darkened with a spoonful of cocoa until they went a dull brown-grey.

Fabien stands up red-faced with his hands full of gears. The jugs stand where they always stood, and Moussa is apologising at length in French and Wolof.

On screen: Dec (sitting on the closed crate behind the screen, phone in hand, worried)

At half past two the trays go out to the minister, the prefect, the cardinal's secretary, the bankers and the famous presenter with the famous tan.

They admire the little clocks, and drink.

His phone buzzes. Where are you. Pichon.

He types: Inside. Swapped the wine. 4 jugs of the real thing in a crate, south transept. Need you. Now.

The answer comes at once: Lunch.

A second later, another: Coming.

The nave of the Panthéon. Twenty to three.

The light drains as if someone were turning down a dimmer on the whole of Paris, and the gold beams go a thin, sick silver.

In the open bronze doors, the crowd in the square falls quiet. A station clock hung for the vigil, with FONDATION VAULMONT in gold beneath it, says 14:40.

On screen: Vaulmont (at the lectern, pendulum cable rising behind him, camera light on, neutral), Inès (dark green dress, mustard scarf, clipboard held to her chest beside the draped easel, worried)

VAULMONT (neutral) Time is not ours.

His voice is warm oil, poured slowly, filling the nave.

VAULMONT (neutral) We speak of spending it.

VAULMONT (neutral) Of wasting it. Of saving it.

VAULMONT (happy) And we are right to.

VAULMONT (neutral) Every hour of every life is lent.

Inès lifts her eyes once as the pendulum passes and finds Dec behind his column in the south aisle. One look, one second: that is the arrangement.

On screen: Fabien (earpiece wire at his collar, scanning the columns, angry), Kessler (behind the red cord, huge, neutral), Dec (half behind a column, scared)

Fabien comes quickly up the south aisle, his narrow face searching the crowd and the columns until it finds Dec's, and stops.

He lifts a hand to the wire at his collar and speaks.

At the foot of the dome stair, Kessler's great bald head turns slowly towards the south aisle.

When Dec looks back at the stage, the clipboard lies on the easel and Inès is gone.

He finds her in the north aisle, walking without hurry towards the narrow service stair behind the pillar.

It climbs sixty metres, round and round inside the wall, with a rope for a handrail. It is the stair she is most afraid of.

She sets her grey boot on the first step and goes up into the dark.

The north service stair, then the gallery of the dome. A quarter to three.

The stone spiral is barely wider than her shoulders, with a caged bulb every twenty steps.

On the inner side there is a hand's-width gap at every step, and darkness going down.

On screen: Inès (one hand on the greasy rope, forehead to the cold wall, scared)

INÈS (scared) Forty-one. Forty-two.

INÈS (scared) One step, one number. No down.

INÈS (angry) Basszus.

INÈS (sad) Saint-Jean, in Lyon. I was nine.

INÈS (sad) He sat on the step and sang about a horse until I stopped shaking.

INÈS (sad) Then he asked: up or down? Down, I said.

INÈS (angry) I'm not nine.

INÈS (scared) A hundred and forty. A hundred and forty-one.

The stair opens onto a stone ring high inside the dome. A waist-high balustrade runs round it, with netting strung between the balusters, and beyond it hang sixty metres of darkening air.

Far below, the pendulum's sphere crosses its ring, no bigger than a pinhead.

From the crown, a second cable, black and thick, runs down the curve of the dome on new brackets to a tall brass cabinet like an antique wardrobe.

On its domed top sits a handless clock with IX at the top. Its doors carry nine thin wheels, green light leaks from the seams, and there is a hum she feels in her teeth.

On screen: Inès (left hand on the wall, eyes on the stone, inching round the gallery, scared)

VAULMONT (neutral) ...and when the light is taken from us...

VAULMONT (neutral) ...we will see what we truly owe...

She reaches the cabinet at 14:47. The letters round the wheels are crisp and perfect. This is Roux's work, and there is no play in it at all.

She grips her shaking right wrist with her left hand, as she does when she lays gold leaf on a halo, and turns the first wheel.

INÈS (thinking) R.

Behind her, from the direction of the south stair, come footsteps.

The gallery of the dome. Totality.

Kessler comes round the curve out of the dim. He stoops under the arch and turns his shoulders to fit between wall and rail, filling the gallery as he filled Lassalle's doorway and the hatch of the tower.

On screen: Kessler (six metres off, hands loose, small pale eyes on her, neutral), Inès (backing round the ring, trembling from teeth to knees, scared)

She backs away from the cabinet. He follows one slow step at a time, as if she were a cat he didn't want to frighten into the road.

VAULMONT (neutral) ...nine minutes, my friends. Only nine.

VAULMONT (neutral) The hour of None...

Below, a hundred voices gasp. Outside, thousands sigh like wind in trees.

The light goes out. The dome turns a deep, glowing blue, with a ring of pale fire in the windows where the sun was.

Down the rue Soufflot the traffic lights die and come back flashing red. The clock on the Lycée Henri-IV goes blank and comes back at nine.

In the nave, the vigil clock's minute hand swings all the way round by itself, and stops at nine.

Phones glow like fireflies. Someone cries out that every board at Montparnasse says nine, and Lyon's too, and the hospitals'.

VAULMONT (happy) Mutuo datum.

VAULMONT (happy) Reddetur.

Something explodes into her face, all wings and dust. Her hip hits the balustrade, and for one endless second there is nothing on her right but sixty metres of blue.

Her hands find a baluster. She is on her knees with her arms round it like a child round a parent's leg, her glasses hanging off one ear.

It is a pigeon, caught by a wing and a foot in the netting and beating itself against the stone.

Inès lets go with one hand. For a few seconds the drop simply goes away, because here is something more frightened than she is.

She folds its wings and works the mesh off its foot, then off the wing. The bird lets her.

Kessler has stopped two metres away, and his head is turned towards the bird.

She holds the pigeon out to him in both hands, palms up, as he held out his hand to her in the tower, and she waits.

Two great hands close round the bird over her fingers, very gently. For a moment her hands are inside his, and they are warm. Then he has it.

He stands quite still with the pigeon against his chest and his head bent over it, like a man in church, and leaves the gallery open for her.

Inès slips past him, between his shoulder and the rail, close enough to touch. His hands stay round the bird. Then she is past him, and running.

On screen: Dec (bursting from the far stairhead, torch swinging, gasping, auburn hair stuck to his forehead, scared), Inès (running along the wall towards him, scared)

DEC (scared) Inès!

INÈS (angry) The cabinet, behind you!

INÈS (angry) It's Roux's lock. You can't pick it.

INÈS (angry) Use the word!

His torch finds the brass doors, the nine wheels and the clock with no hands. She sees him understand.

On screen: Vaulmont (rising unhurried from the arch behind Dec, a small dark pistol loose in one grey-gloved hand, neutral), Dec (facing the cabinet, scared), Inès (stopped dead, scared)

In the swinging light she sees a third way down: a low iron door in the east pier, standing open, with a conservators' stair going down inside it to the nave.

Down there are the microphone, the camera, and the painting with the word scraped out of it.

INÈS (thinking) You look at the door. I look at the painting.

She turns in at the iron door and goes down the stair two steps at a time, into the dark.
