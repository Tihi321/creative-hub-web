Every Clock Says Nine

Salon, a winter evening

Ambience: hundreds of clocks ticking out of step, low voices, a string quartet far off

Forty clocks cover the cream walls, gilt and walnut and porcelain, and every one of them ticks to its own idea of time.

Gold chairs face an auctioneer's lectern. Beside it, a portrait waits on an easel under a grey cloth.

On screen: Inès (navy trench coat, mustard scarf, sharp), Dec (leather jacket, satchel, delighted)

DEC (happy) Listen to that. It's like standing inside a very rich man's pocket.

INÈS (neutral) Don't touch anything.

DEC (thinking) I wasn't going to touch anything. I was going to look at the case on that carriage clock. Lovely little lever lock. Swiss, I'd say. You could open it with a hairpin and a bad attitude.

INÈS (neutral) You were going to touch it.

DEC I was going to admire it at close range.

Inès pushes her glasses up with one knuckle and looks at the covered easel the way other people look at a dessert trolley.

INÈS (thinking) Monsieur Moreau wants me to say his portrait is a Fragonard. If it is, I say so. If it is not, I say that too, and we leave quickly.

DEC And I'm here as what, exactly?

INÈS My plus-one. Stand near the canapés and say nothing in French.

DEC (happy) My French is grand.

INÈS Yesterday you told the baker you were a croissant.

On screen: Inès (amused), Dec (wounded), Madame Vérac (black suit, pearls, smiling)

A slim woman in a black trouser suit glides between the chairs. An ivory gavel swings on a chain at her belt.

MADAME VÉRAC (happy) Mademoiselle Varga, at last. I am Solange Vérac. I sell, my dear, and you tell me what I am selling.

INÈS Good evening, Madame.

MADAME VÉRAC And this tall gentleman with the satchel?

DEC Dec Hartley. Locksmith. Well, escape rooms now. People pay me to lock them in. It's a long story with a short moral.

MADAME VÉRAC (happy) How marvellous. Then you will keep an eye on our last lot for me. It is the only one with a lock.

She pats Dec's arm with a white cotton glove and is gone before he can ask what she means.

On screen: Inès (thinking), Dec (worried), Baron Ostrander (green frock coat, monocle, scowling)

Sound: a cane striking a parquet floor

A big man in a bottle-green frock coat taps his way along the front row. The rubber tip of his cane leaves small black commas on the polished floor.

BARON OSTRANDER (angry) Thirty years! Thirty years I wait for Moreau to sell me the writing boy, and tonight he puts it in a glass case and calls it "not for sale".

DEC Evening. Dec Hartley.

BARON OSTRANDER What?

DEC (louder) Dec Hartley.

BARON OSTRANDER I do not hear that side. Baron Ostrander. Collector of automata. Victim of Gaspard Moreau since nineteen ninety-six.

INÈS What happened in nineteen ninety-six?

BARON OSTRANDER (angry) He outbid me in Copenhagen with money he did not have. In my own city. With my own grandmother watching.

He unscrews a silver flask, sniffs it, thinks better of it, and screws it shut again.

On screen: Inès (neutral), Dec (curious), Rémy Castel (striped apron, flour on sleeves, gloomy)

A thin man in a white chef's jacket and a striped apron offers a tray of tiny tarts with the face of someone serving at a funeral.

RÉMY CASTEL (sad) Tarte au citron. The last of the lemons. Monsieur did not pay for the next ones.

DEC (happy) Thanks. You're the cook?

RÉMY CASTEL Cook. Butler. Gardener when it rains. Thirty years.

INÈS And tonight?

RÉMY CASTEL (sad) Tonight I am everything, and I am paid nothing. Three years of nothing. Butter, sugar, two eggs, zest.

DEC (thinking) Sorry?

RÉMY CASTEL The recipe. I say it when I am nervous.

On screen: Inès (alert), Dec (alert), Moreau (velvet smoking jacket, half-moon spectacles, gleeful)

Sound: an ivory gavel knocking three times on wood

A small bald man in a burgundy smoking jacket climbs onto the lectern step so that he is nearly as tall as the lectern.

MOREAU (happy) Friends, rivals, creditors. Tonight, a portrait, nine fine clocks, and one lot that is not in the catalogue.

MOREAU A relic so old that its owners deny it exists. Lot forty. The Ninth Key.

A murmur runs along the gold chairs. Madame Vérac keeps smiling, but her gloved hand closes on her left cuff.

INÈS (thinking) The Ninth Key. Dec, the Brotherhood.

DEC (worried) I heard. I'm trying very hard not to have heard.

MOREAU (happy) It will be shown at half past nine, after the portrait, after the champagne, after I have made you all very nervous. Excuse me. I must check on my orchids.

He hops down and trots away through a glass door at the end of the salon. Cold air breathes out behind him.

Salon, nine o'clock

Champagne. Small talk. Forty clocks gather themselves for the hour.

On screen: Inès (thinking), Dec (holding two tarts)

DEC Three to nine. Here's the bit I like. Forty clocks, all striking together. It's like a choir that hates each other.

INÈS They will not strike together. Nobody keeps forty clocks to the same second.

The minute hands reach the top.

Sound: forty clocks giving one tick together, then nothing

Every clock in the salon stops. The pendulums hang still. The silence is so sudden that someone's champagne glass rings against a tooth.

DEC (scared) That's not right. That's not mechanically right.

INÈS (worried) Every one of them says nine.

DEC You'd need to reach inside every case at the same moment. Or have set them all hours ago, hands and all, every one put right to the same second, like a, like a...

INÈS Like a trap.

From the glass door at the end of the salon comes a scream, and then the sound of a silver tray falling on gravel.

Winter garden, a few minutes past nine

Ambience: a fountain trickling, wind against glass panes

Frost feathers the glass roof. Palms and white orchids lean over a gravel path. Lanterns glow; the moon is cold above them.

Picture: Moreau lies still on a wrought-iron bench among the white orchids in the winter garden, his spectacles fallen on the gravel, as Rémy Castel stands frozen beside a dropped silver tray.

RÉMY CASTEL (scared) Monsieur. Monsieur Moreau. Flour, butter, salt, cold water.

RÉMY CASTEL (scared) The clocks stopped, and I brought his tisane out, and he was already like this.

On screen: Inès (pale, steady), Dec (horrified), Rémy Castel (trembling)

Inès kneels by the bench without touching the man on it. She looks at the cravat, the hands, the gravel.

INÈS (sad) He is dead. And not long ago.

DEC (scared) He was showing off ten minutes ago. He was standing on a step.

Inès stands and turns slowly, looking at the room as if it were a damaged painting.

INÈS (thinking) Nothing touched. Nobody moved him. He died here.

INÈS (sad) Whoever did this did it here, in the winter garden.

Evidence: Moreau's gold pocket watch, open in his hand, its glass cracked and its hands stopped at nine.

DEC Even his watch. That's not a coincidence, that's a signature.

DEC (worried) I'm going to check the outside door. If someone came in from the courtyard, the lock will say so.

INÈS Dec.

DEC Two seconds. Locks talk to me.

He crouches by the iron door to the courtyard and unrolls the cutlery. It is exactly then that the salon door opens behind him.

Winter garden, half past nine

On screen: Pichon (beige raincoat, napkin in collar, chewing), Dec (caught, lock picks in hand), Inès (exasperated)

A round man in a rumpled raincoat stands in the doorway, a dinner napkin still tucked into his collar and a crumb of pastry on his moustache.

PICHON (angry) A dead man, forty stopped clocks, and Monsieur Hartley kneeling at a door with burglar's tools. I left a blanquette for this.

DEC (worried) Commissaire. Funny story.

PICHON It is never funny, Monsieur Hartley. It is only ever a story.

INÈS He was checking the lock. It was locked from the inside. Nobody came in from the courtyard.

PICHON (thinking) How convenient that the locksmith says so.

He holds out a hand. Dec, with great sorrow, rolls up the cutlery and gives it to him.

PICHON You are under arrest. Provisionally. In principle. Sit on that bench and do not explain anything to me about tumblers.

DEC (sad) They're levers, actually.

PICHON (angry) Sit.

Salon, ten o'clock

The guests have been herded back among the silent clocks. Two officers write in notebooks. Pichon eats a tart while he reads.

On screen: Pichon (reading glasses on, notebook), Inès (attentive)

PICHON My sergeant has made a list of where the things of this house were at nine. Lemons, chairs, a cord, a bottle. The French police are thorough.

INÈS May I hear it?

PICHON (thinking) You may not. But I am reading aloud because I am old.

Clue: "At nine o'clock," Pichon reads, "either the curtain cord or the poisoned absinthe was in the kitchen."

INÈS Poisoned?

PICHON A bottle of absinthe that smells of bitter almonds. Someone meant to poison someone. Perhaps everyone. It is that kind of house.

On screen: Pichon (chewing), Inès (crouching, looking at the floor), Baron Ostrander (outraged)

Inès crouches by the glass door. The black commas of the Baron's cane run across the salon floor and stop at the threshold.

Rémy raked the winter garden's gravel at eight, smooth as a tablecloth. Every footstep since shows, and a cane tip would leave a deep round hole. There is not one.

BARON OSTRANDER (angry) Why is that woman looking at my stick?

INÈS Because it writes everywhere you go, Baron.

Clue: The gravel raked at eight holds no hole from the cane: Baron Ostrander was not in the winter garden.

BARON OSTRANDER (happy) You see? I was not even in the room with his orchids. I am innocent. Also I am deaf on that side.

PICHON Nobody is innocent, Baron. Some people are only slower to be guilty.

Kitchen, half past ten

Ambience: a black range ticking as it cools, a tap dripping

Copper pans hang over a long scrubbed table. A half-made tart waits under a cloth. The small window is misted, frost at its corners.

On screen: Inès (quiet, kind), Rémy Castel (sitting, apron in his fists)

Inès finds Rémy alone, staring at the range as if it might explain something.

INÈS You found him.

RÉMY CASTEL (sad) I took him his tisane. Every night at nine. Thirty years.

INÈS Rémy. The commissaire will ask what you were holding tonight. Tell me first.

He twists the apron. He looks at the copper pans. He mutters something about shortcrust.

RÉMY CASTEL (worried) I carried many things. Trays. Coats. Lemons.

INÈS At nine.

Clue: "At nine I had either the brass carriage clock or the curtain cord in my hands," Rémy Castel admits. "I will not say which. It will sound bad either way."

INÈS (thinking) It does.

RÉMY CASTEL (sad) Monsieur owed me three years. That also sounds bad.

INÈS Yes.

Salon, eleven o'clock

On screen: Dec (sitting on a gold chair, cuffed to it, gloomy), Inès (arriving), Pichon (pocketing the cutlery)

DEC They've cuffed me to a Louis Quinze chair. I could get out of this with a paperclip. I'm not going to, because I'm a very good person.

INÈS You are a very cuffed person.

PICHON The tools stay with me, Monsieur Hartley. Tomorrow we will talk about why every lock in your life opens.

On screen: Dec (gloomy), Inès (looking past him at the dead clocks, determined), Madame Vérac (smiling at the far end of the salon)

At the far end of the room Madame Vérac is telling an officer a funny story. She laughs. Her white gloves are folded neatly in her lap.

INÈS (thinking) Three people were somewhere at nine, holding something.

DEC Inès. You've got the face.

INÈS Which face?

DEC The restoring face. The one where you take a painting apart until it tells you who painted it.

Inès pushes her glasses up with one knuckle and turns towards the dark winter garden.

INÈS (determined) Then tonight I restore a murder.
