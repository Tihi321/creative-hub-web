Silence Protocol

The dark had a weight to it that no lamp could match.

Neve sat at the sonar console with her headphones on and her eyes closed. The Cormorant hung four kilometres down, and for the second time since the descent the boat was not making war on the trench. No active array. No lamps in the water. Only the small sounds a submarine makes when it tries to be quiet and cannot quite manage it: the tick of the hull cooling, the whisper of the scrubbers, the faint high note of the pumps turning over in their sleep.

She kept her eyes closed because the console told her nothing her ears did not. The cold came up through the deck plates into her boots. Somewhere behind her Kofi breathed, and the scrubber breathed back.

Beside her, Lian wrote on her tablet in the dark with the screen turned down to nothing.

“How long?” Rivas asked. He had asked it four times.

“Nearly two hours since we cut the array,” Lian said. “It stopped tapping twice. Now it’s not tapping at all.”

“Stopped,” Rivas said. “Meaning what?”

“Meaning it listened,” Neve said. “And then it got bored, or it got full, or it decided we were no longer worth the trouble. All three are the same thing from here. It’s not answering because there’s nothing to answer.”

“And if it’s just resting,” Rivas said. “Gathering itself.”

“Then we’ll hear it gather,” Neve said. “We hear everything in this trench now, Sal, and it hears nothing from us. That’s the trade we made.”

“The pings,” Kofi said from the rear of the cabin, where he sat with his back against the patched frame and a roll of electrical tape in his hands like a rosary. “What about the pings? The Halcyon’s still singing.”

Neve looked at the clock. She had been counting it the way Rivas counted oxygen, because it was the same kind of number and she had learned to count it from him. The dial said 08:10 and fifty seconds.

She had known it would happen. Achebe had said she would stop her pings when Rivas ordered it, and Achebe had meant it. Women like Achebe always meant it. And then somebody on that boat had looked at the air numbers, or the battery numbers, or the settling stone above their heads, and had reached for the switch, because a submarine in distress has one instrument that tells it where the world is, and it is hard to sit in the dark with a broken boat and not ask the water a question.

The Halcyon pinged at 08:11.

It came through the passive array as a clean bright note, small and hopeful, and Neve heard it in her headphones and felt it in her teeth. She had heard that note a hundred times since the descent, but this was the first time she had heard it alone, with no answer of their own to cover it, and it sounded naked. Like a voice calling in a house that has gone quiet.

Out in the dark the sound went out and touched the trench wall and the floor and the thing below. It came back. And after it came something else. A reply. Low. Slow. Patient.

The hull did not move. Neve noticed that the way you notice a step that is not there: the reply came back and the Cormorant did not shudder, and Kofi, who had laid both hands on the patch out of pure habit, looked up with an expression of surprise that was almost comic on a face like his.

“It’s not…” he said. “Doctor, it’s not hitting us. That was a ping and it answered, and the boat’s fine.”

“Because we’re not adding to it,” Neve said. “It answered Halcyon with Halcyon’s own sound. We didn’t put anything in, so there was nothing to come back at us.” She pressed the headphones tighter and shut her eyes, and counted the beats under her breath. “Lian. What’s it doing with the pattern?”

Lian was silent a moment, and then she made a small unhappy sound, a sound Neve did not like at all. “It’s learning. The same way it learned ours. It’s taking the Halcyon’s ping and running it, over and over, and the gaps are getting shorter.”

“How much shorter?”

“Two seconds between phrases when it started. Now one. It’s picking up speed.”

Rivas turned in his seat. “Talk to me in plain words. What does that mean?”

“It means the Halcyon is feeding it,” Neve said. “On Achebe’s order or against it, someone has put that sonar back on, and every ninety seconds a clean bright ping goes down into the trench, and the thing down there takes it and practises it and gets faster. It’s not a rescue signal any more, Commander. It’s a metronome. And every beat makes the thing below grow more interested.”

“Interested how?”

“The way a thing is interested in a warm hand in cold water.” She took the headphones off and set them around her neck. “I don’t know its metabolism. I don’t know if it’s growing. All I can tell you is that the phone keeps ringing and I hear it answering from closer each time. That is not a thing that will stop on its own.”

“Then tell them to stop,” Rivas said.

“I have been trying. The acoustic phone works. It’s been working since the tow. The trouble isn’t that the channel is shut, it’s that the channel is loud. The phone’s a transducer on the sail, Commander. It puts a voice out into the water the same way the array puts out a ping, and the thing below doesn’t care whether the sound is a number or a sentence. I have called Achebe three times and each time I stopped before I finished the word, because I could hear in the passive array what my own voice was doing down there.”

“You didn’t tell me that,” Rivas said.

“I’m telling you now.”

“You should have told me ten minutes ago.”

“Ten minutes ago you were counting air and I was counting what I could hear.” She held his eyes. “We don’t have a phone we can use, Sal. We have a phone that will work. Those are different things. A whisper through a megaphone is still a shout.”

Rivas was quiet. Then he said, “Then we go over and knock on the door.”

“With what? Our thrusters are surging, our frame is strapped, and the last time we moved we made the crack worse.” She heard her own voice rise and did not stop it; a flat voice was a luxury and she had spent it. “And if we go active to find the Halcyon in the dark, we are ringing the bell again. I do not want to ring the bell again.”

“So we do nothing,” Rivas said, “and listen to it ring the bell for us.”

“For now,” Neve said. “Until we find another way.”

He turned back to the console and began to go through the numbers with his pen, the way a man does when the numbers are all he has, and Neve sat with the dark pressed against the viewport and thought about all the ways a thing could be silent.

It came at the top of the next hour, and it came from Kofi, who had been down in the bilge because a man with nothing to do and a broken boat will find something to do with the broken boat.

He came up through the galley hatch backwards, which was the only way up through the galley hatch, and his hands were black to the wrist and he had a length of cable over one shoulder like a man bringing in a rope from a rising tide.

“Commander,” Kofi said. “The number two thruster’s been surging because the controller’s confused, not because the motor’s bad. It’s been taking its timing off the sonar clock. Every time the array clocked, the controller got a hiccup, and every time it hiccupped, the thruster burped.” He looped the cable, checked it, and looked up. “I can cut the sonar clock out. Run the controller on its own crystal. Steady as a rock, and quiet as one, too.”

“Do it,” Rivas said.

“And there’s a second thing.” Kofi crouched by the console and lowered his voice, which with Kofi meant he had been thinking the whole way up the ladder. “The work lamp on the belly. It’s on its own circuit, it doesn’t touch the array, and it’s a light, Commander. Nothing down here can hear a lamp.”

“So?”

Kofi looked at Neve.

She understood before he finished. “Halcyon’s got her own lamp burning. She’s been running a work lamp on the sail since the first hour. If it’s still on, she can see ours.”

“And if she can see ours,” Kofi said, “she can read it. Flashes. Long and short. Morse, if they’ve got a signaller awake.”

Rivas leaned forward, and Neve watched the thought cross his face and take hold of it, the way a man takes hold of a rail on a moving deck.

“You want to talk to a submarine that’s been pinging for two days,” he said slowly, “at four kilometres, in the dark, with a lamp.”

“You want to keep letting it ping,” Neve said. “We don’t have a phone we can use without feeding the thing. We do have a lamp. The question isn’t whether we can speak to Halcyon. The question is how loud a whisper we can manage.”

“One flash,” Rivas said. “One. That’s my condition.”

“One flash, then listen,” Neve said. “And then we decide what comes next together.”

That was the thing that caught him. She saw it in the way he sat back, the way his eyes moved from her face to Kofi’s and back. It cost her something to say it, and he heard the cost.

But he was not done. He pulled up the log, and the small screen lit his face from below, and Neve saw her own name on it, and his, from that morning.

“We did this once already,” he said. “The log’s got it. Biologist’s recommendation, accepted by the commander, both our names. I’m not going to write a fresh version of a thing I already signed.” He turned the screen toward her. “But a light is a new decision, and I want it logged the same way. If this goes badly, the recommendation is yours and the acceptance is mine, and both of us say so in the book.”

“Agreed,” Neve said. “Log it the same way.”

He typed it out with one finger, then looked up, and his voice changed.

“Doctor,” he said, “I’ve got eleven people on that boat and four on this one, and I’ve got a commander’s oath that says a rescue is loud, and your word that a rescue is quiet, and a crack in my frame that says one of you is wrong. You’ve given me a theory, and a rock, and a metronome. What I don’t have is a reason to trust the theory over the frame.” He tapped the console once with his pen. “Tell me why the scientist in this boat is worth listening to on the day the hull goes.”

The cabin was very quiet. Neve heard the scrubbers and her own pulse and, faint and far, the Halcyon’s ping going down into the dark, ninety seconds on the dot.

“I’ll tell you,” she said.

She moved so that she was facing him, set her hands together in her lap, and made herself say it in order, the way you log a thing.

“Eight years ago I was on a survey in the Southern Ocean,” she said. “We were running a towed camera near a seal colony. There was a species we didn’t have a name for then. We have one now. A benthic worm, blind and pink and about the length of my hand, growing in beds on the ledge where the seals hauled out. Harmless, very nearly the most harmless thing in the water. It couldn’t bite, it couldn’t sting, it had no skeleton worth the name.”

Rivas had put the pen down.

“We had a bad sonar return one afternoon,” she went on. “A dense thing, moving slow, too slow for a fish, too fast for a bed. I was young, and I was tired, and I had the log in my hand, and I had to choose whether we took the camera down into a blind current or cleared the area. I looked at the return and I saw an animal in it. Something big. Something with a shape that could take a seal.” She felt the old cold at the back of her neck. “I called it a threat. I said, get clear, get the divers out, get everyone up.”

“And?” Rivas said.

“And the divers came up too fast. Two of them came up too fast.” She said it flatly, because flatness was the only mercy she had ever been able to give it. “One did not come up at all. And the thing on the sonar was a benthic bed. A worm bank. It had a shape, and I gave the shape a name, and I named it before I had looked at it properly. The panic was mine. I made it out of a return I did not understand, and it killed a man, and what I learned is what I have been saying to you all day.” She looked at him. “I will not name a thing before I have looked at it. That is my whole method, and my whole argument. I have looked at the thing below. It has chosen, more than once, in the only language it has, not to hurt us.”

The cabin was silent. Kofi had put the cable down. Lian had taken her hand off the stylus, and she was looking at Neve with an expression that was not pity, which Neve could have borne, but recognition, which was harder.

“That’s a hard thing,” Rivas said at last. Not gentle. Not cruel. Just exact.

“It is.”

“And you’re telling me so I’ll follow you,” he said. “Because I’m the man with the frame, and you’re the woman with the frame.”

“I’m telling you so that if I am wrong, you will know what wrong looks like on me. I made a decision before the looking. I am not going to make one again. This is the looking. This is the part of the job I am good at. The part where I wait, and listen, and do not flinch first.”

Rivas looked at the oxygen panel, where the number sat, patient and small, and Neve watched him read it and do the arithmetic he had been doing all day.

“Twenty hours and change,” he said. “That’s the Cormorant. And Halcyon’s still got better than thirty.” He said it aloud so the whole boat heard it and the log had it, and then he let it go. “One flash. On the belly lamp, toward Halcyon. Then we wait. If they answer, we keep going. If they don’t, I go active and take my chances, and we log that too. Kofi. Get the lamp hot and rig a shutter I can work from here.”

“A shutter,” Kofi said.

“A thing that goes open and shut. You built a coffee maker out of a sonar housing. A shutter you can do.”

Kofi thought about it. Then he pulled the electrical tape out of his pocket, tore three long strips with his teeth, and laid them over the face of the work lamp controller in a cross, leaving a gap in the middle the width of a coin.

“Not a shutter,” he said. “A shadow with a hole in it. But it’ll make a spot of light, and I can turn the lamp on and off behind it, and that’s a flash. Commander, when I say go, you count.”

“Count what?”

“Count three.”

At 08:58, Cormorant spoke, for the first time since the silence, in light.

Kofi rigged the work lamp on the belly with the tape shadow and fed its cable up through the galley hatch, and Rivas worked the power switch from the pilot’s seat. Neve stood where she could see out across the debris field, where the Halcyon’s work lamp burned like a coal dropped in a field of snow, burning its emergency battery down for no reason but hope.

“Say when,” Kofi said.

“When,” Neve said.

The belly lamp opened its eye, and a long low beam went out across the trench floor and found the Halcyon’s sail and lit it, and for a moment the debris field stood in the light like a room with the curtains pulled back. Neve saw the pale hull and the tow line still fast to her bow, the bent sail, the scar the bow had drawn in the sediment, the little red smear of the swimmer’s marker line still fixed to the railing. Then Kofi closed the tape over the lamp face, and the light went out, and the dark came back like water into a footprint.

“One,” Rivas said.

Out across the floor, the Halcyon’s lamp burned steady.

“Two,” he said.

Nothing. The orange eye did not move. The scrubber turned over and the fan went round and the boat held its breath, and Kofi’s tape roll creaked in his hand as he rolled it tighter without meaning to.

“Three.”

And the Halcyon’s lamp blinked. Once. Then twice. Then a pause, and then once again, slow and deliberate, the way a person blinks when they have understood.

“They’re reading us,” Lian said, and her voice cracked at the top like ice on a puddle. “They’ve got someone on the sail.”

The next minute was the longest in the boat. Neve counted with her eyes on the orange eye across the dark, and her mouth had gone dry. At thirty the lamp was steady. At forty-five it was steady. At sixty the passive array gave her the Halcyon’s ping, clean and bright, and then gave her nothing at all, and she did not breathe.

The ping came once more. And then it stopped.

Ninety seconds. Another ninety seconds. The clock turned over and the numbers changed and no ping came, and the silence that filled the boat was a different silence from the one they had been keeping. It was the silence of a thing completed.

“It’s over,” Lian said. “They’ve gone quiet.”

Rivas was watching the viewport, and out across the debris field the orange eye blinked twice more and then went out entirely, and the trench went black, and the two boats lay in the dark together four kilometres down.

And then, from out in the dark beyond the sonar, the thing in the trench answered the last ping it had been given. It answered low and slow, and it answered once, and the Cormorant did not shudder and Kofi did not put his hands on the patch.

No tapping any more. No practising any tune. It made a long and gentle note in the deep, and then it made silence, and the silence went on and on, and nothing at all came back.

“It’s quiet,” Neve said, and heard how cautious her own voice was.

Rivas let out a breath through his nose and put his hand on the console, and for a moment he sat quite still, a man letting something pass through him that he had been holding since morning. Then he reached over to the log and began to type, one key at a time.

08:58 — one flash, belly lamp, toward Halcyon. Halcyon answered. Halcyon sonar off at 09:02. Recommendation: keep silence. Accepted by commander.

09:09 — source silent. Growth check, passive.

“Get on the passive array, Doctor. I want to know if the thing out there is still where it was, or if it’s gone to sleep.”

“It’s not asleep,” Neve said, and reached for the headphones. “But there’s nothing left for it to answer. Let me see if I can prove it.”

She put them on. The cold of the plastic bit at her ears. The trench came up into her skull, small and dark and full of shapes. She had spent the whole day learning the sound of a thing that answered, and now there was no answer in it, and for a moment she felt the relief of that go through her like warmth.

Then she sat forward, and the warmth went out of her.

“Commander,” she said. “The near edge has moved.”

“Moved where?”

She made herself read it twice before she said anything, because she had promised herself that much.

“Not toward us. Not toward the Halcyon either.” She turned the screen so he could see it, though he could not read it the way she could. “It’s thinning where it lay under Halcyon’s port side. It’s thickening out past the debris line, on the eastern wall, and it’s thickening upward.” She heard Lian’s stylus stop. “It isn’t sleeping, Sal. It’s climbing. And it’s climbing the wall that stands between both boats and the way out of this trench.”

The cabin was very quiet. Out in the dark the sea said nothing at all.

“So we’re not finished,” Rivas said. It was not quite a question.

“No,” Neve said. “We’ve stopped it answering us. What we haven’t done is find out what it does when there’s nothing left to answer.”
