Protected

The wind came off the Atlantic grey and steady, and after four kilometres of water it smelled of nothing at all. That was the part she had not expected. She had expected the world to come back rich. Instead the sky pressed flat and low, the sea ran in long dull ridges to the horizon, and the air was so clean it barely registered as a smell at all.

Neve stood on the deck of the Cormorant with her collar up and her burned hand wrapped in a strip of clean rag, and watched the Halcyon wallow alongside. Both boats rode low and heavy, tired ships in a tired sea. Cloud covered the sky from one edge to the other. There was no sun to be glad of, and she was glad of it anyway. Her eyes still ached from the lights of the cabin. Her ears still carried the note, faint and far down, the way a hall carries a chord after the choir has stopped.

Kofi came up the ladder with a plastic crate held against his chest, both arms around it like a man carrying a sleeping child. Inside, cushioned in foam and sealed in three bags, were four slivers of the Lattice. He had cut them while the current was still off her hand and the note was still going, down at the base of the nearest crown, with the array dark and the field settling around him like snow. Packing them cold and still had cost him forty minutes of crawling in the bilges with a torch in his teeth. He had come out with a cut over one eyebrow and his hands shaking, and he had not complained about either.

“If one of these breaks,” he said, “I’m going to sit down on the deck and cry, and you’re all going to have to watch.”

“Put them in the shade,” Neve said absently.

“Doctor. There is no shade. We are on the sea.”

“Then put them somewhere nobody kicks.”

He found a locker and wedged the crate in with two life jackets and stood guard over it as if it might try to get out. Every minute or so he opened the lid a finger’s width, looked in, and closed it again. Rivas came past and told him he looked like a man minding eggs. Kofi said they were more important than eggs, and Rivas said that was true, and went away to see about the boats.

The Halcyon’s boat came across, and Achebe came up over the rail last, a small, spare woman with a broad face and a careful way of moving, and she shook Rivas’s hand and then Neve’s, and held hers a moment longer than the handshake required. She looked at the rag wrapped around her palm.

“That’s a live-contact burn,” she said.

“It is.”

“I’ve seen a few.” She did not let go of her hand. “You’re going to lose the use of part of it. Not all. Part. It’ll stiffen in the cold and it’ll tell you when the weather’s coming.”

“It can tell me whatever it likes,” Neve said. “It saved the boat.”

“Eleven of mine,” she said, and let go, and stepped back. “All eleven. Counting.”

“That was the number,” Rivas said.

“That was the number.” Achebe looked out at the water, and the wind pushed her collar flat against her neck. “I want to say a thing, and I want to say it plainly, because I’ve had a day and a half to think about it and I don’t want to lose the shape of it. When you went quiet down there, I thought you’d left us. I thought you were saving your own air and leaving us to drown. I said so. I said it on my own bridge and my crew heard me say it.” She looked at Neve. “Then you blinked a lamp at us and apologised. Nobody down here has ever apologised over a lamp before. I want it in your log that I was wrong and you were right.”

“It goes in my log,” Neve said. “In all four of our names. And yours. I’ll write your words in beside them.”

Achebe nodded slowly, as though that settled an account. Then she turned and went back down into her boat, and the sea took her without a sound.

The debrief took three weeks and happened in three different rooms, which struck Neve as being somehow the whole of the matter.

There was the company room, where a man in a good suit asked her, twice, whether she was certain the samples were not mineral contamination, and whether the sonar returns could not be explained by a gas vent, a school of something, a fault in the equipment. The room was too warm. A jug of iced water sat on the table and nobody touched it, and the man in the suit had a way of smiling at the end of each question, as though the smile were a form of evidence.

“The equipment was in a fault,” Rivas said from the end of the table. “She heard the thing with the equipment broken. That’s the point.”

“Commander, I’m not trying to —”

“I know what you’re trying,” Rivas said. “I’ve been doing this a long time and I can hear a man trying.” He put his hand flat on the table. “Write it this way. Put it down that Commander Rivas, who is on the record as a man who has never once in his career recommended silence, recommends that you take Dr. Okonkwo seriously. In my name. All of it.”

The man in the suit wrote something. He wrote it slowly, so that they could watch him write it. Neve sat with her bandaged hand in her lap and said nothing, because she had learned years ago that in rooms like that one, the person who talks least is heard most.

There was the institute room, where the biologists sat, and here Neve found her voice again, because they were her people and they asked the right questions. The windows were open. Somebody had brought in a plate of biscuits and forgotten to pass them round. She laid out the field on the table: crowns spaced metres apart, answering one another in a travelling wave, the learning of the ping, the way it had played back the Cormorant’s note piece by piece.

“It learns,” she said. “That’s the word I keep using and I want to be careful with it. It doesn’t think. I don’t think it thinks. But it remembers a sound and it repeats the sound and it changes the repeat, and I have watched a great many living things do exactly that and I have never once watched a crystal do it.”

“And you’re sure the crystal is the organism? Not a structure something built?”

“I’m sure of nothing except what we heard,” Neve said. “That’s why I brought you the samples.”

The samples sat on the bench in their bags of cold water. Grey and pale, they looked, honestly, like nothing much. Half the room wore the look of people being polite. Neve knew that look. She had worn it herself at conferences, at the back, with a pen in her hand and her mind on the train home.

Then Lian took her headphones out of her bag and put them on the table, and the room changed.

“I have two hundred and six hours of recording,” Lian said. “I kept the array passive for most of it, so it isn’t much to look at. But I want you to hear something.”

She played them the first ping. The single clean outbound note of the Cormorant on the way down, and then, four times louder, and bent by the water, the answer.

“That’s the first one,” she said. “Now listen to what it does on the second day.”

She played the phrase. One phrase, then a second, the gaps shrinking between them, the whole sequence played back almost to the note, and the room went very quiet, and one of the older women at the far end of the bench took her glasses off and forgot to put them back on.

“That’s not a reflection,” she said.

“No,” Lian said. “That’s a reply.”

She played the last one, the long gentle note after the silence, and a man near the window made a sound under his breath that was not a word.

They had the recordings analysed in eleven days. Neve sat in on the last session with her hand still bandaged and heard a young postdoc say the word organism without hedging, and she had to look at the floor for a while. Grey lino, with a scuff in it the shape of a boot. She looked at that. Easier than looking at the room.

Rivas was not there for the last of it. He had made his one speech in the company room and would not make another, and when she asked him why he said he had used up all his words and the rest were Lian’s to say, and that a captain who talks at a debrief is a captain who is frightened of it. She thought that was probably true and also probably an excuse, and she let him have it.

There was the third room, which was not a room but a long conference call with the ministry and the fisheries people and a lawyer whose name she never caught, and in that room the question was not whether the Lattice was alive but what to do about the hole it lived in.

“The trench is a trench,” the lawyer said. “On paper, it is also nothing. There is no reservation, no protected area, no status at all. Prospecting outfits have been sniffing around the whole ridge for two years.”

Neve had not known that. The floor seemed to tilt a little.

“Then give it a status,” she said.

“Dr. Okonkwo, you can’t put a fence around four kilometres of water.”

“You put a fence around a whale,” she said. “You put a fence around a bird with a number on its leg and you tell people not to go near the cliff. You can do the same for a place. It’s only paperwork if you do it badly.” She leaned forward. “I’ll write it. Give me a fortnight and I’ll write it. I know what lives in that trench and I know what would harm it, and I can put it in your language.”

There was a pause on the line, the pause of people deciding whether to be cross with her.

“All right,” the lawyer said. “Write it.”

She wrote it on Rivas’s chart table aboard the Cormorant, which by then was in a shipyard in Brest having her viewport cut out of her, and the shipyard noise came in through the open hatch all afternoon. Rain came in through it too, in fine slanting drops, and she kept moving the papers in under the rail. Kofi sat on a crate with a soldering iron and a broken beacon and told her whenever a sentence was too fancy.

“That word,” he said. “That one there. You’ve got three words for the same thing. I’d use the short one.”

“It’s a legal document.”

“It’s a legal document about a rock that sings. Use the short one.”

She used the short one. The terms came out tighter than she had expected: no extraction, no anchoring, no active sonar over the trench except by permit, a run of passive buoys on the rim to listen and do nothing but listen, and a standing note in the annex that the soundscape itself, the whole acoustic environment, every hum and knock and question, was part of what was protected.

That clause gave the lawyer trouble. She argued for it for an hour on the call, and won it in a way that felt less like winning than like having been listened to.

“Why does the noise matter?” the lawyer asked her, at the end. “Not the animal. Why does the noise matter?”

“Because we are the loudest thing for four kilometres in every direction,” Neve said. “We come down into a quiet place and we shout, and then we wonder why it shuts up.” She clicked her pen and looked at the page. “A quiet place is a kind of heritage. I’d protect it the way I’d protect a church.”

The paperwork came back in the autumn with a seal on it and a schedule attached, and her name was on the third line, and Rivas’s on the fourth, and Lian’s and Kofi’s below, because she had put them there and refused to take them off. Achebe’s correction was in the annex, in her own plain sentences, recorded as she had asked, signed where a signature could reach her. The seal was red and square and it had bled a little at one corner, and she found she liked that. It made it look handmade.

Lian read it first, standing under a grey sky on the pier with the wind pulling at the page.

“It says designated on the recommendation of,” she said. “It says our names.”

“It says your recording,” Neve said. “Two hundred and six hours of it. I only wrote the words.”

“You wrote the words,” Lian said, and read it again, and did not give it back for some while. “You know what it is? It’s a title. We never had a title. It was just a noise on my screen that I couldn’t explain and I was frightened to say too much about.”

“You said a lot about it,” Neve said. “You said it first.”

Lian folded the page along its folds, very careful, and put it inside her jacket.

The last night of the crew was a corner table in a place in Brest that served fish and nothing else, and Kofi ordered for the table with the air of a man awarding medals. There was a candle in a bottle on the table and it had been lit so long it had sunk in its own wax.

There was a gap in the conversation late on, the good kind of gap, and Rivas filled it.

“I’ve got a thing to say,” he said. “And I’m going to say it once, and nobody at this table is allowed to be kind about it.”

He turned his glass a quarter turn.

“I was wrong,” he said. “Not about the air, and not about going quiet. About the other thing. I had a picture in my head of how the world works: you go down, you find the trouble, you get your people out of it. Trouble doesn’t have a reason. Trouble is just the thing that keeps you from getting your people out.” He looked at Neve. “Then a woman I’d never met told me the trouble was frightened and wanted to be talked to, and I thought she was a scientist with a soft heart and a hard head and I’d best manage her carefully until this was over.” He lifted his eyes. “That’s the wrong way round. You weren’t managing the deep. You were listening to it, and I was managing you, and I’m sorry for it.”

The table was quiet. Kofi had put down his fork.

“The log says I was wrong,” Rivas said. “I want it said where people can hear it, too.”

Neve looked at him. There were things she could say. She had been carrying a set of them for years, in the shape of that other diver, in the shape of a sonar return she had called trouble when it was only an animal going about its business, in the shape of the panic that had followed and the name she had never quite been able to put down. The whole set had lived in her like a held breath. A decade of being careful the way a person is careful with a bad knee: always making the smaller step, always choosing the quieter route.

“I have been wrong before,” she said. “Deeply. I called something a threat that wasn’t, and a man died, and I have carried that dive around with me ever since like a stone in my pocket. I told you about the worm bank because I thought if you knew I could be wrong you might trust me more.”

“It worked,” Rivas said.

“It worked because you were brave enough to be wrong out loud,” she said. “I wasn’t, for years. I kept my head down and my name in my own mouth and I never once let anyone see me frightened of being mistaken.” She turned her glass the way he had turned his. “That’s finished. If I’m going to be wrong, I’ll do it out loud and get the next one right.”

Kofi said, “That’ll be a hard habit to keep.”

“I know. I’ve got a burnt hand and a very loud friend to remind me.”

“That’s me and the hand,” Kofi said, and lifted his glass. “To the two of us.”

Rivas lifted his. Lian did not lift hers for a moment; she was looking at the candle, and then she looked up and said, “I’ll miss hearing them talk,” and lifted it, and that was that.

They gave the last of the Lattice samples to the institute, and the institute gave one back to Neve, sealed in a case of cold water and lit from underneath, and she put it on a shelf in her office where the light came in sideways in the afternoon. On bright days it threw a small pale pattern on the wall behind her desk, and she found herself turning to look at it the way she used to look at the sonar screen.

She went back down in the spring.

The dive was official now. Passive only, no sonar over the trench, a slow descent with the lights off for the last eight hundred metres because the buoy network on the rim was listening and she did not want to add a single superfluous sound to it. The sub was smaller than the Cormorant and much quieter, and the pilot was a young woman who had read the report twice and asked good questions all the way down. Her name was Rosa, and she had a calm way with her hands that reminded Neve of Kofi, which was not a thing she expected to find at four kilometres down.

“You won’t hear it at first,” Neve told her. “You’ll hear the water. Then you’ll hear the water go quiet. Then you’ll hear it.”

At four thousand metres the floor came up out of the dark, and Rosa took her in slow and set her down soft as a hand on a table, and the sediment rose in a slow pale cloud around them and settled, and the lamps were off, and the whole world was a soft undifferentiated black with the pressure creaking gently around it.

Neve lay on her belly at the viewport with her bad hand flat on the cold glass and looked out at the field.

It was there.

It was there exactly where she had left it, crowns on the floor spread away into the dark, faintly luminous at the edges where the light found them, and she could see the way the light moved over them and did not come back the same way, and she thought about a note going down into the trench and a note coming back stretched by four kilometres of cold water and the whole field keeping the shape of it in its bones. Cold water bends a note down. Everything down here spoke a little lower than it meant to. She had learned that in thirty-six hours and she would spend the rest of her life unlearning her surprise at it.

She had brought one sound to say to it. She had thought about it for months, and it had seemed presumptuous, and she had decided to be presumptuous.

“Give me the array,” she said. “Passive.”

“Passive is on, Doctor.”

Neve put her mouth close to the pickup and made a sound. Not a note, exactly. Something between a note and a word, low and clean and going down, the way a person says something to a horse. It did not carry far. It was not meant to.

The field was still. The water was still. Rosa was so quiet that Neve could hear her breathing.

Then it came back.

Low, stretched by the long cold climb of it, one clean downward curve of sound that travelled up through the hull and settled in Neve’s chest, and it was her own sound, and it was different, and it was the same, the way a voice comes back off a far wall.

She listened to it fade. Then she turned her hand over on the glass, palm up, and left it there. The stiff fingers would not close all the way. She had stopped minding that. It was the hand that had held the note, and it could be a little crooked now if it wanted.

“What was that?” Rosa said, very softly.

“That’s the neighbours,” Neve said. “They know us now.”

The field went quiet, and the dark went back to being the dark, and the Marrow Deep lay under them, four kilometres of cold water and something alive in it, and Neve Okonkwo lay at the window and did not call it a threat, and did not call it an enemy, and did not call it anything but what it was.
