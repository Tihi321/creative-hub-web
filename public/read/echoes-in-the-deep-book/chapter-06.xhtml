The Pulse

The drum was a beacon and a timer and a roll of tape, and Kofi held it up in the aft bay like a man holding up a fish he was proud of.

“Behold,” he said. “The world’s smallest, worst drum kit.”

The galley timer sat wired into the beacon’s belly with red and blue leads and a strip of tape already lifting at one corner. Kofi pressed the dial with his thumb. The beacon gave a soft knock against the deck, waited, knocked again.

“Gap’s set at eight seconds,” he said. “This dial does not have numbers, it has moods, but I know the moods now. I’ve been living with it for an hour.”

“A heartbeat,” Lian said from the doorway, her head tipped. “A slow one. A resting one.”

“Good or bad?” Rivas called from the console.

“A start,” Neve said.

They had written the plan down by then, the way Rivas had insisted. It sat on the tablet in his careful one-key-at-a-time typing: at 12:15, Cormorant lowers the beacon on a weighted line out of the belly hatch, paying out two hundred metres toward the eastern wall, away from the Halcyon and away from the boat. At 12:20, the drum begins, eight-second gaps, low power. At 12:35, if the field has turned toward the drum, Neve calls for the line to be paid out further, and they walk it. If the field turns toward the Cormorant, Kofi cuts the drum and they go flat and silent, no worse off than when they started, except one spare beacon poorer. And at seventeen hours of air, if the thing is not moving off, they give up on the drum.

“That last line,” Rivas had said when he typed it, “is the only line in this that I wrote myself. Keep it in your head.”

“Seventeen,” Neve said. “I have it.”

“Say the rest.”

“If it goes wrong, the responsibility is mine first, logged in my name, then yours, then Kofi’s, then Lian’s. Because I called it.”

“Because you called it,” Rivas said, and pressed the key.

They lowered the beacon at 12:14, one minute early, because Kofi said the timer had a mood on it and he wanted to start while the mood was good.

The belly hatch opened with a groan Neve felt in her teeth. The beacon went down and away into water that had never in the history of the world held a lamp. On the outside camera it was a small pale shape going out and out, then a smudge, then nothing the eye could name, the line running back to the Cormorant like the string of a kite.

“Two hundred metres,” Kofi said. “Or thereabouts. The tape measure is my arm and my arm is lying.”

“Stand by,” Rivas said.

Neve had worn the headphones so long that the pads had gone warm and soft and smelled of her own skin. The trench was so quiet now that she could hear the boat’s small body sounds through them, and her own pulse like a knock a long way off.

“Kofi,” she said. “Start her.”

The first knock went out into the deep.

She felt it more than heard it, a soft pressure at the soles of her feet, and then the sound travelled away across the floor of the trench, and for eight seconds nothing happened.

Then the field answered.

The crown nearest the beacon answered the knock with a knock, a soft tap off to the east of where it should have been, and the space between the two taps matched the eight seconds the dial had set. Then the next crown, and the next, and the answer ran along the field like a hand along a fence, going away from the Halcyon and coming toward the drum.

“It’s answering in time,” Lian said, her voice up half a step.

“A metronome,” Kofi said. “Doctor, it’s a metronome.”

“Quiet,” Neve said, and said it because something moved under the returns, the way a floorboard moves before you hear it creak. “Commander. The field’s turning. It’s turning away from the Halcyon.”

Rivas leaned over her shoulder. The pale hole of the Halcyon was larger now, the growth thinning along her port side like a tide going out, and the leading edge of the field had swung off its line and pointed east, out toward the drum.

“It’s working,” Rivas said. “Kofi, pay out another fifty metres. Slowly.”

“Paying out.”

The line went out. The beacon knocked and the field answered and the two sounds went on ahead of each other into the dark. It was working. For one whole minute the Cormorant felt like a boat that was going to be a boat and not a coffin.

The change came before she saw it, because the taps stopped going away.

One moment the answer was east of the drum, fading. The next the taps came back on a different bearing, and they were not going away any more, they were coming in, and they were closer together than eight seconds.

“Kofi,” she said. “What’s the gap?”

“Eight. I set eight.”

“They’re coming at four.”

The bloom on the screen swung through the north and settled, and Neve watched it for two taps and refused to believe it, and then Lian said it for her.

“It’s on our bearing. It’s coming toward us.”

“Kofi, kill the drum.”

His hand was already on the dial. The taps stopped. The silence came in on them like water into a held breath, and for one second it was the good silence, and Neve thought, We’re all right, that’s all it was, we startled it.

Then the leading edge of the field hit the Cormorant.

It did not strike. No bang, no impact like a rock. The floor of the boat simply began to talk. She felt it up through the deck plates and into her knees, a low buzz and a tap and a buzz, and the whole hull rang with it like a bell somebody had touched in the dark.

“It’s under the keel,” Kofi said. “The whole front of the field is under the keel, it’s on the battery bay—”

“Hull stress,” Rivas snapped.

“Left of the red. Left of the red, but it’s climbing.”

The first crack sounded like a knuckle.

A line had run out from the edge of the forward viewport, white in the glass and fine as a hair, and behind it a new line, and the old crack from this morning, the one Kofi had taped at the viewport seal, was spreading. A bead of something glittered at the worst of it. Air. Not much. A bead of air.

“Seal breach,” Kofi said, and was out of his seat before the words were done. “Lian, the patch kit, the big one, the one with the two-part resin, no, not that one, the one with the blue label—”

“There is no blue label, they’re both grey—”

“The one that’s heavy!”

She got it. He tore the lid off with his teeth. Neve got to the viewport and pressed her palm flat over the crack, feeling the little cold breath of the sea against her skin, and the hull ringing and ringing under her hand.

“Pressure’s holding,” she said. “Sal, it’s holding, but it’s breathing.”

“Commander,” Kofi said. “I need the frame. Somebody hold the frame straight. Doctor, other side of the window, put your back to it and push the seal corner in by a finger’s width, when I say.”

She put her back to the window and braced her boots on the console foot and pushed, and Kofi mixed the resin on the back of his own glove, a grey paste going warm in the cold air, and reached around her and laid it on and smoothed it with his thumb and did not flinch.

“Hold,” he said. “Count with me. Sixteen seconds.”

“Seventeen hours of air,” Rivas said from behind them, like a man tolling a bell. “Seventeen hours. Sixteen fifty-nine. Hold it.”

“Sixteen seconds,” Kofi said. “Fifteen.”

They counted it out. Neve’s back ached and her boots slipped and the breath of the sea came cold against her shoulder, and under her back the hull ran on with its buzz and its tap and its buzz.

“Four,” Kofi said. “Three. Two. Set.”

The cold stopped. The resin went from warm to cool to hard against her shoulder, and she stayed with her back to it because her knees did not trust themselves yet.

“It’s holding,” Kofi said. He sat down hard on the deck. “It’s a patch on a patch on a crack, and the heavy resin is all gone now, every last squeeze of it, and the tape with it. That was the last of what we had. Do not slam any doors.”

“Where’s the original crack?” Rivas said. “The one you strapped this morning.”

Kofi turned his head to the starboard frame, where the webbing strap ran across the weld-line fissure like a belt on a fat man.

“It’s gone longer. Two fingers longer than it was. The strap’s holding it, but the metal’s tired there, and tired metal does not get fresh again. I can tape a crack and I can strap a frame, and neither one of them is a repair. They’re a promise that we won’t move fast. That’s what I bought this morning and that’s what I just spent again.”

“How much did it cost us?”

“The last of the resin, the last of the tape, and about four hours off the boat, because after this she wants to be handled like a sleeping baby and we are not going to handle her like a sleeping baby tonight.”

Rivas said nothing. Neve stayed with her back against the window and made herself look at the number.

“Sixteen,” Rivas said at last. “Sixteen hours. All right. It’s holding. Kofi, sit still for a minute.”

“I’m sitting.”

Neve did not sit. She went to the screen where the returns still came in, and stood with her hands on the rail and let herself be told what she already knew. The drum was stopped. The viewport was holding. And still the field was ringing, and it had not gone back to the Halcyon.

“Listen,” she said.

“I am listening,” Rivas said. “I’m listening to my boat being played like a—”

“No.” She put the headphones on and the sound came up and she had to take them off again. “It’s not answering the drum any more. It’s answering everything. The scrubber. The hull. Us.”

“What do you mean, everything?”

“The drum woke it up, and the crack woke it up, and now it’s answering all of it at once.” She turned the pad to face them. “There’s a chorus down there. Lian, tell me I’m wrong.”

Lian had her headphones on and her eyes shut and her hands flat on the console.

“You’re not wrong,” she said, in a thread of a voice. “There are hundreds of them out there, and they’re all singing at once, and they’re all singing different things. Some are singing the drum. Some are singing the crack. Some are singing this boat’s own noise coming back off the water. It is not a choir any more, Commander. It is a crowd in a room with too many doors.”

“And a crowd does what?” Rivas said.

Nobody answered him, because the hull buzzed and tapped and buzzed, and then something moved against the Cormorant’s flank that was not a tap, that had weight to it, and the whole boat lurched a hand’s width to port and came up shivering.

“That,” Kofi said, “would be a crowd pushing.”

The strange thing was that the boat was quiet. The drum was off, and the ordinary small noises of the Cormorant had started back up like a room after a noise. And in that quiet, sitting on the deck with the patch still tacky on the glass behind her, Neve found she could not get up. Four hours. That was the bill on the table, paid, gone, not coming back.

“Doctor,” Kofi said, from the floor. “Say the thing you are going to say.”

“I’m not going to say anything.”

“You have got the face of a woman who has thought of a second idea and does not want to be seen thinking it, and I have known you one day and I already know the face, so save us the ten minutes and say it.”

“We’re all right,” Neve said.

“We are not all right,” Rivas said. “I have a boat with two cracks and a spent patch kit and a thing in the water that is pushing on the hull because it likes the sound of me. I would very much like to be told something that isn’t all right, because I have had all the all right I can take.”

She got up, and went to the screen, and stood looking a while at the crowd, swollen and bright and crowded in under the boat, and let the quiet sit, because the quiet was owed.

“The drum failed,” she said. “I want to say that plainly, before I go on. It brought it off the Halcyon and then it brought it onto us. It was too loud and too hard, and every knock was a hit, and a thing that lives by answering will answer a hit louder than it answers anything. That was my plan and my call and the four hours are mine.”

“Four hours off a crack,” Kofi said, “and the last of my resin, which I want in the log, and my ice cream, which I want in the log twice.”

“All of it’s yours,” Neve said. “And what I have not been able to stop thinking since the window cracked is this. It answered the drum in time. It took our eight seconds and gave them back to us. It sang our own boat back at us, round and soft, like a room rounding off a voice.”

“And?”

“And a thing that hates noise does not sing in time. A thing that hates noise goes and hides. This thing learned our song and gave it back, and then we knocked too hard and it answered too hard, and that is not what an animal does when it is hunted. That is what a thing does when it is talking and the other party will not stop shouting. We have not been fighting it. We have been drowning it out.” She put her hands flat on the rail. “It’s not strength it hates. What if it doesn’t want to be hit? What if it wants to be sung to?”

Rivas did not shout. That was the thing. He sat with his hands folded and looked at her across the little cabin, and it was worse than shouting.

“You have brought this boat to the edge of her life once today,” he said.

“Twice,” Neve said. “Once with the ping, once with the drum.”

“Yes. Twice. And you are now proposing to make a third noise at the same thing, and you want me to believe you that the third one will go better than the first two.”

“I know exactly what I’m asking.”

“Then give me a reason.”

“Not from me,” Neve said. “From it.” She lifted the headphones off the console and held them out to Lian. “Tell him. Tell him the thing you told me, the thing that isn’t the drum and isn’t the ping.”

Lian took the headphones and sat with them in her lap, and when she spoke she set each word down as if it had to be carried somewhere.

“Commander, remember the silence,” she said. “When we all went dark. The quiet was not an empty quiet. The quiet was a long low note, and the low note had a shape. That shape is still in the water. It is underneath the crowd.” She closed her eyes. “The crowd is on top. The long low note is underneath, and it comes and goes, and the crowd does not eat it. It is like a person in a loud room humming to herself.”

Rivas said nothing.

“That note is what it sounds like when it is not frightened,” Lian said. “It is what it sounded like when we were silent and it thought we had gone away. I cannot sing it. I have not got the machine for it. But if Kofi can make the drum not knock, but hum, and if the hum is the shape of the long low note, then I think it will hear the shape it already knows. It hears the shape and it thinks, that is the thing that was quiet with me, and not the thing that shouted.”

“Can he make the drum sing?” Rivas said.

“It’s a buzzer,” Kofi said, turning the beacon over in his hands. “A buzzer with a gap is a knock, and a buzzer without a gap is a bad note somebody already complained about.” He turned it again. “But the timer’s got a setting where it doesn’t open and close so much as lean. It’s the galley bell, the one that’s meant to be quiet and goes hmmm. Give me twelve minutes.”

“You have ten,” Rivas said.

He got the log tablet out from under the chart table and put it on his knee, and typed, one key at a time, his lips moving with it.

“Twelve forty-one,” he said. “First pulse failed. Loud. Lattice surfaced a crowd; viewport cracked and sealed; starboard weld line extended; last of the resin and tape spent. Recommendation of Dr Okonkwo, accepted by commander in his own name: soft pulse, harmonic, the shape of the long note. Decision mine. Judged wrong in the first attempt by Okonkwo, who called it, logged in her name first and mine second.”

He turned the screen round so she could see it.

“You wrote it in my name first.”

“I promised you that,” Rivas said. “This morning I promised it in front of everyone, and I am a great many things but I am not a man who promises in front of everyone and then writes his own name at the top. And you have not said your piece.” He held out the tablet. “Say it into the log, in your own hand, and then we’ll go and see whether your soft bell knows the tune.”

She took the tablet and typed it herself, and it took a while, because her hands were not steady.

Twelve forty-three. I recommended the pulse drum. I said a steady knock would draw it toward the drum and it did, and then it turned and came to us, and the pressure cracked the viewport and cost us the last of the resin and about four hours. My recommendation, my call, my name first. I do not withdraw it. I withdraw only the knock.

“That’s the lot?” Rivas said.

“That’s the lot.”

“Then stop writing and start listening,” he said. “We’re going to sing to it. In all four names.”

Kofi worked the timer. They sat round him in the red light while the field bumped once, twice, against the flank, no worse than a boat nudging a quay, and then stopped.

At 12:52 he held the beacon up. It looked the same as before. The only difference was the dial, taped over with a mark in ballpoint pen, and the fact that when he pressed it the beacon did not knock. It hummed. One long low note with a slow swell to it, and a pause, and then the same note again, and the swells did not begin with a hard edge and they did not end with one. It was the sound of the trench itself, the long gentle question, put into a machine.

“Sing to it,” Kofi said.

“Lian,” Neve said. “The line. Pay the beacon out slow and steady and we’ll walk it east along the wall, further from both boats, and if the field so much as leans on us, you tell me and Kofi cuts it. Commander?”

“Do it,” Rivas said.

They put the soft pulse out at 12:53, and paid the line out slowly, and the long low note went out into the dark.

For forty seconds nothing happened, and Neve had time to be very sure of the wrongness of her last idea and to begin to be sure of the wrongness of this one.

Then the field answered.

It did not tap. It swelled. One crown took up the note, and the note went out along the field, not in a ripple but in a held breath, all of them humming the same long low note together and the note swelling a little and settling, swelling and settling, the way the sea swells on a long slow day. The buzz left the hull. The buzzing under her feet simply went out of it, like a held muscle letting go, and the tap and the tap and the tap stopped, and the Cormorant rode quiet in a dark that was full of one note.

“Hull stress coming down,” Kofi said, his voice cracking in the middle of the word. “It’s coming off.”

“It’s slowing,” Lian said, eyes closed. “It is not stopped, it is slowed. It has turned back toward the drum and it is going slow, the way you walk when you are listening to something.”

The bloom was thinning under the Cormorant, the leading edge swung east again along the wall after the beacon, and the space it had been filling with itself was opening up like a room after a crowd goes home. Along the Halcyon’s side there was nothing at all now, just a wide pale vacancy, and out there in the middle of it a little orange coal of a sail lamp, still burning.

“The Halcyon’s clear,” Neve said. “There’s nothing on her at all. Sal, she’s clear.”

Rivas sat back and rubbed his face with the flat of his hand.

“We’re not clear,” he said. “But we’re clearer. Sixteen hours and a soft bell. Keep it going, nice and slow, and tell me what it costs.”

It was Kofi who told him what it cost, about twenty minutes later.

“Commander.” He had the power panel open and the torch in his teeth, and when he took it out of his mouth his face in the light was not the face of a man with a joke in him. “We’ve got a leak.”

“Where?”

“Not in the hull. In the power. The drum is pulling steady off the spare battery in the aft bay, and the aft bay battery is not getting any fresher, and about four hundred years of engineering experience says we can do this for another two hours and then the drum takes the last of it, or the thrusters get it, and the thrusters are the thing that takes us up.”

“Two hours of drum,” Rivas said. “And it’s working.”

“It is working like a dream. It is working so well that we’re pouring our own air into the sound it makes.”

Lian took her headphones off and laid them down carefully, and for a moment she looked much younger than she was. “Commander,” she said. “If we starve the drum, it stops singing, and the crowd comes back. We cannot just switch it off.”

Neve sat very still and looked at the screen. It was a map of a live thing, going slow and steady east along the wall, and behind it the Halcyon sat clear, and above them both was a surface four kilometres up and a sky she had not seen since yesterday.

“Two hours,” she said. “Can we make two hours into more?”

Kofi looked at the open panel, and at the broken galley timer, and at the ceiling, and something in his face changed.

“Depends what you’d like to stop doing,” he said.
