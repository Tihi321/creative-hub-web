The Note

The climb out of the Marrow Deep began with nobody saying anything.

For the first four hundred metres the Cormorant went up stern-first at a shallow angle, thrusters labouring the way a tired animal labours, and Rivas held her to it with both hands and his jaw set. Kofi watched the aft battery draw and kept his mouth shut, which was its own kind of alarm. The air counter had stopped being a number they read aloud. Fourteen hours and twenty minutes. It had read fourteen hours and twenty for so long that Neve had begun to suspect the counter of lying.

At eight hundred metres Lian said, quietly, “I’ve got the Halcyon. Above us and to port. She’s climbing steady. She’s pinging.”

Rivas’s hands stayed where they were. “How many times?”

“Four. Once a minute, regular. Not panic any more. That’s a survey boat doing what it knows. Ping, wait, ping.”

“Is she getting answers?”

Lian tilted her head, listening with the whole of her face the way she did. “No. The trench is quiet under us. The field’s still where we left it, over the floor. It’s not following.”

“Then let her ping,” Rivas said. “She’s earned the right to be a little noisy on the way home.”

Neve almost smiled. Twenty hours in, and that was the first time she had heard him give permission for a sound.

The trouble came at three thousand one hundred metres.

The Cormorant shuddered hard enough to rattle the loose cup in its gimbal, and then she shuddered again, and the shudder had a rhythm to it that Neve did not like at all.

“Kofi.”

“Already looking.” His head came up from the panel. “Not the frame. The patch is holding, the strap’s holding, I’d bet the boat on it. It’s the thrusters. Number two is losing her bite. She’s surging.”

“Same as before?”

“Not the same as before. Before, it was the controller taking its timing off the sonar clock. That’s fixed. Been fixed since eight o’clock this morning, I ran her off her own crystal and she’s been good as gold.” He wiped his forehead with his wrist. “This is different. This is the aft battery. She gave everything she had to the drum and the song, and now she’s remembering she’s a battery, and she’s got opinions about it.”

Rivas glanced at the instruments. “Can you give me full on one and three?”

“I can give you ninety on one and three and she’ll climb like a bear on a ladder. It’s slow.”

“Slow is fine. Slow is up.” Rivas eased the throttle back, and the shudder softened, but did not stop. “How much battery have we got?”

“Enough for the climb and the manoeuvring at the top, if we don’t get clever. Not enough for clever.” Kofi paused. “Commander, there’s a thing. The transducer’s still mounted on the frame ring. It’s drawing a trickle even switched off, and I want to pull the lead.”

“Pull it.”

Kofi was already reaching. Then he stopped with his hand on the cable, and went very still, and Neve felt the cabin change the way a room changes when someone stops breathing.

“Lian,” Kofi said. “What’s the field doing?”

“Dormant. It’s been dormant since the throat.” Lian’s voice was careful. “Why?”

“Because the transducer’s not switched off,” Kofi said. “It’s cold, but it’s not dead. There’s a standing charge in the frame ring, a residual, from the note. Steel rings for a long time. She’s been ringing this whole climb, just too soft for us to hear.” He looked up, and his cheerful face had gone strange and flat. “And it’s going down. The trench is taking it. It’s been listening to us crawl up her throat for the last two hours, and it’s been hearing it as a very quiet, very steady note.”

The cabin was silent.

“Tell me it’s not going to answer,” Rivas said.

“It’s not going to answer,” Lian said. “It’s dormant. It’s not growing, it’s not turning, it’s just there, a field on a floor. It’s not — wait.”

That word again.

“Wait,” Lian said. “Wait. It’s moving.”

“Which way?”

“Up.”

“Up where?”

Lian’s hands were flying. “Up. Off the floor. The whole field is lifting, Commander, not growing, not reaching, lifting, the way a carpet lifts when something breathes under it. It’s slow. It’s very slow. But it’s coming up the throat.”

“How long have we got?”

“At this speed?” Lian looked at the screen and her face went pale in the red light. “It’s four kilometres down, we’re at three one. If it keeps this speed and we keep ours, it comes up under us before we’re at two thousand. It won’t even have to hurry.”

Rivas looked at Neve.

“Cut the charge,” he said. “Kofi. Kill the resonator, kill the frame, kill everything that could be making a noise we can’t hear.”

“I can ground the frame against the hull, that’ll kill the standing charge in about a minute. But Commander, she’s still climbing. A sub climbing makes noise. Water going past a hull makes a note whether you want it to or not. I can’t make her quiet enough without stopping her, and if I stop her we’re going down or we’re sitting still, and both of those are a kind of dying.”

“Then we go faster,” Rivas said.

“Battery.”

“Then we go loud,” Rivas said, and there was the old edge in his voice, the one Neve had not heard since the first crack. “We go loud and we go fast and we beat it to the top.”

“You can’t beat it,” Lian said. “It doesn’t have to hurry. It’ll just come up behind us and close the throat at three thousand and we’ll be climbing into the underside of it.”

“Then we fight it at the throat.”

“With what?” Kofi said. “We’ve got a cracked window, a battery with opinions, and a transducer we’re trying to switch off.”

Neve listened to them, and looked at the screen, and felt the thing she had been carrying since the first ping settle into her chest like a stone.

“Nobody fights it,” she said.

They both turned.

“We can’t outrun it and we can’t outshout it,” Neve said. “We’ve spent this whole dive learning one thing about it, and I keep forgetting the lesson because I keep being frightened. It doesn’t want to hurt us. It wants the noise to stop. When we were quiet, it was quiet. When we sang the clean chord, it opened. Every single time, it does exactly what a living thing does when it hears something it doesn’t like: it moves away, or it closes up, or it listens to see whether the thing is a friend.”

“Doctor,” Rivas said, “the thing is coming up to meet us.”

“Yes. Because we’ve been humming at it for two hours and it wants to know who’s there.” She looked at Kofi. “Ground the frame. Take away the hum, and it goes back to sleep, and it goes back to sleep in the throat, and both boats come home.”

Kofi began to move, and then stopped. His eyes went somewhere far away, and his mouth came open by a hair.

“That’s a lovely idea,” he said slowly. “Lovely and clean and scientific. There’s just one problem. If I ground the frame, the standing charge doesn’t just disappear. It goes into the hull, and the hull is already the loudest thing in the water, and the thing it goes into is not a hum. It’s a bang. A one-off, big, sharp, steel bang, right where the frame meets the pressure hull.” He lifted his hands. “Like slapping a sleeping dog with a frying pan. It might go back to sleep. Or it might bite the hand holding the pan, and we are the hand.”

The cabin was quiet.

“Then we don’t ground it,” Neve said at last. “We give it a proper note. A clean one. Down at it, before it gets any higher.”

“We already gave it a note,” Rivas said. “It nearly killed us.”

“The bloom nearly killed us,” Neve said. “The note opened the way. We got it wrong because we gave it two hulls and a crowd of overtones and we called that a song. Lian found the frequency it trusts. It’s sitting in the throat right now singing it to itself. If we give the same note back, clean, once, it settles.”

“And if it closes on us instead?”

“Then it was always going to close on us,” Neve said. “This is a better chance than going loud, and you know it is.”

“With what power?” Kofi said. “A clean note comes out of a clean emitter, and the only clean emitter on this boat is the active array, and the array draws more than everything else combined. What do I switch off to feed it?”

Neve looked at the panel. “Everything.”

Kofi looked at the panel as though it had asked him something rude. “Lights? Pumps? The heater? Commander, if we turn off the pumps, the air goes stale, and stale air is the same as dead air with a nicer name.”

“Not for the whole climb,” Neve said. “Long enough to sing and see if the field settles. We don’t have to like it.”

“There’s more than the power,” Kofi said. “If we throw the array into the water from here, the note that comes out is the right note up here. Four kilometres down, it’s not. Cold water bends a sound down. The deeper it goes the lower it lands, and the field’s whole song is the bent version, the one it’s been singing at itself since we left. Give it the clean note and it hears a stranger.”

“So we bend it ourselves,” Neve said. “We give it the note it will hear, not the note we want to sing.”

Kofi stared at her. Then he started to laugh, the short breathless laugh of a man who has run out of objections and found a job instead. “We tune the array flat. Of course we do. We sing it the stretched version. Doctor, I could kiss you and I could also strangle you.”

“Later,” Neve said. “Which of us pushes the button?”

“Not you,” Kofi said, instantly. “It’s a two-handed job and it’s a bad one. The array contactor’s upstream of everything we’re about to switch off. There’s a hot leg coming in off the emergency cells that we didn’t route through the panel, because we didn’t have time. You throw the switch with your left and you hold the contactor shut with your right, and if the surge comes back through it, it comes back through you. We should do this with a fuse.”

“We don’t have a fuse,” Rivas said.

“We don’t have a fuse,” Kofi agreed.

“Give me the switch,” Neve said.

“Doctor—”

“You’re the only one who can read what the array’s doing,” she said. “Lian’s the only one who can hear what comes back. Sal’s the only one who can fly the boat when everything else is off. And I’m the only one here with a hand that already doesn’t work properly.” She held it up: the palm still pink and stiff from the earlier work, the fingers slow to close. “If something gets cooked, let it be the hand I’ve already lost half of.”

Kofi looked at Rivas. Rivas looked at the counter, at the crack, at the whole small ruin of his boat, and then at Neve.

“Say it for the log,” he said. “First person. Your voice.”

Neve took a breath and let it out.

“The recommendation is mine,” she said. “One note, clean, bent for the depth, aimed down the throat before the field comes past three thousand. If it goes wrong, it goes wrong because I chose it. Neve Okonkwo, senior. Sal Rivas, as commander, concurring.” She looked at him. “Is that the shape of it?”

“That’s the shape of it,” Rivas said. “Both names. Write it after.” He turned to the console. “Kofi. Strip her down.”

Kofi stripped the boat with the speed of a man who had thought about it for weeks and hoped never to do it. The heater went first, with a click that was itself a kind of loss. Then the galley circuit, the work lamps, the recorder on the spare channel, the charger for the hand torches. The cabin dimmed and then dimmed again, and the cold came up through the deck-plates and settled against the skin, and Neve could hear her own teeth before she stopped them.

“Pumps stay on,” Kofi said. “Air stays good. Everything else I can sell, I’m selling.”

“The array?” Rivas said.

“The array gets what’s left. It’s going to sound like a man who’s been shouting all day. But it’ll be the right note, bent the right way, and it’ll be in the right place in the water.” He looked at the screen. “Lian, where’s my target?”

“The field’s at three two four and rising. It’s slow. It’s like a hand coming up through the dark looking for the edge of a table.” Lian’s voice was steady, which frightened Neve more than panic would have. “If you sing at three thousand one, straight down, it meets the field’s song about eight hundred metres under us. That’s where the note will land. That’s the number.”

“Then that’s the number,” Kofi said. He wiped his hands on his trousers and put them on the panel. “Tuned flat, gain as low as I can get away with, and I’ll ramp it slow because if I hit her with full power the array will cough and the cough will make the surge.”

“Do it,” Rivas said.

Kofi threw the switch.

The note came out of the array clean and low and enormous. It was not the shout they had made at the bloom. It was a single held tone, tuned down to where the cold would bend it, and it went away from them into the trench without a ripple or a quaver, one long patient downward curve of sound going to meet the thing that was climbing.

Then the surge came.

It came back up the cable, through the array mount, through the deck, and into Kofi’s panel with a bang that spat light. Kofi yelped and pulled his hands back, and the note faltered, and the counter dropped half a metre on the gauge, and in the same instant Neve understood that if the note stopped now, the thing coming up the throat would hear not one voice but a broken one, and close.

“Hold it,” she said. “Kofi, hold it.”

“I can’t hold it, it’s running through the panel—”

“Then I’ll hold it.”

She reached past him and put her left hand on the switch and her right hand, the burned one, flat on the naked brass of the contactor, and closed it.

The note came back, steady.

Then the current found her.

She felt it in her teeth first, a bright buzzing wire running from the brass up her arm and into her jaw, and then the world went white at the edges and her right hand clamped shut on the contactor and would not let go. There was no pain, exactly. There was a vast, hot, buzzing pressure, as though the sea had decided to come in through her arm, and under it, thin and clear, the note going on and on and down and down, and she thought, quite calmly, so this is how it takes me, and I am holding the note.

“Neve!” Kofi’s voice came from far away.

She could hear him. She could not answer.

“She can’t let go,” Kofi said, and his voice cracked. “She’s locked on. Commander, she’s locked on and I can’t pull her off, not without killing her, not while the current’s live.”

“Then kill the current,” Rivas said.

“If I kill it, the array dies, and the note stops, and the thing in the throat hears it stop.”

The cabin was very quiet under the hum. The counter ticked. The field knew the note was faltering, Neve could see that on the screen with the corner of her eye, and she could not make her mouth work to say it.

Then Kofi said, “Oh, no. No. All right. All right.”

She heard him move. She heard the clatter of something, and a scrape, and his quick, light, panicking footsteps, and then his voice, close by her ear, low and steady the way you speak to a frightened horse.

“Doctor. Doctor, I’m going to bridge the contactor with the wrench. It’s steel and it’ll take the current off your hand and give it somewhere else to go. You won’t like the look of it. Try not to watch.”

She tried to say no. The wrench was a wrench, it wasn’t a conductor made for it, it would flash, he would burn himself, he was the only engineer she had.

“It’s a good wrench,” Kofi said, as though she had spoken. “It’s my favourite wrench. Hold on.”

The note went on. She could feel Kofi’s shoulder against hers, and then the pressure of the brass against her palm changed as he laid the steel across it, and there was a crack, and a smell like a struck match and hot metal and something worse, and the buzzing in her arm went out of her all at once like a tide.

She was on the deck. She could not remember the deck. Kofi was above her, one hand on the contactor, the wrench still jammed across it with the current crawling bright over the steel, and his face was grey and his jaw was set, and the note was still going.

“I’m all right,” she said. Her voice came out a croak. “Kofi. I’m all right. Keep the note.”

“Keep the note,” Kofi agreed. He did not let go of the contactor. His hand was trembling and he held it anyway, the way you hold a door for someone. “Keep the note. Lian, tell me it’s working.”

“It’s working,” Lian said. Her voice wobbled and then firmed. “It’s listening. The field’s stopped coming up. It’s stopped entirely. It’s listening to the note. Doctor, it’s holding station at three two four and it’s just — listening.”

“Hold it,” Rivas said. “Hold the note until I tell you to stop.”

They held it.

Neve lay on the cold deck and listened to the note and to Kofi’s breathing and to the sea making its slow way past the hull, and she could not feel her right hand, and she could not have said for how long. Then Lian said, softly, “It’s settling. It’s going back down. It’s going down over the floor and it’s curling up the way it does, and it’s going to sleep. Commander, it’s going to sleep in the trench.”

“Kofi,” Rivas said. “Stop the note.”

Kofi let go of the contactor. The note stopped. The cabin was suddenly a small cold room full of the sound of three people breathing.

“Pumps on,” Rivas said. “Heater on. Everything we’ve got, put it back.”

“She’s a flat battery and a bad mood,” Kofi said. “But she’s got enough to climb. Thrusters are yours, Commander.”

Neve sat up. Her right hand was hanging at the end of her wrist and did not answer her. When she lifted it, the palm showed a red mark across it where the brass had been, and the mark had a shape to it, a small bright line like a struck match, and it hurt now, properly, the whole arm of it, and she looked at it with a kind of sorrowful interest.

“You’ll want that wrapped,” Kofi said. He was crouched beside her with a rag off his belt. “Here. Not clean, but it’ll do.” He wound it round and round her palm, and his own hands were shaking, and he did not mention them. “Don’t use it. Don’t hold anything with it. When we’re up, I’m going to want a doctor to look at it, and I don’t mean you.”

“Thank you,” Neve said.

“Don’t,” Kofi said. “Not yet. I’m still cross with you.”

The Cormorant climbed.

The black water went past the viewport and the counter came down steadily, a handful of minutes at a time, and Neve sat in the co-pilot’s seat with her wrapped hand in her lap and watched the depth call out. The Halcyon appeared in the lamp-light at three thousand four, climbing steady like a big patient animal, and Rivas flashed her twice and got a steady blink back.

At two thousand two hundred metres Kofi put the heater back on, and the cold loosened a little, and he sat down on the deck beside her with a mug of water and a face still grey at the edges.

“The Halcyon’s going to beat us up by twenty minutes,” he said. “Achebe’s boat can climb faster than this old cow even when it’s wounded.”

“Then tell her we’re coming up behind her.”

He went to the lamp.

At one thousand eight hundred metres Lian said, “It’s settling. It’s going back down. It’s going back over the floor.” A little later she said, “Doctor, I want you to hear this. I want you to hear it with me.”

Neve came forward and stood behind her chair. Her right hand was a bundle of rag, and she set her left palm flat on the console instead, and listened with Lian.

It was one note, soft and long, coming up the trench behind them, and it was the note she had given it, given back to her the way it had given back the very first ping on the very first day. It went on and on and did not stop and did not change and did not demand anything, and then it faded away into the water like a hand going back down a long corridor.

“Silence,” Lian said. “It’s dormant. It’s quiet. It’s done.”

Rivas said nothing. He brought the boat up at a steady angle, and the black became a black that had a colour in it, and the colour became a sky, and the Cormorant broke the surface close enough to the Halcyon's bow that Kofi yelled and threw up his hands and said they had nearly come up under her.

The hatch went open and the air came down into the cabin, wet and salt and warm as a bath, and it had never in Neve’s life smelled of anything so good, and Kofi started to laugh and could not stop, and Rivas sat with his hands still on the controls and his head down and his shoulders going once, twice.

Neve stood up. She was not sure her legs would hold her, and they did, just, and she picked up her log and held it against her chest with her good hand.

“Twenty minutes of air,” Rivas said, without lifting his head. “Most of it in the pipes. We’d have had the rest in the top of the boat before we surfaced.”

“We had thirty-six hours, Commander,” Neve said. “That was the number. That was the whole number.”

“I know.” He lifted his head. His eyes were red and his face had come undone a little around the mouth, and he looked at her for a long moment. “Write it down before you sit. All of it. The lot. Every name in it.”

“I’ll write it,” Neve said.

“And Neve.” He was not looking at her now; he was looking out through the cracked viewport at the grey Atlantic and the Halcyon’s sail, and the wind came down the hatch and lifted the papers on the chart table. “The next time you tell me something about the deep, before I’ve decided whether to believe you, I’m going to remember that you held a live contactor in your hand and sang. And I am going to believe you.”

She stood in the open hatch with her wrapped hand lying against the coaming and the air going past her like a river, and she did not have anything to say to that, so she said the true thing instead.

“It was never the enemy, Sal. It was only ever frightened.”

“I know,” Rivas said. “Write that down too.”
