Power Play

Kofi lifted the galley freezer wire with two fingers. The compressor sighed, wound down, and stopped. The little green light over the counter went out. The noise of the Cormorant changed by one small degree.

“That’s my ice cream gone,” he said. “I want it noted. Commander, I want it noted that I am a man who gave up his ice cream.”

“Noted,” Rivas said, without looking up. “Fifteen hours, forty.”

“Second, we stop being pretty.”

He killed the cabin strip lights, the two lamps over the bunks, the bulb in the head. The cabin dropped into the red glow of the emergency lamps. After the first tick of surprise Neve found she could see better in it, not worse.

“We keep the console lights,” Kofi said. “We keep the work lamps inside. We keep the scrubber, because we like breathing. The heater stays at the lowest setting that stops our fingers going stupid, and Doctor, do not touch that dial. Last time I set it low you turned it up in your sleep.”

“I don’t touch dials in my sleep.”

“You do. You’ve been touching them all day.”

“Kofi,” Rivas said. “The drum.”

“Yes.” He sat back on his heels. “Here’s where it gets ugly. The drum’s pulling off the aft battery, steady, and the aft battery is meant for the thrusters, and the thrusters are meant for going up. I can’t make a battery bigger. I can stop feeding things that aren’t going up.”

“What’s not going up?”

Kofi counted on his fingers. “The oven. The hot water. The starboard floodlight, which we’re not using, since we’re doing this whole thing in the dark like sensible people. The forward camera heater, and before anybody says anything, I know, we lose the camera if the lens fogs, but we’ve got the passive array and Lian’s ears, and ears don’t fog. And the sonar rack’s standby draw, which is a greedy little thing that sits there breathing power for a job it’s not doing.”

“Lian?” Rivas said.

She had come aft and stood with her arms folded. “The passive array won’t run without the rack warm. If it goes cold, the crystals drift, and I lose the low end. I lose the undernote. That’s where it lives, Commander, all the gentle part of it.”

“How long to warm it back up?”

“Twenty minutes. And I’d be deaf for all twenty.”

Rivas thought about it. His thumb rested on the air counter like a thumb on a bruise.

“Not the array,” he said. “Kofi, give her the twenty minutes now, before we need her. Then take it down and we’ll go blind together later if we must.”

“Doing it,” Kofi said.

He did it slowly, talking to himself the whole way. Neve held the torch and watched him move around the spinning heart of the boat. He pulled the fuse for the camera heater with a pair of pliers and a soft apology. He disconnected the hot-water coil and capped the line. The line dripped once into his palm and he licked it off his thumb, which Neve decided not to mention. He pulled the oven’s feed and then, catching her eye, said, “The oven was never good. It had one temperature, and the temperature was disappointment.”

At the end of half an hour the power panel told a different story. Kofi read it out the way a doctor reads out a chart.

“Draw’s down by a third. The drum’s got maybe three hours now, instead of two. Thrusters have a real charge behind them again.” He wiped his hands on his trousers. “It’s not more air. It’s more time on the same air. But time is the thing we’re short of, so.”

“Fifteen hours twenty,” Rivas said. “And the note’s still singing.”

The note was still singing. Neve could feel it under the deck more than hear it, the long soft swell going out east into the dark, and the field riding it slow and steady. The bloom had drifted away from both subs and lay strung out along the eastern wall in a long pale ribbon. The Halcyon sat clear in the middle of the water with her sail lamp burning.

“Commander,” Lian said. “The Halcyon’s moving.”

They all went forward. The screen showed the long body of the submarine and the bent sail. The shape was coming up off the sediment, the bow lifting first and then the whole length of her.

“She’s surfacing,” Neve said.

“That’s the plan,” Rivas said. “We sing, the field follows, and when the throat clears she goes up. Achebe’s got air, and half a hull of it, and the rock’s off her. She’s not going to sit in a trench full of singing rocks while I do the science.” He leaned into the console. “But she’s early. We haven’t given her the word.” A lamp flashed from the Halcyon’s sail then, short and bright, and he read it and grunted. “There. WAY CLEAR ENOUGH. GOING UP SLOW. WATCH THE ROOF. She’s signalling, at least. Watch her.”

The Halcyon climbed. The camera caught her once against the faint pale bloom of the field along the wall, a long dark arrow with one orange star on top, going up out of the bottom of the world. Then she was past the camera.

“She’s stopped,” Lian said.

Her hand was up, flat, a traffic cop’s hand, and Rivas had not ordered her to say it.

“Why?” he said.

“I don’t know. She’s stopped, and she’s—” Lian pulled the headphones down around her neck. “She’s pinging. Three times now.”

“What does the field do with a ping?”

“What it always does. It answers.”

On the screen, the long pale ribbon along the eastern wall had begun to move. Not as a tide moves, but the way it had moved when the drum first woke it, its whole attention turning like a neck, and the leading edge swung off the wall and up.

“It’s growing upward,” Neve said. “Watch the depth. It was growing along the floor. Now it’s leaving the wall, it’s reaching out over the open water, toward the line she’s coming up on.”

Kofi had come forward and was looking over her shoulder. For once he did not say the funny thing. He said, “It’s building a roof.”

“It’s growing toward the light,” Neve said.

The words came out before she had thought them. She heard them land in the quiet cabin, and knew at once they were true and not quite the whole truth.

“It’s darker than a cellar down here,” Rivas said. “There’s no light.”

“Not yet. But she’s pinging, and the ping goes up and comes back off a hundred things, and she’s the brightest noise it has ever heard. And the warmest.” She widened the view and found the pattern she had been half seeing all day, in the crowns, in the way it gathered. “The surface is the warmest water in the world, the only part of the ocean that ever sees the sun, and it’s been following our heat since this morning. It grew toward us because we were warm. Now she’s going up, and it’s going up after her.”

“It’s not a bad thing, then,” Kofi said. “It’s going home.”

“It’s going to something it has never seen,” Neve said. “It’s been down here in the dark since before there were eyes. It doesn’t know that a submarine is a thing that can be hurt, or that if it keeps growing the way I think it’s growing it will reach the exit and not know to go around. It isn’t chasing her. It doesn’t know what chasing is. There’s a potted fern on somebody’s sill, and we have just opened the curtain.”

“Then we close the curtain,” Rivas said. “Tell her to stop pinging.”

“We can’t tell her fast enough. She’s sounding her way up, the way a pilot does. By the time we blink at her she’ll have sounded twenty times.”

“And each time we answer with a ping, the field answers louder,” Lian said. “Commander, it’s stacking up. Every one of her pings comes back doubled, and the doubling is what it learns. She’s teaching it faster because she’s frightened.”

Rivas said nothing. The air counter ticked over, and he did not say the number aloud, which was worse than when he did.

“What does it do when it reaches her line?” he said at last.

Neve made herself look at the screen before she named it.

“It won’t harm her. It’s not a thing that strikes. But it’s growing up across her only way out. There’s one throat to this bottle, Sal, and it’s building across it, and when it closes, nobody gets up before the air runs out.” She looked up. “Both of us. Not just her.”

The cabin was very quiet. The scrubber sighed. The drum sang its long low note into the dark, and the field swelled to answer it. Neve noticed for the first time that the swell was no longer quite in time.

“Then we starve the drum,” Rivas said. “We stop singing, she stops answering, and we all sit still until it thinks better of it.”

“We tried quiet,” Lian said, and then, seeing his face, “I know. It worked before. But before, it wasn’t going up. It has the taste of it now. If we stop the note, the crowd comes back, and the crowd is the thing that cracks us open.”

“Kofi. Is she right?”

“I’m always right,” Kofi said. “She’s righter than usual.”

“I’m not going to feed it and I’m not going to fight it,” Rivas said. “So what’s left?”

Neve stared at the screen. There was a thing in her head she had been carrying all day and had not let herself take out, because the last time she had shown them a plan, the plan had cracked the viewport. But the plan had been half right, and half right was better than nothing. The crowd had come because she asked too hard. It wanted the long low note. The note was the only word they both knew.

“We give it what it wants,” she said.

“You said the note was the soft thing.”

“It is, and it works. We’ve been walking it east for an hour and it’s been following like a calf behind a bucket. But I’ve been singing it the same small song over and over, and it’s learning the song. Pretty soon the song won’t be enough.” She put her hand flat on the screen, on the bright growing edge. “What if we stop walking it and let it bloom? One sustained note, the biggest, longest, gentlest note we can make, everything we’ve got pointed at it. Not a knock, not a nudge. A voice. If it’s growing toward the light, and we give it a big long beautiful one, it’ll throw itself at the note, it’ll bloom all at once across the throat, and it’ll be down here with us instead of up there with her.”

“Bloom,” Rivas said. “You want it to bloom in the doorway.”

“Yes.”

“The doorway we have to get through.”

“Yes.”

“Neve.” He said her name the way he said the air number. “Last time I let you try a clever thing, my window cracked.”

“I know.”

“Then why should I do it?”

“Because if we do nothing, it closes the throat and we all die in the dark, and if we try this, we might not. I’m not asking you to trust me. I’m asking you to do the arithmetic.”

Rivas looked at the screen. The Halcyon’s ping came through the hull then, faint and patient, and the field answered it, and the leading edge of the growth went up another metre toward the narrowest part of the throat.

“Kofi,” he said, without turning. “Can the drum do a long note? Sustained. Not the swells.”

Kofi was quiet. He had his hands in his pockets, which was where he put them when he was doing the hard sums.

“A sustained note means the driver doesn’t rest. That means we burn through the aft battery in about forty minutes. Once I’ve stripped the boat as far as she’ll go, I might find another ten minutes out of the last emergency cell, and that’s it. After that there’s no thrusters, no scrubber, and we’re a cautionary tale.”

“How much do the thrusters need to get us up?”

“With the Halcyon out of the way and a clear throat, half an hour of steady climb. If we have to stop and think, more.”

“So this plan spends the battery on the bloom and leaves one run at the surface.”

“If the throat opens,” Kofi said. “One run, no stopping, and I would like everyone to go to the toilet beforehand, because there will be no second try.”

“Neve,” Rivas said. “If the bloom closes the throat and doesn’t open again, we’re not just out of air. We’re done.”

“Yes.”

“And if it opens and we’re too early, or too late, or the note carries too loud and it turns on us the way it turned before, we’re done.”

“Yes.”

“And this is your idea. All of it. Not mine.”

The red light made everyone look tired and honest, and she was both. She thought of the unlit pit and the sonar return she had called wrong, and of how the rest of her life had been spent learning that being sorry was not the same as being right, and neither one saved anybody.

“My idea. My call. If it goes badly, it’s on me, and I want it written down before we do it, not after. It was my design, the commander accepted it over his own judgment because I asked him to, and that is its own kind of going wrong. Put that in the log.”

“You’re scared,” he said.

“I’m frightened to death,” Neve said. “I’ve misjudged a species before and caused a fatal panic, and I have never since been able to hear a ping in the dark without hearing that panic in it. I’m quite sure I’m about to get this wrong again.”

“Then why?”

“Because wrong is not the same as doing nothing. Doing nothing is a guarantee. Wrong is only a chance.”

Rivas looked at her a long moment. He pushed off the console and went to the little drawer under the chart table and got out the log tablet, and typed, slow and deliberate, one key at a time, his lips moving.

“Seventeen forty-eight. Power audit by Mensah complete. Non-essential systems off; drum duration extended. Halcyon ascending on the agreed plan and is now blocked by new growth rising toward the exit. Recommendation by Okonkwo: sustained tone, lure the Lattice to bloom at the throat, then close it behind us. Risk: the throat may not open again. Decision accepted by commander over his own judgment, at the recommendation of Dr Okonkwo, who takes responsibility first.” He turned it round. “Read it.”

She read it. It said everything she had asked and a little more, and the little more was the truest part.

“It’s right,” she said.

“Sign it.”

She signed with her fingertip. Rivas signed after her, then Kofi, who made a little ceremony of it, then Lian, who signed and did not look happy about it.

“One more thing,” Rivas said, and looked at Lian. “You’re the one who hears it. If the note’s wrong, if it’s turning on us, you say so. You don’t wait for her, you don’t wait for me. You say so and Kofi cuts the note.”

Lian nodded. “If it’s wrong, I’ll say so.”

“Good. Now we need a voice. Neve, if we’re going to sing to this thing, we need to sing from somewhere it can hear. What’s the strongest thing we’ve got?”

“The drum’s a buzzer,” Kofi said. “It’s a beautiful buzzer, the best I’ve ever made, but it’s the size of my head.”

“What’s bigger?”

“The hull,” Lian said.

Everyone looked at her.

“The hull is a bell,” she said. “It rings whether we want it to or not. This morning it rang because the Lattice touched it. What if we rang it on purpose? Not with a knock, with a note, low and held. The thrusters on their lowest setting, the two together, not pushing, just humming. That would go for miles. That would be the whole boat singing.”

“The thrusters are the one thing we can’t spend,” Kofi said. “That’s the ladder out.”

“Then we don’t spend them,” Neve said, the sum already running. “We don’t run them. We let the boat ring. Kofi, is there a way to make the whole hull sing without spending the thrusters?”

Kofi was very still. Then he began to smile, the smile of a man whose head had caught fire, and he pulled the panel cover off the power bay again and knelt down and looked into it.

“There’s a frame ring,” he said. “Sits around the resonance chamber. I was going to give it the humming note through the hull transducer and I didn’t, because the drum did the job. But the ring talks to the hull. We could put a standing wave in her. Not a big one, just enough to make the steel hum, and the water will carry the hum a long way. Cleaner than the drum. It’ll draw on the emergency cells, but slower than the thrusters ever did.”

“How long will they last?”

“Long enough,” Kofi said, and then, because he did not lie twice in a day, “I think. Give me an hour, and I’ll tell you yes or no.”

“Fifteen ten,” Rivas said. “Get me a voice, Kofi. Then we teach the Halcyon to listen.”

Neve went forward to the console, and began to spell the plan out against the dark, one flash at a time.

The lamp work took longer than the note. She spelled it in the fastest flashes the Halcyon’s signaller could read, and kept it short, because a frightened crew does not want a plan, it wants the shortest plan. WE HAVE A VOICE. STAY PUT. DO NOT PING. WE SING LOW, HOLD TWO MINUTES, YOU SING HIGH WITH US ON OUR COUNT. WHEN THE THROAT OPENS, UP FAST.

The answer came after a long time. WE ARE UNDER SOMETHING. WHAT IS IT.

Neve flashed back: ALIVE. FRIGHTENED. WE ARE BEING KIND TO IT. DO NOT PING.

Another long wait. Then: UNDERSTOOD. ACHEBE SAYS MAKE IT FAST. SHE SAYS THE ROOF IS COMING DOWN.

She read it twice. Rivas leaned over her shoulder and read it too.

“She’s giving us the top of the chord,” Neve said. “The high note carries least, so she wants to lead.” She flashed the acknowledgement; the Halcyon answered with one hard blink and went dark on the lamp.

By seventeen-twenty Kofi had the panel closed and sat back on his heels.

“Done. Transducer bolted to the frame ring, tone generator off the emergency cells, and she’ll sing through her own bones. It’s not a knock, Doctor. It’s the boat herself.”

“Will it be pretty?” Lian asked.

“Steel doesn’t give you one note, it gives you a bundle. Some of the overtones are not pretty. If one lands wrong we don’t calm the thing, we annoy it in a key it’s never heard. I want you on the array to tell me if the note’s wrong.”

“She will,” Neve said.

“I know. I like saying it out loud where the commander can hear.”

“Start her soft,” Rivas said. “Just enough so we hear she’s in tune. One minute. If the field so much as turns its head, we stop.”

The Cormorant sang.

It was not loud. Neve had braced for something enormous, and instead the note came up through the deck into her soles like the lowest pedal of an organ played three doors away. The hull whispered. The water took the whisper and stretched it out, and the passive array drew back a picture of it travelling east and up along the wall, long and clean and patient.

“That’s the note,” Lian said. “That’s the one. It matches the long note from the silence. Doctor, it matches.”

“Hold it there,” Neve said. “Don’t push.”

A minute. The field did not turn. And then, slowly, the long pale ribbon along the eastern wall began to swing off the wall and down, out over the open water.

“It’s leaving the throat,” Lian said. “It’s coming down toward us. Commander, it’s coming the way a cat comes when you call it.”

“Let it come,” Neve said. “Kofi, hold the note exactly where it is.”

Half a minute, and the field came over the western ridge in a slow pale tide, and Neve saw it whole for the first time. Not a wall, not a ribbon. A forest of crowns and stalks and long translucent blades, packed so close the shapes blurred into one soft brightness, and it moved with the note as grass moves in a wind that starts far away.

“It’s fanning out,” she said. “Lian, the bloom’s going everywhere, not to the note. It’s spreading.”

“It’s the overtones,” Lian said. “The note’s clean, but the steel’s singing its own. There’s a high one riding on top and another under it, and the field’s answering all of them. It’s not one song any more. It’s a crowd.”

The bright tide kept spreading, and the leading edge of it was moving back up the wall toward the throat, swelling as it went.

“The throat,” Neve said.

“It’s moving across it,” Lian said. “It’s gathering at the edges like frost on a window. It’s creeping inward. Commander, the opening’s getting smaller.”

“Kofi. How long can she hold that note?”

“Forty minutes on the cells, and then it gets ugly.”

“Cut it,” Rivas said. “Kofi, cut it now.”

Kofi was already moving. The whisper stopped. The silence after it was the loudest thing Neve had heard all day, and in the silence she heard the Cormorant tick and settle.

“The throat?” Rivas said.

Lian was watching the screen with her hands very still. “Still open,” she said, and then, after a moment, “It’s stopped growing inward. It’s settled like frost on a window and it’s just gone to sleep.”

“Then we’ve lost nothing but power,” Rivas said, and then he looked at the air counter and did not say the number.

“Commander,” Kofi said, from the power bay. “We’ve lost power, and I can’t find us any more of it. The drum can sing or the thrusters can climb, not both. Not for more than half an hour of climb.”

“Then we have one song left in us,” Rivas said, “and we spend it whole, not in pieces. Neve, tell the Halcyon to hold her depth and listen for us.”

Neve put the lamp to the window and flashed it, short and slow, so the signaller could read it: HOLD DEPTH. WE SING. LISTEN. DO NOT PING.

The Halcyon’s lamp blinked back, one long eye-watering burst: HOLDING.
