The Answer

Forty minutes of work in the dark, and for the first ten of them the only voice in the cabin belonged to Kofi, who talked to the hull in a low steady murmur while his hands moved.

“All right,” he said. “All right, sweetheart. Let’s have a look at you.”

He had rigged a work lamp on a magnetic base and stuck it to the deck above the starboard ballast tank. By its light he lay on his back with his head in the gap between the hull frame and the equipment rack, so that from the front of the cabin he looked like a man who had dropped something and was thinking about giving up on it. The resin tape was warm in his hand. He had three rolls. He had been saving two.

Neve watched from the console, where she had no business watching. She should have been at the sonar with Lian, or on the passive array, or anywhere at all. Instead she stood with her arms folded and her eyes on the crack.

A crack it was now. Not a hairline. Not the small bright thread in the viewport seal that had made her blood go cold in the first hour. This one ran through the frame above the ballast tank, along the weld line, and it had opened the way a seam opens in a coat that is too tight, one stitch at a time and then all of it at once. Kofi had measured it with a steel rule and said a number, and then said the number again in a different tone, and Rivas had heard both tones.

“How long?” Rivas had asked.

“Two hours,” Kofi had said. “Maybe three. On tape and good behaviour.”

“And on no tape?”

“Commander,” Kofi had said gently, “let’s not.”

Now he pressed the first strip across the crack, worked it down with the heel of his hand, and pressed the second crossways over the first. The tape made a small wet sound as it took. Kofi smoothed it, smoothed it again, then put his ear against the hull an inch below the patch and closed his eyes.

“Is that a good face?” Rivas asked from the pilot’s seat. His hands stayed on the throttles, his eyes on the console, where the thrusters still read amber, a patient amber that had not improved in forty minutes.

“That’s my listening face,” Kofi said. “This is my worried face. The difference is subtle. They’re both handsome.”

“Kofi.”

“She’s held,” Kofi said, and sat up, and wiped his hands on his trousers. “For now. That’s the honest answer. The tape’s not a weld. It’s a tourniquet. It buys us hours, not days, and every time the thrusters surge, the frame flexes, and every time the frame flexes, the tourniquet gets a little less fresh.” He looked at the patch, then at Neve, and his voice lost its joke the way a room loses its warmth when a door opens. “I can keep her together. But I can’t keep her together and fight a war. So one of you has to decide what we’re fighting, because I’d like to know before I run out of tape.”

The cabin went quiet. Outside the viewport the water was black and still and full of nothing, and the Cormorant hung in it the way a spider hangs in a corner of a ceiling, out of the way and not safe.

“We’re fighting a rescue,” Rivas said. “We dive back in, we take Halcyon’s people off if she can’t surface, we go home. That’s the whole war.”

“And the thing in the trench?”

“Is a problem for the trip home,” Rivas said. “Not the trip there.”

“It doesn’t work like that,” Neve said.

She had been quiet a long while. Her voice sounded thin and strange in the small cabin, and she kept it even. “It hears us. Every time we make a sound, it makes a sound. We pinged it, it answered. We stopped, it stopped. We started up again — lamps, thrusters, sonar — and it came closer and it changed its song.” She turned from the viewport to face him. “You’re planning a dive that involves active sonar all the way down. And a haul. And talking to Halcyon the whole time.”

“Yes,” Rivas said. “That’s what a rescue involves.”

“Then every metre we go, we get louder, and every metre it answers louder, and the last time it answered louder, my viewport seal grew a crack and your frame grew a fissure.” She said it flatly, without heat, because heat would have been a kind of giving up; she wanted him to hear the numbers. “Sal. The pattern isn’t that it’s chasing us. The pattern is that we are ringing a bell, and something is ringing back, and each time it rings back harder.”

“Which is why I’m going to ring a bell it can’t ignore,” Rivas said.

The cabin got quieter than before. Lian took her headphones off.

“Say that again,” Neve said.

“The sonar,” Rivas said. “Full power. Every ping together, both arrays, at the loudest setting the array can take, and then again, and then again until it goes away.” He turned his hand over on the console as though he were turning over a card. “I’m not going to fight a thing I can’t see. I’m going to shout at it until it goes somewhere quieter to sulk. That’s not war, that’s housekeeping.”

“It’s a weapon,” Neve said. “Underwater, a military array used close and hard is a weapon. It can stun a whale from a mile off. It can—”

“It isn’t a whale,” Rivas said. “It’s a wall of crystal at four kilometres that doesn’t like being looked at.”

“We don’t know what it is.”

“No,” Rivas said. “We don’t. Because the only tool we have for finding out is the tool you want me to put away.”

A good stroke, and Neve felt it land. She stood with her hands at her sides and made herself breathe. Four kilometres of water above her, and above the water, air, and above the air, everything she wanted: a lab, a set of samples, a quiet room where she could look at what she had seen. None of it was here, and the thing she had seen was out there in the dark, doing something.

“Commander,” Lian said. “Can I say the other thing?”

“Everybody else has,” Rivas said. “Say it.”

Lian held up her headphones the way a person holds up a letter. “I’ve been on passive since we backed off,” she said. “It’s still out there, and it’s still making sound, and I’ve been writing it down.” She turned her tablet around so they could see. “Listen. I’m not going to play it, I’m going to show you. This is the echo from before, when it was three notes and a hum. And this is the echo now.”

On the tablet, two wide lines of wavy ink ran across the screen. The first was simple: three smooth crests and a long fall. The second was dense, a comb of small teeth repeating, and repeating, and repeating.

“That’s a pattern,” Neve said.

“It’s a phrase,” Lian said. “Listen—sorry. Look. There’s a break here, and here, and here. It plays the same run of notes, a fast bit, then a slow bit, then a run of little taps, and then a silence, and then it starts again from the top. It’s the same shape every time.” She put her finger on the screen. “And the gap between one phrase starting and the next is getting shorter. Not much. A few seconds each time.”

“Shortening,” Kofi said slowly. “That’s what people do when they want you to answer. They leave a space and then they say the whole thing again. Slightly louder.”

“Yes,” Lian said. “That’s exactly what it is.”

Rivas was looking at the lines on the tablet, and his face had gone from impatient to something Neve could not read, which was worse. “Or,” he said, “it’s a thing in a trench getting nearer, and the nearer it gets, the louder it gets, and the faster it gets.”

“That’s the other reading,” Lian admitted. “I don’t have a way to tell them apart from here.”

“I do,” Rivas said.

“Sal,” Neve said. “Don’t.”

“I’m not going to fire a torpedo at it, Doctor. I’m going to make one ping. One. Loud, short, and aimed down and away from both boats.” He reached past her for the array control. “If it’s talking, it’ll stop talking and listen, and we’ll learn something. And if it’s coming, it’ll flinch, and we’ll learn something faster.”

Neve put her hand on his wrist.

She had never done that in her life. Not to a ship’s commander, not to anyone. His skin was warm and his pulse was steady and he did not move his arm.

“One ping,” she said. “Because if I’m right, one ping is survivable. If you’re right, one ping settles it and we dive. But not a volley. Not a shout. One question, and then we listen to the answer for ten minutes before you ask another.”

Rivas looked at her hand for a moment. Then at her face.

“One ping,” he said. “Aimed low and away, and then ten minutes of quiet. And if it comes at us, Doctor, we’re not going to have ten minutes, and you’re going to hear me say the thing I don’t want to say.”

“Which is what?”

“That I told you,” Rivas said.

The ping went out at 06:32.

Neve felt it in the hull before she heard it: a small hard shiver, a kick in the frame like a heartbeat that was not hers. The screen lit. The green lines of the trench wall bloomed, the floor resolved into a wide scatter of returns, and somewhere out past the limits of the screen, down and away, the sound ran out into the dark and touched whatever was down there.

“Return,” Lian said. Her voice was very calm. “Coming back. Going out. Coming back.” A pause. “Coming back louder.”

The Cormorant shuddered.

Not the shiver of the ping. The hull taking a blow, a long low flex that ran down the length of the boat and rang in the steel, and above the ballast tank the lamp on Kofi’s magnetic base jumped sideways and swung. Kofi was already out of his seat, already under the patch, both hands flat on the frame.

“It moved,” he said. “The crack moved. It’s under the tape, it’s spreading under the tape, I can feel it through the weld—”

“How much?”

“A centimetre. Maybe two. Commander, it’s a frame, it’s not a skin, and when a frame goes, it goes all at once, that’s the whole point of a frame—” Kofi grabbed the second roll of tape, then stopped, and looked up at Neve with an expression she would remember. Not fear. A man deciding to spend his last good thing. “Doctor. That crack moved because the ping hit us, or because the thing out there answered?”

“It answered,” Lian said. “It answered at four times the strength. I’ve got it on the array, it’s—” She pulled her headphones off as if they had grown hot. “That wasn’t an echo. That was a reply. It used our own ping. It took our ping and gave it back bigger.”

“It’s a resonator,” Neve said. She heard her own voice come out steady and did not know how it was doing that. “That’s what crystal does. It takes sound and it gives it back. The more you put in, the more it gives out. And every time it gives out, it hits this boat.”

“Then we hit it first,” Rivas said. He had both hands on the console, and the amber light had gone red. “Full array. Now. Before it answers again.”

“No,” Neve said.

“Doctor—”

“Sal, listen to the box you’re standing in. You hit it with the full array, and if it’s a resonator, you are not shouting at a dog. You are striking a bell with a hammer while you stand inside the bell.” She pointed at the crack. “That’s what the last ping did. Twice that, from both arrays, and Kofi runs out of tape, and then we are all in a sub that is coming apart at four kilometres, and the Halcyon is eleven people who will watch it happen and then wait for air that isn’t coming.”

The cabin was very quiet. Rivas stood with his hands on the array control, and did not move them, and his jaw worked once.

“You’re asking me to go into a rescue blind,” he said. “Silent. No sonar, no lamps, in a boat with a crack in her frame.”

“I’m asking you to go in quiet,” Neve said. “Those are different things. Passive hearing still works. We can see the rock with the floor-mapper at low power, we can work by Halcyon’s lamp and ours, and we can talk to Achebe on the acoustic phone, which is a whisper, not a shout.”

“A whisper into a microphone with a crack in my hull.”

“Yes.”

Rivas took his hands off the console. He turned in his seat and looked at her for a long moment, and behind the bluntness of him she saw something she had not expected: he was scared, and he was managing it the way he managed everything, by making it into a procedure.

“You asked me for a number, and I gave you one,” he said. “Now I want yours. What happens if you’re wrong?”

“We lose air,” Neve said. “We take longer, we go slower, and we have thirty-five hours in Halcyon and twenty-one in this boat. If I’m wrong, that’s the cost. Hours.”

“And if I’m wrong?” Rivas said. “If we go loud and it’s not a resonator, if it’s just a thing that gets angry, and I’ve given its position away and made it decide we’re the problem?”

“Then the same thing happens,” Neve said. “Except faster. Because there is one other thing I haven’t said, and I’m going to say it now.” She put her hand on the hull beside the patch, where Kofi had been listening. “This boat is already a noise. Do you understand? The thrusters, the pumps, the fans, the oxygen scrubbers, the hum of the lights—we’ve been singing to the trench since the moment we arrived. Every boat that comes down here does. The only quiet thing we have left to give it is us not shouting on top of it.”

Kofi had not said anything for a while. Now he sat back on his heels and looked at the two of them.

“If it helps,” he said, “and I know it won’t, I’ve done a thing.” He held up the roll of tape. “This is the last roll. I’ve laid a second patch over the first, crosswise again, and I’ve put a strap of webbing around the whole frame and tensioned it to the equipment rack. It’s ugly. It’s the ugliest thing I have ever built, and I once built a coffee maker out of a sonar housing. But the frame can’t flex past the strap. It’ll hold. Call it four hours of hard work, or six hours of going gentle.” He looked at Rivas. “Commander, I’d like to go gentle.”

“That’s not a reason,” Rivas said.

“No,” Kofi agreed. “But I fix engines, not monsters, and here’s what I notice. If a man comes into my shop banging two pans together and says the noise is how he fixes the problem, I tell him to put down the pans. You don’t shout at a thing you don’t understand. You get quiet and you look at it.”

“That’s not how a rescue works,” Rivas said. “A rescue is loud. A rescue is somebody hearing you and coming. If we go quiet, Achebe’s people won’t hear us and we won’t hear them, and we’ll be two submarines feeling our way across a trench at four kilometres down by touch, like blind men in a dark room.”

“Yes,” Neve said. “That’s exactly what it is.”

“And you’re fine with that.”

“No.” The word came out harder than she meant. “I am not fine with it. I’m frightened of it. But I have been wrong about the sea before, Commander, and it cost people, and the way it happened was that I decided what I was seeing before I’d finished looking. So I’m not going to do that again. And the thing is, the reason I know it’s reacting and not hunting is that it keeps matching us. It went quiet when we went quiet. It came back when we came back. It gave us back our own ping with our own shape in it. A hunter doesn’t do that. A hunter doesn’t care what you sound like.”

Rivas sat with that. The tunnel of the deep pressed in on the viewport, black and heavy, and somewhere below it something was still sending up a slow, patient pattern of taps.

He did not concede. That was the thing Neve remembered afterwards, and the thing she was grateful for. He sat with his hands flat on the console and his eyes on the red light, and he took his time, and when he answered her he answered as the man who had to live with whatever came next.

“I’m not going to say the word,” he said. “Not because I think you’re wrong. Because I don’t like a decision that costs me nothing to make. If I say it now, I own the quiet and I sleep tonight, and if it comes apart it comes apart on your name and not mine.” He looked up. “Put it in the log. The recommendation is yours, the acceptance is mine. Log it as the biologist’s recommendation, accepted by the commander. Both our names in the same line.”

“Both our names,” Neve said. “In the same line.”

“And one more thing.” He turned back to the console. “We go quiet, and we go slow. Half speed on the thrusters, no lamps in the water, and if the passive array gives me anything I don’t like, I’m not going to sit on it and be brave. I’ll say so out loud and we’ll argue again.”

“That’s all I want,” Neve said.

“Then we’re agreed.” He began to shut things down. He did it in order, the way he did everything: array, transmitter, then the lamps one by one, and the cabin went from red to almost nothing, and from almost nothing to a single small glow on the oxygen panel like a coal in a cold grate.

The silence was almost complete. The Cormorant spoke only in the small sounds a submarine makes when it is trying to be quiet and cannot quite manage it: the tick of the hull cooling, the whisper of the scrubbers, the faint high note of the pumps turning over in their sleep. Neve sat down at last, and found that her legs had been waiting for permission.

In the dark, Lian put her headphones back on. Neve could hear the faint delicate tick of a stylus on the tablet as Lian began to write, and then the hull, settling, the last creak of a frame with a strap around it going quiet.

“It’s still there,” Lian said at last, very softly. “Still tapping. But it’s slower now.” A pause, and her voice changed, went careful and wondering, the voice of a person who has found a thing in the dark and is holding it up to see. “Doctor. It played our ping back. But it played it back in order, not as one bang. It’s been going through it, bit by bit, over and over. Like—” She stopped, and started again. “Like a child picking out a tune on a piano. It’s got our sound. It’s practising it.”

Neve turned the words over the way she turned over a specimen, and found nothing in them that needed forcing. “Then we give it time to learn,” she said. “And we learn it while it does.”

Rivas did not answer. He sat with his hand on the throttles and his eyes on the dark, and after a while he said, quietly, to nobody in particular, “Twenty-one hours. In this boat.”

The number went round the cabin and settled. Neve thought of the Halcyon’s thirty-five and the eleven people breathing it, and of the patient tapping going on and on below them, and of how strange it was that the only thing they had left to spend was their own silence.

Outside, the deep was black and cold and patient, and four kilometres of water stood over the Cormorant like a held breath, and down in the dark something tapped out a song it had copied from them and waited, with enormous interest, to hear what came next.
