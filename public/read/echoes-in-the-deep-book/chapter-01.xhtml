The Descent

The Cormorant began to sink at 05:48. By 05:49 Commander Sal Rivas had already started counting.

“Fifty metres,” he said. The viewport gave him black water and the occasional spark of bioluminescence, tiny cold fires that bloomed and died. “One hundred. One-fifty. That’s the last of the light gone, Doctor. Say goodbye to it.”

Neve Okonkwo kept her eyes on the sonar screen, where the trench wall fell away in green lines, and thought about the last time she had been this deep. She was younger then. Sure of herself, which is a different kind of danger.

“Two hundred,” Rivas said. “Pressure’s climbing. Kofi, talk to me.”

“Hull’s happy,” Kofi Mensah said from the rear of the cabin. He sat with his knees against a crate of spare parts and a roll of resin tape looped over one shoulder like a bandolier. “Stress levels nominal. Which is engineer for nothing has exploded yet. Give it time.”

“Comforting,” Rivas said. “Two-sixty. Three hundred. You know, I’ve done this depth eleven times and it never gets less strange, going down when the water stops being water and starts being a wall.”

He was not wrong. At four hundred metres the Cormorant began to groan, a low complaint from the hull as the sea pressed in, and the sound settled into Neve’s teeth. She felt it in her back molars, a vibration you could not blink away. The cabin was small enough that she could touch both walls if she spread her arms. She kept them in her lap. The air smelled of warm electronics and Kofi’s coffee, which he brewed in a battered flask strapped to the console and which he claimed was the only thing keeping the sub’s morale above sea level.

“How’s our patient?” Neve asked.

Lian Wu looked up from her station. The youngest of them, quiet in a way that made people lean in to hear her, and she wore her headphones around her neck even when she was not using them, the way other people wore a watch. “Halcyon is holding,” she said. “She’s still pinging. Every ninety seconds, regular as a heartbeat.”

“Good,” Neve said. “That means they have power and someone’s thinking clearly.”

“It also means they’re scared,” Lian said. “That’s a scared ping. Same interval every time because someone set a timer and is watching it.”

Rivas grunted. “Five hundred. I’ll take scared. Scared crews keep their helmets on.”

“Or they run out of air on schedule,” Lian said, almost to herself, and then pressed her lips together as if she had said more than she meant to.

The descent went on. Neve had done the arithmetic before they left the surface: the Marrow Deep was four thousand metres down, which was roughly four hundred atmospheres of pressure, which meant every square centimetre of the viewport was carrying the weight of a small car. The glass was several inches thick, tapered at the edge so the sea itself pressed the window into its seat. She had helped design the seal. She trusted the seal. That did not stop her from watching it.

“Six hundred,” Rivas said. “Seven. Lian, give me a course correction for the shelf. I want to come in wide of the wall.”

“Ten degrees port,” Lian said. “And then hold. There’s a spur at eleven hundred that’ll shadow the thrusters if we hug the rock.”

“Copy.”

Neve listened to them work and found herself, as she often did when the descent grew long, sorting her own reasons for being here. The official ones she would give a review board: the Halcyon was a research submarine, the Marrow Deep was a place she had studied from the surface for a decade, and no one alive knew more about what lived in the cold water than she did. The other reasons lived in the space behind her ribs and did not care about paperwork.

“Eight hundred,” Rivas said. “Half an hour to the shelf at this rate. Kofi, what are you doing back there?”

“Talking to the hull,” Kofi said. “She’s a good girl. She’s a little stiff in the starboard flank, but she’s holding. I’ve got strain gauges on the frame every two metres, so if anything starts singing out of key, I’ll hear it.”

“We’ll all hear it,” Lian said softly.

Neve glanced at her. “Something wrong?”

“No.” Lian put her headphones on, then took them off again, as if she could not decide whether she wanted to hear more or less. “It’s the geology. The trench wall should be layered sediment and basalt, and mostly it is. But there’s a return coming back that doesn’t fit.”

“What kind of return?”

“A soft one. Like sonar hitting something that isn’t quite solid.” Lian frowned at her screen. “Or maybe it’s a ghost. The trench is full of thermoclines. Cold water bends sound. I’ll keep listening.”

“Nine hundred,” Rivas said. “One thousand. We’re passing the midnight zone now. No light at all down here. If you turned off the lamps, you couldn’t see your hand in front of your face.”

“Thank you, Commander,” Neve said. “That’s very restful.”

“I aim to soothe.”

The Cormorant shuddered. A small thing, no more than a hiccup in the thrusters, but Neve felt it travel up through the deck and into her knees. Kofi’s head came up.

“That wasn’t me,” he said.

“Current,” Rivas said. “We’re in the wall’s shadow. There’s a cold waterfall coming down the eastern side. It’ll push us around a little.”

“It’s a four-kilometre waterfall,” Kofi said. “Forgive me if I’m not comforted.”

“Fifteen hundred,” Rivas said. “Two thousand. Halfway. Air’s good. Cryo’s good. Everybody breathe normally, and if you can’t, breathe like you’re being charged by the hour.”

Neve went back to the sonar. She had a habit, which she knew was not scientific, of talking to the deep as if it were listening. A bad habit. It had cost her before. But the trench was so quiet, and the green lines on the screen were so patient, and she found herself thinking: we are the loudest thing for four kilometres in every direction. Everything down here lived in a hush. Sound travelled five times faster in water than in air, and in the deep cold it travelled clean and far. A single ping could cross a valley and come back changed. She had spent years telling people that the sea was not a place of total silence, and still, every time she came down, the quiet surprised her. Not empty quiet. Crowded. A room full of people who had all agreed to whisper.

“Two thousand five hundred,” Rivas said. “Getting heavy now. Kofi, give me a read on the frame.”

“Starboard flange is running a little warmer than I’d like,” Kofi said. He climbed half out of his seat, braced a hand on the hull above him, and pressed his palm flat to the metal as if taking a temperature. “Not hot. Warm. Like a kettle you haven’t switched on yet. I’ll keep an eye.”

“Three thousand. Three-two.”

The Cormorant groaned again, longer this time, and the sound did not fade so much as sink into the background and stay there. Neve looked at the viewport seal. The glass sat in its tapered seat, black water beyond it, and she could see the faint reflection of her own face in the inner pane: a woman in her late thirties with her hair pulled back so tightly it pulled at her temples, and eyes that had learned to watch for things going wrong.

She had learned that on a different boat. A warmer sea, a smaller budget, a species she had called dangerous when it was only frightened. A crew who had trusted her. A man who had not come back up. She could still feel the exact quality of the water that day, the way it had been almost too warm to be honest, like a bath someone had left running. She had been right about the animal’s teeth and wrong about its intentions. The distinction had not saved anyone. The review board had cleared her. She had not cleared herself. Sometimes, in the small hours, she still wrote the report in her head, a sentence at a time, looking for the line where certainty had become something else.

“Three thousand four,” Rivas said. “Doctor, you’re quiet. That’s usually a bad sign.”

“I’m thinking.”

“Don’t. It’s a rescue, not a research cruise. We go down, we hook the Halcyon, we pull. Nice and simple.”

“Nothing is simple at four kilometres,” Neve said. “The Halcyon’s pinned under a rockfall. We don’t know the angle, we don’t know the load, and we don’t know what the debris field is sitting on. If the slope is loose, our thrusters could bring the whole face down on both of us.”

“That’s the cheerful version,” Rivas said. “Give me the other one.”

“The other one is that we have thirty-six hours of air for the Halcyon, and rather less for us, and I would like to spend the first of those hours learning the ground before we touch it.”

Rivas made a small sound, not quite a laugh. “You know what I like about you, Doctor? You do the arithmetic I don’t want to hear and you make it sound like a lecture.” He checked his gauges. “Three thousand six hundred. You have one hour. Kofi, lights on.”

The lamps came up, and the black water turned a hard, cold blue. The trench wall came rushing out of the dark, close enough that Neve could see the ledges and the cracks and the pale mat-like growths that clung to the rock. Then the wall fell away, and they were over the floor of the Marrow Deep, and the floor went on and on. The lamps reached out maybe twenty metres and gave up, and beyond that the dark was not a colour but a weight.

“Four thousand metres,” Rivas said, at six thirty-two on the console clock. “Welcome to the bottom of the world.”

Neve let out a breath she had not known she was holding. In the cockpit it was silent for a moment, the four of them looking out at water that had never been lit by the sun. There was a quality to the deep, a thickness, a sense of having descended into the sea’s own hold, where it kept the things it did not show anyone.

They found the Halcyon by her pings before they found her by light.

Lian had the bearing first. “She’s off to port, about six hundred metres, and she’s still on her ninety-second timer.” She adjusted a dial, and the pings came through the cabin speaker, each one a clean, bright tone followed by the long fall of its echo. “Hear that? That’s her. That’s the heartbeat.”

“And the rockfall?” Rivas asked.

“Big,” Lian said. “She’s under a slab. I can see the edge of it in the return. They’re not going anywhere without help.”

“Six hundred metres,” Rivas said. “Kofi, status.”

“Starboard flange is warmer,” Kofi said. “Still nominal. Still boring. I’d like to keep it that way.”

The Cormorant slid forward over the trench floor, and Neve watched the lamps pick out the strangeness of it: the pale sediment, the scattered rubble. She caught herself leaning toward the glass to look at a fish with a lure on a stalk, patient as a fisherman, and made herself sit back. The deep was not empty. She had spent her whole career saying so, and still, when the lamps found something new, she felt the old pull in her chest, the wonder that came before the science. She had learned to distrust that pull a little, in the years since the warmer sea. Wonder and certainty could wear the same coat.

“There,” Lian said. “I’ve got a hard return. That’s her hull.”

The Halcyon came out of the dark slowly, a long grey shape lying at an angle under a slab of rock the size of a house. Her sail was bent. Her running lights were dark, but a single work lamp burned at her bow, and in its glow Neve could see the sediment settling out of the water like snow. The water around her was not moving. It hung there, still as oil, and the lamp made a small brave circle in all that black.

“She’s alive,” Rivas said. “Halcyon, this is Cormorant. We have you visual. Do you read?”

The speaker crackled. “Cormorant, Halcyon. We read you. Are we glad to see you.” The voice was a woman’s, tired and steady. “This is Captain Achebe. We’ve got eleven aboard. Hull’s holding, but we’re pinned. We’ve tried reverse thrusters. The slab doesn’t move.”

“Copy that,” Rivas said. “We’re going to come alongside and take a look at your tow points. Don’t do anything energetic. We’ve got time.”

“Thirty-six hours of it,” Captain Achebe said. “We’ve been counting.”

“So have we,” Rivas said, and clicked off. Then, quieter: “Kofi, I want a full stress read before we get any closer. The floor here looks soft, and I don’t like the way the debris is stacked.”

“On it,” Kofi said, and began moving along the rear bulkhead, checking the gauges with a small torch held in his teeth.

Neve was not listening. She was watching the sonar, where the Halcyon’s ping had gone out and come back, and then gone out again, and in between there had been something else. A small return, faint as a fingertip brushing a drum.

“Lian,” she said. “Play back the last ping. The one after.”

Lian frowned, but she ran the recording, and the cabin speaker gave them the clean bright tone of the Halcyon’s sonar, and then its echo, and then, after it, the soft pulse again. It came at a regular interval.

“That’s not geology,” Lian said. Her voice had gone very quiet. She pulled her headphones on with both hands, pressing them to her ears as if she were listening through a wall. “Geology doesn’t keep time.”

“What is it, then?” Rivas asked.

“I don’t know,” Lian said. “But it’s close. And it’s getting closer. And it’s… wide. It’s not a point. It’s like a whole cathedral wall of sound coming back at once.”

Neve leaned toward the screen. The return was soft and it was not moving like a rock. It was moving like something growing.

“Cormorant,” Captain Achebe said over the speaker, and her voice had changed. “We’re getting a vibration in the hull. It started about a minute ago. Is that you?”

“Negative,” Rivas said. “We’re holding station. Kofi—”

“I see it,” Kofi said. He had dropped the torch into his hand and was staring at a gauge as if it had insulted him. “Starboard flange just jumped two degrees. And the frame’s singing. Commander, something’s loading the hull.”

“From where?”

“From outside.”

At the edge of the lamp’s reach, the sediment moved. Not the way it moves under a current. It moved the way a field moves when the wind crosses it, in a slow, wide ripple, and the ripple was coming toward them.

“Commander,” Neve said. “Turn off the sonar.”

“What?”

“Turn it off. Active and passive. Now.”

Rivas stared at her. His hand stayed on the console. “Doctor, we’re in the middle of a rescue. I’m not flying blind because you’ve got a feeling.”

“Every time the Halcyon pings, something answers. Every time we ping, something answers louder. That ripple is not the trench and it is not the rock. It is something alive, and it is reacting to us.”

“Something alive at four thousand metres,” Rivas said. “In the dark. In the cold.”

“Yes,” Neve said. “That’s exactly where things live.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No,” she said. “I don’t. But I know the alternative. If I’m wrong, we lose a few minutes of data. If you’re wrong, we lose the boat.” She heard the words come out of her and recognised the shape of them. They were the words she should have said on a warmer sea, years ago, and had not. She had not wanted to be wrong in front of people who were watching. She had wanted to be right, and the wanting had been louder than the listening. “Please, Sal. I’ve been wrong before. I know exactly how it feels. This is not that.”

Rivas looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw in her face, he did not say.

The Cormorant shuddered again, harder this time, and somewhere in the hull a small, sharp sound rang out like a fingernail tapped against a glass. Kofi came out of his seat.

“That’s the starboard sheer strake,” he said. “Commander, the flange weld has opened a little. Nothing through. But I need everybody to be very calm and very still.”

“Define still,” Rivas said, and his voice had flattened into the place it went when he was counting costs.

“Still like a mouse in a room with a cat,” Kofi said.

Neve looked at the frame. At the edge of the weld, a hairline had opened in the paint, thin as a thread of light. Not deep. Not through. But new, and they were four kilometres down, and the sea was already pressing at it with the weight of a small car.

“Rivas,” she said. “Please. Go quiet.”

Rivas looked at the paint crack. He looked at Neve. He looked at the sonar screen, where the soft return was still moving toward them, slow and patient and wide. Then he did the thing that Neve would remember later, when everything else had gone wrong and come right again: against his own judgment, he turned his head away from her, and reached past her to the console, and held the active array on.

“Lian,” he said. “One more ping. Aimed low, along the floor and away. If she’s wrong, we get a picture of whatever it is and we haul the Halcyon out while it watches us. If she’s right—” He shrugged, a small hard movement of the shoulders. “Then we’ll have learned something.”

“Sal,” Neve said. “Don’t.”

“I hear you, Doctor. I’m doing it anyway.” He nodded to Lian. “Now.”

The ping went out. In the cabin they heard nothing; the array was outside, and the sound it made was meant for water. What they heard was its return.

It came back four times as loud as it had gone out, an answering report that struck the hull like a struck bell, and the Cormorant rang with it from bow to stern. The paint crack spread, a long grey line running up the weld, and Kofi was already moving, already reaching for the resin tape, his voice going up the way a man’s voice goes when he is doing arithmetic in his head and does not like the answer.

“That’s opened again, that’s a working crack now, and she’s loading the frame every time it answers. Commander, I can patch this, but I can’t keep patching it if the thing outside is going to shout at us every time we say a word.”

Rivas stood with both hands on the console. His jaw was set. He had been wrong, and everyone in the cabin knew it, and he did not turn around to say so. Neve did not say it for him. She had wanted to be right, once, more than she had wanted to listen. She knew that face from the inside.

“All stations,” he said at last. “Silence protocol. Cut the sonar. Cut the lamps. Nobody says a word until I say so.”

The cabin went dark. The screen went dark. The deep came up against the viewport, black and total, and in the silence Neve could hear her own heart and Kofi’s breathing and the faint, far-off complaint of the hull as the pressure found the crack and tested it. She could hear, too, the low hum of the batteries, a sound she usually forgot, and the soft creak of the cabin returning to its own temperature, degree by degree. She had never in her life heard the Cormorant sound so small. The dark outside the glass was not empty, she knew. It was full of water, and the water was full of weight. There was a moment, a bad one, when she felt all four kilometres above her like a hand on the top of her head.

And then, from somewhere below them, in the dark, something answered.

Not a ping. Not an echo. A low, long, humming note, like a finger drawn around the rim of a glass. It rose and fell and rose again, and it did not sound like a warning.

Lian had her hand up, flat, as if to say wait. Her headphones were on, and her eyes were closed, and in the dark her face was a pale shape turned toward a sound the rest of them could only hear as a rumble.

It sounded like a question.
