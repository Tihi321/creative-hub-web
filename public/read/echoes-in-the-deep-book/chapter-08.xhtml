The Bloom

The lamp work took longer than the note. Neve spelled the plan into the dark one flash at a time, each flash a word, read off a bent sail four hundred metres away by a signaller with cold hands. We will sing. When the rocks answer, hold your depth. When the way is clear, go up fast. Do not ping. Do not ping.

The lamp blinked back: UNDERSTOOD. HURRY.

“That’s the whole vocabulary of the sea,” Kofi said from the power bay. “Hurry and don’t.”

“How’s the voice?” Rivas asked.

“Ask me in ten.” Kofi’s head was inside the bay. “I’m bolting the transducer to the frame ring. She’s going to sing through her own bones. Either genius or a very expensive way to find out what a submersible sounds like when it cries.”

Neve crouched beside him with the torch. “Explain it like I’m not an engineer.”

“You know how a guitar body makes the string louder? String’s thin, moves a hair, you’d never hear it across a room. But it shakes the box, and the box is big, so the box shakes the air. We’ve got a string. It’s the transducer, size of a coffee cup. The box is the Cormorant. I’m going to hold a low tone through the frame ring, and the hull will swell it up like a bell.”

“And the water carries it.”

“For miles. It’s not a knock, Doctor. It’s the boat herself, singing.” He picked up his wrench. “There’s a catch. Steel doesn’t give you one note, it gives you a bundle. Some of the overtones are not pretty. If one lands wrong, we don’t calm the thing down, we annoy it in a key it’s never heard. That’s why I want Lian on the array. She hears the note go out and she hears what comes back, and if the note’s wrong, she says so.”

“She will,” Neve said.

“I know. I like saying it out loud where the commander can hear.” Kofi grinned, tape on his knuckle, grease in the creases of his hand. “Twenty minutes. Tell Achebe to warm her engines and keep her finger off the button.”

The Halcyon read the lamp with her own work lamp. ENGINES WARM. DEPTH HELD. WAITING ON YOUR SONG. — ACHEBE.

“She still wants a voice,” Rivas said, reading over Neve’s shoulder.

“She can have one at the surface,” Neve said, and flashed it. The Halcyon’s lamp went quiet, then: THEN SING WELL.

At ten minutes to the hour Kofi closed the panel and sat back on the deck.

“Done. Tone generator, transformer off the spare inverter, and the hull. It’s on the emergency cells and draws them gentle. She’ll sing. I don’t know yet if she’ll sing pretty.”

“Then we find out where it’s safe to find out,” Rivas said. “Lian.”

“On the array. I’ve got the low end back.” Her eyes were half closed. “The field’s still on the Halcyon’s bearing, still going up. Leading edge maybe eighty metres below the throat. It’s stopped answering the drum in time. It’s singing its own thing now.”

“What’s its own thing?”

“Bigger,” Lian said. “And faster.”

Rivas glanced at the air counter, and whatever number he saw he kept behind his teeth. “Mensah. Start her soft. Just enough so we hear she’s in tune. One minute. If the field so much as turns its head, we stop.”

“Soft it is,” Kofi said, and threw the switch.

The Cormorant sang.

Not loud. Neve had braced for something enormous, and instead the note came up through the deck into the soles of her feet like the lowest pedal of an organ played three rooms away. The hull whispered. The water took the whisper and stretched it out, and the passive array drew back a picture of it travelling east and up along the wall, long and clean and patient.

“That’s the note,” Lian said. “That’s the one. It’s the harmonic, the one from the trench’s own return. It matches. Doctor, it matches. It’s the same note the field sings to itself when it’s quiet.”

“Hold it,” Neve said. “Don’t push. Hold it exactly there.”

A minute. The field did not turn.

“It’s listening,” Lian said. “It’s stopped growing up. It’s coming down off the throat.”

“Toward us or toward her?”

“Toward us. No. Wait.” Lian’s hands were flat on the console. “It’s turning all the way round. It’s not coming at us, it’s coming to us. Commander, it’s like a cat when you call it.”

“Let it come,” Neve said. “That’s what we want. Keep the note exactly where it is.”

Half a minute, and then the field came over the western ridge in a slow pale tide, and Neve saw it whole for the first time. Not a wall, not a ribbon. A forest of crowns and stalks and long translucent blades, packed so close the shapes blurred into one soft brightness, and it moved with the note as grass moves in a wind that starts far away.

“It’s beautiful,” Lian said, almost apologetically.

“Eyes forward,” Rivas said, but quietly.

“It’s spreading,” Neve said, one palm open on the screen. “It’s not just coming toward us. It’s fanning out. Lian, where’s the Halcyon’s ascent line?”

“North-northeast of the throat, about a hundred and twenty metres.”

“And the field?”

Lian redrew the picture, and Neve watched the brightness spread out from the centre of the trench, and her mouth went dry.

“It’s going everywhere,” Lian said. “It’s not going to the song. It’s flooding.”

“Kofi,” Rivas said. “What’s happening to the note?”

“It’s the same note. The hull’s ringing the way she’s meant to.” He had come forward, hands on the back of Lian’s chair. “I don’t like the overtones. Look at the upper band. There’s a bright line I didn’t put there.”

“The hull’s adding notes,” Lian said. “The note we gave her is fine, but the steel’s singing its own. There’s a high one riding on top and another under it, and the field is answering all of them. It’s not one song any more. It’s a crowd.”

“Kill it,” Rivas said.

“Wait,” Neve said.

“Doctor—”

“Look at the throat.” She enlarged the picture. There it was, the narrow neck where the eastern and western walls almost touched, and the field was moving into it. Not blocking it, not yet, but gathering at the edges like frost on a window, creeping inward, meeting in the middle.

The note went on.

The frost became a bloom.

No sudden blow. It gathered the way frost gathers on a cold morning, cell touching cell, and Neve could not look away. The pale shapes met in the throat and locked together, and the opening in the middle shrank to a bright ring, and the ring shrank, and the ring was gone.

“That’s the throat,” Lian said, very small. “It’s shut. Commander, there’s no way through.”

The Cormorant’s keel touched something, softly, and the whole hull rang like a struck cup.

“Cut the note!” Rivas shouted. “Kofi, cut it now!”

Kofi was already moving. The whisper stopped. The silence after it was the loudest thing Neve had heard all day, and in the silence she heard the Cormorant tick and settle, and something outside the hull shift its weight against the viewport, gently, like a hand on the glass.

“It’s all around us,” Lian said. “It’s not hitting us, but it’s closed over the top of us. We’re inside it.”

“Signal Halcyon,” Neve said. “Tell them to hold.”

The lamp flashed three times. The Halcyon’s lamp flashed back before she had finished, one long stream, unreadable, then readable, then unreadable as the signaller on the sail began to skip.

WAY BLOCKED. WAY BLOCKED. WHAT IS THIS. WE ARE UNDER SOMETHING. ACHEBE SAYS—

The lamp stopped. Then it started again, flashing fast and wrong, the beam swinging off the sail and across the water and back.

“They’re panicking,” Lian said. “I can hear their prop turning up. Commander, they’re going to try it.”

“Emergency ascent,” Rivas said. He did not make a question of it.

“Full power, straight up, no waiting,” Kofi said. “They’ll hit the bloom doing every knot they’ve got, and it won’t matter, it’ll be a bird hitting a window. If they do that they’ll stove their own bow and we’ll be picking them up in a bucket.”

Rivas stood over the console with his fists on the edge of it, the cords standing in his wrists, and Neve watched him fight the thing he had fought all day: the urge to do something decisive and wrong.

“Signal them to hold,” he said. “Again.”

“They’re not looking at the lamp,” Neve said. “I wouldn’t look at the lamp. If I were down here in a dark trench with something growing over my head, I wouldn’t be reading a lamp on the other sub either. I’d be going home.”

“Then what do I do, order a woman not to run by ringing a bell at her?”

Neve looked at the screen. The bloom hung in the throat in a soft bright tangle, and the Cormorant sat under its roof like a beetle under a leaf, and the Halcyon was off the port bow with her prop turning up into a scream Neve could hear through the hull.

“The note did this,” she said. “We sang, and the field heard two subs at once, both of us loud, both of us warm, both of us frightened, and it did what it does when it’s frightened. It closed the door. It’s not sealing us in to kill us. It’s sealing the door because there’s a noise outside and it wants the noise to stop.”

“Fine theory,” Rivas said. “She’s going to hit it in three minutes.”

“Then we stop being a noise,” Neve said.

“We’re already quiet. Kofi cut the note.”

“The note isn’t the noise,” Lian said slowly, and then sat up straight, eyes wide and bright in the red light. “Oh. She’s right. The noise is us. Two hulls, two props, two generators, two dozen pumps and fans and heartbeats. We’re the loudest thing in the trench and we’re on both sides of it. The bloom isn’t answering the note any more. It’s answering the crowd.”

“Kofi,” Neve said. “How quiet can we make her?”

“I’ve already turned off everything that turns off. We’re at the floor. Nothing left but breathing.”

“Then the bloom isn’t going to open for quiet,” Neve said. She looked at the soft bright ceiling over the viewport. “It’s shut because it’s scared. You can’t argue a scared thing out of a door. You have to be the thing it trusts more than it’s frightened.”

“The note made it bloom,” Rivas said. “We’ve got one note.”

“It bloomed because we sang it through the whole hull, and the whole hull was screaming overtones,” Kofi said. He had gone very still, eyes moving over a broken circuit only he could see. “I told you. Steel doesn’t give you one note, it gives you a bundle. We wanted a voice and we gave it a shout.”

“Can you make it give one note?” Neve asked.

Kofi laughed, which was not the response the moment deserved, but he caught himself. “Not from one sub. I can tame the worst overtones, maybe. I can’t make steel sing like a flute.”

“But two subs can,” Lian said.

The cabin went quiet.

“Say that again,” Neve said.

“The Halcyon. She’s a bigger bell than we are. If she rings the low end, and we ring the Cormorant at the overtone, and the two of us go at the same time. Not the same note, two notes, one under the other. The thing between them is clean. That’s the cleanest note in the trench. That’s something a scared thing might believe.”

“Two subs, in tune, four hundred metres apart, on a clock,” Rivas said. “That’s not a plan. That’s a string quartet under water.”

“It’s the only thing that is a plan,” Neve said.

“And if it doesn’t work?”

“Then we’ve lost nothing we hadn’t already lost. We’re shut in. Their air is going down and so is ours. Silence bought us an hour. Fighting bought us a cracked window. This is what’s left.”

Rivas looked at her, and she had seen him look at the air counter and at the crack and at the log, but not quite this way before. It took her a moment to understand it, and when she did she had to look down at the console.

He was asking her. Not testing her. Asking.

“Kofi,” he said, without looking away from her. “How long?”

“Two bells. One in each boat. Ours is a matter of retuning what I just built. Theirs is a matter of telling a frightened captain four hundred metres away to bolt a transducer to her frame in ten minutes and hold a note on my count, blind, by lamp.” He paused. “If they have a transducer. They’re a survey boat. They might hold a steady tone on their array, if Achebe’s man can keep his finger still.”

“Ask her,” Neve said. “Short as you can make it. Lian, give me the field.”

“One chance,” Rivas said. “If they can’t hold the note, or they go up, or the field turns, we take our chances with the thrusters and go up into it anyway, and I want it written down that I made that call.”

“It’s written,” Neve said. “Give me the lamp.”

She spelled it out in the fastest flashes the signaller could read, and made it short, because a frightened crew does not want to hear a plan. WE HAVE A PLAN. NEED YOUR BELL. ONE NOTE LOW, HELD. WE SING HIGH, YOU SING LOW. TOGETHER IT IS CLEAN. WHEN IT OPENS, BOTH UP.

The answer came back after a while. WE ARE UNDER SOMETHING. WHAT IS IT.

Neve stared at the beam and the answer she would not give. ALIVE, she flashed. FRIGHTENED. HELP US BE KIND TO IT.

Another wait. The Cormorant ticked. The roof over the viewport shifted, and something touched the hull and breathed, and the sound went through the deck into her spine.

ACHEBE SAYS TEN MINUTES. SHE SAYS IF IT CLOSES ON US WE GO UP THROUGH IT.

“No, you don’t, you daft brave woman,” Kofi said softly. “You’ll bounce.”

DON’T GO UP THROUGH IT, Neve flashed. SING WITH US.

A pause, then: COUNT US YOUR HIGH. WE HOLD THE LOW.

“She’s giving us the top,” Lian said. “And she’s taking the bottom herself. The low note’s harder to hold steady. The low note’s the whole thing.”

“She’s been doing this longer than we have,” Neve said. “Count us off, Lian. And then give them the mark by lamp, three long flashes, because they can’t hear you.”

Kofi put his hand on the switch. “The transducer’s dead centre. Her note’s clean, near as I can make steel clean. The overtones are down, so long as I don’t push her.” He looked at Rivas. “I can’t promise the note’s going to be pretty. I can promise it’s going to be the truest thing this boat has ever said.”

“Say it,” Rivas said.

Lian counted them down aloud in the cabin, and her voice was quiet and even. “Five. Four. Three. The Halcyon’s on her lamp, she’s counting her own. Two. One.”

She flashed the mark: three long beams across the black water.

Kofi threw the switch.

The two subs sang.

The Halcyon’s low note came through the hull from the outside, huge and steady and closer to the earth than anything Neve had ever heard, and a breath later the Cormorant’s note came up underneath it and met it in the water between them, and the two did not fight. They braided. She had expected a muddle, a quarrel of frequencies, and instead the notes found each other and held, and the braid went out into the trench like a hand held out in the dark.

“Clean,” Lian said. “It’s the note from the trench’s own return, and the low one Achebe’s holding, and they’re in tune all the way. There’s nothing between them.”

The bloom over the viewport was still. It might have been painted on the glass.

Then the middle of it shifted.

“It’s looking,” Lian said.

“Hold,” Rivas said. “Everyone hold.”

“The edge under the Halcyon’s opening. Slow. It’s listening. It’s not pushing, it’s opening, it’s opening under her.”

Neve had not been aware she was gripping the console. She let go of it and set both hands flat on her knees instead.

“The throat,” she said. “Lian, the throat.”

Lian was silent for four seconds that lasted an hour.

“The throat’s still shut,” she said. “The roof over us is still closed. The Halcyon’s got a way through. She’s got a hole under her, Commander. But ours is still shut.” Her voice cracked. “It heard one of us and not the other.”

“It heard the low note,” Kofi said, and there was a frantic, laughing edge in his voice. “It heard Achebe’s note. Ours is the top of the chord, the one the water carries least, and we’re not where it’s looking. It’s looking at the Halcyon.”

“Then get it to look at us,” Rivas said.

“If I turn up to be heard I go ugly. If I go ugly it closes on the Halcyon again, and this time it won’t be a roof, it’ll be a fist.”

“The Halcyon could lend us her low note,” Lian said. Her eyes were shut, her hand flat on the console, her voice strange and certain. “It’s her note it trusts. If she holds the low and throws a little of it our way, a reflection off the west ridge, the field will come and look down here to see where her other note is coming from, and it’ll hear us underneath, both of us together, and know we’re one thing.”

“There’s no time to rig a reflection,” Kofi said. “There’s no time to rig anything.”

“There’s the ridge,” Neve said. “We’re close under it. If we stop our note and back her up the slope, her own voice will come off the wall, and the bloom will have to follow it down to find where the echo’s coming from. And where the echo is, we’ll be.”

“Back the boat up,” Kofi said. “Into a closed bloom. With the thrusters we just spent half a day saving.”

“Yes.”

“Doctor, if we touch the bloom with the thrusters, the vibration alone—”

“I know,” Neve said. “Lian. Tell the Halcyon to hold the low note, and on our lamp to double it. Then listen for us. We’re going quiet for one minute and we’re going up the slope.”

Lian nodded and was already flashing.

Rivas put his hands on the thruster controls.

“Neve,” he said. “One thing. Before I run this boat backwards into a wall made of something alive.” He looked at her. “You’re sure.”

“I’m not sure,” Neve said. “I’m nearly sure.”

“That’s the truest thing you’ve said all day,” Rivas said, and backed the boat up.

They went up the slope slow, and the Cormorant rang the whole way, and Neve kept her eyes on the screen and would not let herself say a word. The bloom came down after the echo, feeling its way along the western wall like a hand along a corridor, and the bright mass slid across the viewport and up over the glass and over the sail, and the note went out from the Halcyon and came back off the ridge, and the field followed it, and the field found where the echo was coming from, and it found the Cormorant under the ridge, singing.

“Now,” Lian said. “Doctor, now.”

Kofi threw the switch.

The Cormorant sang its high note into the field, and the field did not close. It opened. The bloom peeled back off the glass and off the sail, and the bright shapes drew away from the hull and turned in the water, and the roof over the viewport came apart in long pale banners and folded upward toward the throat.

“The throat,” Neve said. “Lian, the throat.”

“It’s opening. In the middle. It’s opening from the centre out, and both of us are underneath it and the way up is there. Commander, the way up is there.”

Rivas did not ask permission. He put both hands on the controls and turned the boat for the throat.

“Halcyon,” he said, and Neve was already flashing. GO. GO. GO. UP NOW, BOTH OF US, UP.

The beam came back through the dark, one long eye-watering burst: UP.

The Cormorant climbed. The throat went past them on both sides, and the bloom still parted like a curtain, and Neve looked up through the viewport and saw the two walls go by and then recede, and beneath them the field settled slowly back over the floor of the Marrow Deep. The last thing she saw before the water went dark was the bright ring of it closing gently over the place where both subs had been, soft as a hand over a cupped flame.

“Thirteen hours and fifty,” Rivas said. “Give me the ascent. Give me the climb.”

“Both subs climbing. Clearing the throat. Clearing. Commander, we’re clear.”

“Kofi,” Rivas said. “What have we got in the thrusters?”

Kofi was already at the panel, face grey and shining, and grinning.

“Everything she’s got and a bit more,” he said. “Go on. Take us home.”
