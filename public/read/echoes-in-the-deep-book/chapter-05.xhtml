Light in the Dark

The light came back by degrees.

Neve kept the headphones clamped over her ears, the tablet balanced on her knees, and watched the numbers climb out of the quiet one after another. Passive only. The array listening, the boat holding still, the lamp dark behind its tape shadow. She had promised herself she would not put a name to a thing before she had looked at it. So she looked.

“Commander,” she said at last. “It’s growing.”

Rivas turned from the air figures, from the sum that never came out any better no matter how many times he ran it. “Growing where?”

“Away from the Halcyon.” She swung the screen around. The soft grey bloom of the return spread like a stain through blotting paper, and the pale hole at the centre of it was the stranded sub. “Two hours it was thickening along her port side. Now it’s thinning. Pulling back from her the way a hand pulls back off a hot stove.”

“That’s good,” Kofi said. “Tell me that’s good.”

“It’s good for the Halcyon.”

“You’ve got a voice,” Rivas said. “What’s the rest of it?”

“It’s coming toward us. Slow. But the leading edge has crossed the debris line, and an hour ago it was nowhere near.”

The cabin went quiet. Beyond the viewport the black was the same black it had been at midnight and would be at noon, and still it had a direction now, like a slope you cannot see but can feel under your feet.

“How long do we have?” Rivas asked.

“Define have. It’s not a bomb. It’s a thing growing on a sea floor that has been growing there a very long time and is now growing a little less slowly than usual. If you want a number, it will be under our keel before the air is under a day.”

“Under our keel,” Rivas repeated. “On the hull.”

“That’s what growth does when there’s a hard thing in its way. It goes round it, or it goes over it, or it lifts it.”

Rivas set the pen down and folded his hands. Neve watched the moment when a number stopped being a number and became a decision.

“Then we move,” he said. “We lift off the floor, put a hundred metres between us and it, and hover.”

“On surging thrusters,” Kofi said. “I fixed the timing. Lift, and we use the thrusters hard, and the thrusters talk to the same bus as everything else, and the hull had a crack this morning that I taped and strapped and gave four hours of hard work. We’ve spent one of those already. I can hover for maybe twenty minutes, and then I want to come down and let the strap do its job.”

“And in twenty minutes the thing is still coming,” Rivas said.

“It’s slow,” Neve said. “It crossed that whole debris field while we sat here doing nothing, which tells me it was following something. Not only us. I want to know what.”

She spent the next hour finding out, drawing a picture of a thing she could not see from the noises it made, the way you would draw the shape of a room by walking round it in the dark.

The picture was not a wall. That was the first surprise. She had thought of the Lattice as one vast growth, a single structure rising off the floor. What the passive array gave her was a field of it: hundreds of small crowns, each the size of a dinner plate, spaced a few metres apart, every crown answering every other the way a hedgerow of crickets answers itself on a summer night. The song moved across the field in a slow travelling wave, and the wave had a direction, and the direction was toward the Cormorant.

She was still bent over the tablet when Lian came up from the galley with two cups of tea that would taste of metal and old coffee and set one by Neve’s elbow without a word.

“Lian,” Neve said. “Sit down. I want your ears.”

Lian sat. She had a way of listening with her whole face, eyes gone slightly away, lips a little apart, as though the sound were something she could lean toward.

“Play me the field,” Neve said, and Lian put on the headphones and called up the passive log, and they went through an hour of the trench’s small noises together, and under all of it the field, the field, the field.

“It isn’t just growing toward us,” Lian said at last. She pressed the pads together with her fingers, frowning. “It’s growing toward us now. It grew toward the Halcyon when the Halcyon was pinging. But the Halcyon went quiet at nine, and it kept coming, in the same line. It’s like a note that was struck, and now it’s ringing on by itself.”

“A note doesn’t ring on and on,” Neve said.

“No,” Lian said. “It doesn’t.”

“Kofi,” Neve said. “If you wanted to grow toward something, and all you had to go on was water and sound, what would you grow toward?”

Kofi answered without looking up from the thrusters’ manual. “Heat. Every battery on this boat is a little coal fire going out through steel. If I were a crystal trying to find the warmest thing in a cold trench, I’d sit up and sniff.”

Neve looked at the crowns nearest the boat, at the lovely ruin of them all pointing, in the only way a crystal can point, toward the Cormorant’s keel.

“The battery bay’s under the galley floor,” she said.

“It is,” Kofi said. “It’s been under my feet all day, and it’s the warmest thing for a hundred kilometres in any direction.”

The Halcyon’s lamp was still burning.

Neve found it with the outside camera on its lowest setting, a small orange coal in the field of the viewport, and she thought about Captain Achebe, who had been under that slab with eleven people and thirty-six hours of air and had still sent a swimmer out along a tether to make a tow line fast.

“She’s still got her sail lamp up,” Neve said. “Someone’s still on the sail. They read us, they shut the sonar off on our say-so, and now they’re up there in the cold wondering what’s going on.” She turned from the viewport. “Commander, I want to talk to her. Properly.”

Rivas turned the way a man turns when he has been rehearsing his part. “The lamp’s a yes. The phone’s the no. We agreed.”

“The phone is the no because the phone makes sound. The lamp makes light. I want more than one flash.”

“For what more?”

“For a conversation.” She set the tablet down where he could see it. “Kofi’s shutter puts a spot of light through that gap. Long spot, short spot. Blink it out slow, and a good signaller reads it just as well at four hundred metres as at four.”

Rivas looked at Kofi. “Can you do the shutters?”

“I can do anything with a roll of tape and a prayer. There’s a crate of spare lamp masks in the aft locker. I can cut a proper one.”

“Then talk,” Rivas said to Neve. “Tell her what?”

“Tell her the truth. That this is a rescue and we are on her side. That there is a living structure in this trench and it answers sound with sound, and that every sound is a meal to it. That we need to be quiet together, both boats, long enough that it loses interest.” She paused. “And that we are working on a way to move it away from both boats, and that I will not ask her people to do anything until I know what that way is.”

“You’re working on it,” Rivas said.

“I’m working on it,” Neve said. “I won’t promise her a plan I haven’t got. I’ll promise her the quiet, because the quiet I can give her, and I’ll tell her I’m thinking about the rest.”

“Which is not a plan,” Rivas said.

“No,” she said. “It’s a promise I can keep.”

She worked on it with Kofi over the next half hour, down in the cramped dark of the aft bay with the spare beacon crates, and the plan took shape the way a plan does, sideways and out of pieces that happen to be lying around.

“Here’s the trouble,” Kofi said. He had the spare beacon open on his knee, looking into its workings as though he had met them before. “It’s a pinger. Nice new pinger, emergency battery sealed in, tap on the casing so you can turn it on with a magnet from outside your suit. But it’s on all the time. Not a speaker you can play like an instrument. One note, and the note is help.”

“Can you make it a speaker?”

“I can make it a drum. If I cut the drive and put the trigger on that broken galley timer, I can make the thing beat. A pulse, then a gap, then another pulse. Whatever gap I set on the dial.”

“How loud?”

Kofi tapped the beacon with his fingernail. “Loud enough to put a thump in the water at a few hundred metres. Not a sonar ping. A knock. A tap on a door.” He looked up. “Doctor, what are we knocking for?”

“We are going to lure it. Put the beacon out on a line away from both boats, off toward the trench wall, and pulse it on a long, slow beat. If Kofi’s right, it’s drawn to whatever is warm and whatever is singing. It comes toward the beacon. We move the beacon. It follows. We walk it away from both subs like a dog on a lead, as far as the battery and the hour in this boat will allow.”

Rivas heard the whole of it without interrupting, which by now she had learned could mean two different things.

“You want to ring the bell again,” he said.

“I want to ring the bell, but not on the boat. That’s the whole of the point. The last time we made a noise, it was made on this hull, and the thing below answered this hull. This time we make the noise somewhere else, and the answer goes somewhere else too.”

“And if it doesn’t? If it likes a drum as much as a ping, and comes not for the drum but for us?”

“Then Kofi cuts the beacon, and we are no worse off, except one spare beacon poorer.”

“And if it takes the drum for a threat, and does to us what it did to the frame this morning?”

There it was. Not a man defending his rank, but a man with a cracked hull and eleven lives beyond the debris field and four more in his own boat, running out of arithmetic. He had been right about the frame when she had been sure of a worm bank and wrong about everything.

“Then you will have been right, and I will have been wrong, and I will write it in the log in my own name first and yours second, because that is what I promised.” She leaned forward. “But before you decide, listen to Lian. She knows its habits better than any instrument we own.”

Rivas looked at Lian.

She sat very still and spoke in her soft and careful way, as though every word had to be set down. “It does not strike this boat. That is not a theory, it is what the log says. Every time we go quiet it goes quiet. Every time we make more noise than it likes it comes closer. It is not hunting, Commander. It is answering. A thing that answers can be told things. A thing that answers can be led.” She paused. “It is like the first rehearsal of a choir. All the voices singing louder and louder because nobody has told them the tune. Somebody has to give them one clear note and hold it.”

Rivas looked at the air figures, and then at the tablet with the field of crowns on it, and then at the two of them.

“Kofi,” he said. “How long to build your drum?”

“Forty minutes if the galley timer’s in a good mood.”

“It is never in a good mood.”

“Then an hour,” Kofi said, and grinned, and the grin was the first light thing that had happened all day.

“Build it,” Rivas said. “Doctor, I want it written down before we test it: exactly what we’ll do, exactly what we’ll do if it goes wrong, and at what number on the air we give up. Not in my name and yours this time. In all four of ours. If this bell rings and the roof comes in, I’m not having it said that the scientist talked the pilot into it.” He stood up. “And the first thing we do is the lamp. Proper conversation, slow as you like. If Achebe’s people are going to be near that drum, they need to know what’s about to happen and be ready to go flat.”

“Sir,” Lian said. “Before we signal, may I have one more thing to say?”

“Lian, you’re the only one on this boat who’s never asked to say one more thing. Say it.”

“The first thing we tell her shouldn’t be the plan,” Lian said. “It should be sorry.”

They all looked at her.

“Her people have been in the dark under a rock since before we got here. We flash her lamp once, she answers, she goes quiet, and then she hears nothing from us for an hour and a half while we sit in our boat with our batteries. If I were Achebe I would watch that lamp and think, what do you want from us now.” She looked up. “The first thing we send her should be sorry for the silence. The second should be we are alive. Then the rest. She’ll read it better if she knows we’re people first.”

Rivas looked at Neve. Neve looked back at him.

“She’s right,” Neve said.

“She is,” Rivas said. “All four of you right at once, that’s a first.” He sat down again. “Kofi, cut the mask. Lian, you read the shutter, you’ve got the steadiest eye. Doctor, you call the letters. I’ll work the lamp.” He reached for the switch and paused. “And log the time.”

They began at 10:24, and it took an hour and ten minutes to say it.

Neve called the letters and Kofi worked the tape mask and Rivas threw the lamp, and the spotted beam went out across the trench and found the Halcyon’s sail and called to her in long and short, the way you would spell it out for someone in another room.

Sorry for silence. All four alive. Cormorant safe. Do you read.

There was a pause of three minutes, and then the little orange eye on the Halcyon’s sail came to life and answered, and Lian read it aloud from her tablet, letter by letter, her voice pitched small with concentration: “Reading you. Halcyon all eleven. Air thirty-three. Who is this.”

“Tell her,” Rivas said. “All of it. Straight.”

Neve told her. Thirty-five minutes of it, letter by letter, dot and dash: the living structure in the trench, the answering echoes, the silence that had calmed it, the growth now coming on across the floor. And then the rest, which was harder to spell than any of it, because it was the truth and the truth was thin: that Cormorant was working on a way to move the structure off its line, and had no way yet, and would not ask the Halcyon to move or speak or breathe until it did. That the one thing she could promise tonight was quiet, and the quiet she could promise.

At 11:31 the Halcyon answered, in a signaller’s steady hand: “Keep lamp on sail. My signaller is reading. But I want to hear a voice.”

“A voice,” Rivas said flatly. “Tell her no. Tell her the phone makes noise.”

Neve stood at the console with the stylus in her hand and did not write. She could feel the whole of the Cormorant holding its breath behind her, and out across the debris field the Halcyon holding the same breath, eleven people and a bent sail and thirty-three hours of air. She had been sure of her science all day. What she was not sure of was whether Achebe, tired and right to be wary, could be brought over by light alone.

She wrote it out.

“Cormorant has no safe voice. Phone is sound. Sound feeds the structure. Lamp only. I know it is not enough. Trust the light, Captain. I would not ask if I had another way.”

The Halcyon’s lamp went dark for four long minutes, and it came back with two words: “Reading you.”

And then, at 11:41, before anyone aboard the Cormorant spoke, the passive array gave them a sound it had not made in three hours. It came in low and slow, and the crowns across the floor answered all at once, and the answer went out like a ripple across the field. Neve heard it in her headphones and felt it through the cold metal at her elbow. Not the harsh bright thump of a ping coming back at four times its strength. Soft. A long, gentle hum with a shape to it that might almost have been a question, and it was very close, closer than it had any right to be.

“Doctor,” Lian said, her voice gone thin and careful. “The crowns under our keel. They’ve started to grow. All at once.”

Neve turned to the viewport, and there was nothing to see; there never was; the black was seamless and complete. But the return on the tablet was ugly. The front of the field had come off the floor a hundred metres away and was moving, slowly and steadily and without hurry, in one direction, and the direction was here. The crowns at the leading edge had lifted, the way a bed of flowers lifts when the wind gets under it, and behind them the rest of the field leaned in to follow.

“It’s found the battery bay,” she said. “Or it’s found us. It’s all the same to the crystal.”

Rivas looked at the clock. “Air’s at eighteen.” He said it the way he always said it, flat and exact. “Lian, how long till it reaches the keel?”

Lian put the headphones on and listened with her eyes closed and one hand flat on the console, and she listened for a long minute, and when she took them off her mouth was set in a way Neve had not seen before.

“Commander, if it comes on at this rate it will reach the keel after dark. Not tonight. The dark after the next one.” She paused. “But I would rather we didn’t find out how it knocks on a boat. It has never knocked on a boat. It has only ever knocked on the water.”

Rivas sat back. He looked at the log, and at the number, and at the three of them.

“Kofi,” he said. “The drum.”

“Nearly there, Commander.”

“How nearly?”

Kofi held up the beacon, and behind it, in the crook of his arm, the broken galley timer with its dial and its little tick.

“I can make it go,” he said. “The question is whether we want it going.”

Neve looked at him, and at the beacon, and out into the black beyond the viewport where a thing she had looked at, and looked at, and looked at, was coming toward a warm battery and a cracked frame and four people and eleven more beyond.

“We want it going,” she said. “And we want it going away from both boats.”
