Rockfall

The silence lasted forty-one minutes. Neve counted them on the console clock, the one red stare left in the cabin, a light that made everything around it darker than it had been. Nobody spoke. Rivas kept one hand on the throttles. Kofi sat very still at the rear with his palm pressed to the seam above the viewport, reading the hull the way a doctor reads a chest. Lian wore her headphones, eyes closed, lips apart, listening.

The humming had stopped after three notes. The dark went back to being dark. Slowly, the Cormorant stopped complaining.

“Say something,” Rivas said at last, very quietly. “Whisper it. But say something, because I’m starting to hear my own pulse and I don’t like the company.”

“Crack’s stable,” Kofi whispered. “Surface crack in the resin, not the glass. Hasn’t spread in forty minutes. Good sign. That’s the hull telling us she’s still got opinions, but she’s not panicking.”

“The music?” Rivas asked. “Lian. The humming. What was it?”

Lian took her headphones off and set them around her neck. “Three notes,” she said. “The same three the Halcyon’s ping came back with, only lower and slower. Like the echo decided to answer in its own voice.” She paused. “It’s still there. Quiet now, but still there, and when we cut the sonar it stopped moving toward us.”

“So the doctor was right,” Rivas said.

“The doctor was right about the first thing,” Neve said. “The second thing is that we can’t do a rescue in total silence. We need the sonar to see the rockfall, and we need the lamps to hook a line. We have to know how much noise we can afford before it comes back.”

Rivas looked at her in the red light of the clock. “Then I want a number, Doctor. Something I can write in the log. Not a feeling.”

“Give me twenty minutes and the passive array,” Neve said. “And a look at that debris field. If it’s a rockfall, it fell from somewhere. I want to know what’s still up there, waiting to come down.”

They brought the lamps up to a quarter power, one at a time, and watched the sonar screen for a full minute after each one, like people easing into cold water. Nothing answered. The soft return had gone quiet and flat, part of the floor again.

“All right,” Rivas said. “Lian, passive only. I want a count of every moving thing between here and that slab. Kofi, the tow line. Doctor, you’re with me on the debris.”

“I’m always with you on the debris,” Neve said.

The Halcyon came back out of the dark, closer than before. They had crept forward a hundred metres during the silence, and from here the rockfall filled the viewport. The slab was the colour of old bone, layered and cracked, and it lay across the Halcyon’s forward hull at an angle, its broken end propped on a ridge of rubble. Around it the trench floor was a scrapyard of fallen stone: boulders the size of cars, gravel fans, a scatter of pale rubble that looked, from the wrong angle, like rubble and not like the edge of something.

“Achebe, this is Cormorant,” Rivas said. “We’re going to be working around you. Keep your people off the hull and your thrusters cold. And Captain, I need you to stop pinging.”

A pause. “Say again?”

“Cut your sonar. All of it. We’ve had a response we don’t like. Passive listening is fine. Sending out a ping is not.”

The speaker clicked. When Achebe came back her voice had a careful flatness, the voice of a captain deciding how much to argue in front of her crew. “Commander, we’ve been pinging every ninety seconds for eleven hours. It’s the only way anyone finds us.”

“We’ve found you,” Rivas said. “And something else has found us. Cut the pings.”

Another pause, longer. “Ninety seconds from now,” Achebe said. “If it gets strange out there, I want to hear about it.”

“It’s already strange,” Rivas said.

Kofi had opened the equipment locker and was pulling out cable. The tow line was stored in a coil as wide as his arms could make, a braided thing of steel and synthetic fibre, and he laid it along the cabin floor in long S-curves and walked its length with a torch, pinching the braid between his fingers at intervals like a man checking a rope for frayed strands.

“Good line,” he said. “Rated for twice what we’ll ask of it. The problem isn’t the line. The problem is where we clip it on. Halcyon’s tow points are under the slab, and I’m not sending anything down there to crawl around in the dark with a rock the size of a house over its head.”

“Where can we get a line from Cormorant to Halcyon?” Rivas asked.

“Her forward hardpoint, if we can get to it.” Kofi looked up. “Her bow’s clear. The slab’s got her amidships. We come in bow to bow, we pass a line across, we make it fast, and we don’t touch the rock.”

“Fifty metres of open water between the hardpoints,” Lian said. “Maybe less if the floor’s soft and we can settle.”

“Home run,” Kofi said. “Except the floor is soft, and settling is the one thing I don’t want to do around a slope that’s still deciding whether to fall over.”

Neve had stopped listening to them. She had her face close to the viewport, the glass cold against her cheek, and she was reading the debris field the way she read a tide pool. The slab was the obvious thing: layered, ancient, its broken face raw and pale. It had come off the eastern wall, high up, where the wall was cut back in a long shelf and a fresh scar of white rock showed through the sediment. But the fall had not come down clean. She could see it in the fans of gravel and the way the boulders sat, each in its own shallow crater, and in the fine pale dust that lay over everything like flour.

“Commander,” she said. “The slope’s moving.”

“Moving how?”

“Not fast. But the dust is fresh. There’s a film of it over the Halcyon’s hull and it’s only on the upper surfaces, which means it came down recently and it came down gently, in a slow rain, the way sediment settles out of still water. If the slope were stable, that film would have either never fallen or already washed off.” She pointed. “And look at the rubble ridge under the slab’s broken end. The stones in it are sorted. Big at the bottom, small at the top. That’s not how a rockfall lands. That’s how a rockfall lands and then keeps shifting, the big pieces sinking and the little ones riding up.”

Rivas leaned over to look. “In English, Doctor.”

“The slab is propped on a pile that’s still settling,” Neve said. “It could come down the rest of the way on its own, at any time. Or it could come down the moment we put a load on it. Or the whole eastern shelf could let go and post us all some more rock.”

“Hallelujah,” Kofi said. “What do we do about it?”

“We move fast and we move gentle,” Neve said. “We don’t touch the ridge. We don’t send the thrusters anywhere near the floor under the slab. We make the line fast to Halcyon’s bow and we pull at an angle that lifts her forward end, so what’s under her midships is a slide, not a drag.”

Rivas was quiet. Then: “The angle’s going to be shallow. We can’t lift much.”

“Then we don’t lift much. We lift enough that she can use her own thrusters to skate backward off the ridge.” Neve looked at the slab again, and at the bright fresh scar high on the wall, and the numbers in her head turned over once, the way they always did before they stopped being numbers and became a decision. “We have until that ridge finishes sorting itself. Then we don’t have at all.”

Kofi had the line rigged in twenty minutes. He did it the way he did everything, talking the whole time, a running commentary that kept the cabin from going quiet enough to hear the hull.

“So here’s the thing about braided tow cable,” he said, unreeling the last of it with a flick of his wrist. “Everyone thinks steel is the strong part. Steel’s not the strong part. The strong part is the weave. You pull on a braid and every strand grabs every other strand, and the whole thing gets tighter, it gets stubborn, and a stubborn rope is what you want when you’re dragging a submarine off a rock.” He clipped the seaward end to Cormorant’s bow hardpoint, checked the shackle, checked it again, and then checked it a third time in a way that was not checking. “There. My masterpiece. If anyone asks who made it, tell them it was me, and also tell them I was very brave.”

“You were on the floor the whole time,” Lian said.

“Emotionally brave,” Kofi said.

Rivas brought the Cormorant in slowly, the way you would approach a sleeping animal. The floor came up under the lamps, pale and soft, and the Halcyon’s bow grew in the viewport until Neve could see the scratches in her paint and the line of riveted steel where her hardpoint sat. Achebe’s crew had put a work lamp on the hardpoint and taped a strip of orange cloth to it, which fluttered in the current, a small bright flag in all that dark.

“Cormorant, Halcyon,” Achebe said. “I can see your line. Hold position. I’m going to send a swimmer out.”

“Negative,” Rivas said. “We’re not putting a person outside in this. We’ll shoot a messenger line across and you can winch it in from inside.”

“Commander, my seabay’s flooded and I can’t open it. The hardpoint’s outside, and I don’t have a remote winch. If you want the line made fast, someone has to put their hands on it.” A pause. “I’ve asked. I have three volunteers and a fourth who didn’t volunteer but is already in his suit.”

Rivas looked at Neve. She looked back at him, and something passed between them that had nothing to do with the sea: the old calculation, the one where you weigh a small, certain risk now against a large, uncertain one later.

“One swimmer,” Rivas said. “Tethered. And the moment that ridge so much as sighs, he’s back inside.”

“Understood,” Achebe said.

Neve watched the Halcyon’s forward hatch open. A figure came out in a hard suit with a lamp on his helmet, trailing a tether, and moved along the hull with a swimmer’s deliberate economy, one hand on the steel at all times. He was small in the lamps and the water around him was very big. When he reached the bow, he took the messenger line from Cormorant’s launcher, clipped it to the hardpoint, and gave them a thumbs-up that had a kind of jauntiness to it, as if he had done nothing more than hang a picture.

“Line’s fair,” Kofi said, and reached for the winch control, and that was when the floor moved.

Small, at first. The sediment lifted, a wide pale breath, and the boulders in the ridge shifted against each other with a sound like a door opening in a distant room. Then the slab grated. A long, low grinding came through the hull and into Neve’s feet, and she felt it in her teeth and her knees and the thin bones of her wrists, and then it got louder, and the ridge let go. Stones came down in a slow avalanche, not falling so much as sliding, a brown river of rubble that took the light and ate it, and the slab dipped.

“Swimmer’s clear!” Lian shouted. “He’s at the hatch!”

And then the Cormorant spoke. Not a groan this time. A crack, sharp and bright, a sound like a gunshot in a small room, and the hull rang with it.

“New fissure!” Kofi was already moving, already reaching for the resin tape, his voice going up the way a man’s voice goes when he is doing arithmetic in his head and does not like the answer. “Starboard midships, above the ballast tank. Commander, that’s not the seal, that’s the frame. It’s a weld line and it’s just opened, I’ve got a pressure rise on the sensor and I need us to move.”

“Is the line made fast?” Rivas asked.

“Made fast,” Kofi said. “We can haul, but not like this, I need to patch first, I need ninety seconds—”

“You’ve got forty,” Rivas said. “Grab the tape.”

Neve had been holding the viewport rail and now she let go. The dust was still settling outside, a slow brown curtain, and through it the slab was visible and lower. It had come down a hand’s breadth, and it was still moving, the angle of it narrowing by the second. “The slab’s coming down,” she said. “If it lands, it takes Halcyon’s bow with it. We have to pull now.”

“We pull now and we tear my line through a broken weld,” Kofi said. “Which is worse, and if you’d like to argue about it, the answer’s still worse.”

“Then patch and pull,” Rivas said. “Doctor, how long to the slab?”

Neve did the sum with her eye, and then with the sum, and then decided she would not say it aloud because the number was not the number they could act on. Instead she pressed her fingertips to the glass, as if she could hold the slab up by the strength of her hand, and watched the dust. “Less than a minute,” she said. “Sal. It’s going.”

“Kofi, now.”

“Now,” Kofi agreed, and tore the backing off a strip of tape and pressed it across the fissure with both palms, and then slapped a second strip crossways over the first, and then a third, and then he put his ear to the hull and held up one finger. “It’s held. It’s held. Go.”

Rivas put his hand on the winch control and drew her in hand over hand, and the line came out of the water taut and dripping, and the Cormorant leaned against it with all her weight. The Halcyon shuddered. For a moment nothing happened, nothing at all, just the line humming and the slab grinding and the pale dust settling out of the water. Then the slab dropped, hard, and the sound came through the hull like a bell being struck, and the Halcyon came backward.

She came backward all at once, sliding down the ridge and off it, her bow digging a long white scar in the sediment, her hull slewing, and then she was clear, free of the rock, and Captain Achebe was on the speaker saying, “We’re clear, we’re clear, we’re clear—” and Rivas was laughing, a short, disbelieving laugh, and Kofi was saying, “That line, that beautiful line, I made that line—”

Then the Cormorant’s thrusters stuttered.

A hiccup, a catch in the steady push of the motors, and the sub yawed to port before Rivas caught it. The warning light on the console went from green to amber and did not go back.

“Thrusters are surging,” Rivas said, hands moving fast and precise. “Both sides. I’ve got intermittent draw. Kofi, what’s eating my power?”

“The patch is holding but the frame’s flexing,” Kofi said. “And the tow line’s still taut, she’s pulling against us, she’s loading the hull every time the motors catch. Commander, we’re not going to hold her like this. We need to slack off.”

“If I slack off now, the slab’s side of the floor is still falling,” Rivas said. “I’m not putting her back under it.”

“Then back us out. Take her astern slow, give the line a long curve, get us clear of the slope before the stutter turns into a stop.”

Rivas didn’t answer. He was looking at the console, at the amber light, at the numbers ticking over on the oxygen panel, and Neve could see him counting. She had watched him count a hundred times on this descent, and now she watched him do the other sum, the one he had been dreading.

“Halcyon’s air,” he said. Call it thirty-five of her thirty-six. Ours—OCR.” He tapped the panel once with the flat of a nail, as if the number might flinch and give him back a better one. “Twenty-two hours. That’s the Cormorant. Twenty-two, and I want it in the log where everyone can see it, because it changes every hour we sit here.”

“Twenty-two hours,” Kofi said.

“And if those thrusters stop, we’ve got less. Because then we’re not a rescue sub, we’re a second wreck.”

The cabin was quiet. Outside, the dust was still settling, and the Halcyon lay free of the slab, her bow in a long white furrow of sediment, her work lamp burning a small brave circle in the dark.

“Back us out,” Rivas said. “Slow. Kofi, I want that stutter fixed before we do anything else, and I want it fixed with what’s on this boat.”

“Yes, Commander,” Kofi said. “But I need to say the thing I always say, which is that I’m very good at this and it’s still going to take an hour.”

“Take forty minutes,” Rivas said. “We’re going to need the other twenty.”

He eased the throttles back and the Cormorant began to drift astern. The tow line lifted off the floor and made a long slow curve in the water, and the Halcyon fell away from them, smaller and smaller, until she was a grey shape and then a patch of grey and then a work lamp. The thruster stuttered again, once, like a heart missing its beat.

Neve stayed at the viewport until the work lamp was the only bright thing left, and then she went and stood behind Lian’s shoulder, where she could see the trace of the water going by and not the dust.

“The echo,” she said quietly, “the way it answered before. Don’t tell me what you hope. Tell me what the array says.”

Lian had her headphones on, both hands pressed to the cups, and her voice was far away and a little frightened, the way a person sounds when they are trying to say two things at once. “It’s back. It’s closer. And it’s got more to it than three notes now.”

“More how?”

“It’s patterned,” Lian said. “It’s not just answering. It’s—” She stopped. “It’s like a hand tapping a table. Like someone tapping out a song they want you to hear.” She turned her head, and in the red light her eyes were wide and open. “Doctor. It’s been waiting for us to stop making noise so it could start.”
