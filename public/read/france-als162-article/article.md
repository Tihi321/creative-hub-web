# France's Atomic Clock on Air: The ALS162 Signal That Still Runs the Nation's Time

*A long-wave transmitter at Allouis keeps more than 300,000 clocks in step with atomic time, from station platforms to traffic lights. Its life has been extended to 2034, but no one has yet said what replaces it.*

By Culo, Technology, 21 October 2026

At eight o'clock on Tuesday 1 September 2026, the long-wave transmitter at Allouis in central France stopped sending the time. It came back at noon, and the same thing happened on 8, 15, 22 and 29 September, five maintenance pauses of four hours each, logged by the Paris Observatory's time laboratory in its monthly bulletin [7].

Those Tuesday silences reach further than most people would guess. The signal, called ALS162, matters to more than 300,000 devices in France and abroad: public clocks, information panels, traffic lights, public lighting and parking meters [2]. Its users include the national railway SNCF, the electricity distributor Enedis, airports, hospitals and town halls [2].

This year brought the signal two pieces of news. The specialist site Telesatellite reported in April that the transmitter had been turned down to 675 kW, with no critical fault logged between December 2025 and March 2026 [8]. In March, France Horlogerie, the trade body of the French watch and clock industry, said the public authorities had extended the signal's life to the end of 2034 at its request [9].

That gives the mast eight more years. It also sets the question this article tries to answer: what keeps those clocks right if the long wave goes quiet for good?

## A radio station that lost its voice

Allouis used to talk. France Inter, the public radio network, broadcast on 162 kHz from the site until 31 December 2016 [3]. It had announced the switch-off that year, expecting to save about €6 million a year [3].

The voice went, and the ticking stayed. The time signal was to carry on because so many devices relied on it [3]. In December 2016 the Government gave the frequency regulator ANFR the job of keeping it on air from 1 January 2017, working with the transmitter's owner TDF [15]. Since then Allouis has carried nothing but the time signal [1], under the name ALS162, short for Allouis on 162 kHz [2].

The change shows up in the engineers' diary. Before 2017 the weekly maintenance slot ran from 01:03 to 05:00 on Tuesdays, when few radio listeners were awake [2]. Today it runs from 08:00 to 12:00 [2]. With no audience left to protect, the work moved into daylight.

## Where the time comes from

The precision does not come from the mast. Caesium atomic clocks at the transmitter generate the signal, and they are checked against UTC(OP), the official French time scale, through a system called SYREF and through GPS common-view measurements, in which two sites compare their clocks against the same satellite at the same moment [2]. The legal time itself is produced by the observatories of Paris and Besançon [9].

A station clock listening to Allouis needs no satellite. The clocks behind the signal do.

The results are tight. Over 24 hours the carrier frequency holds to a relative uncertainty of 2 × 10⁻¹², and over 30 days to 1 × 10⁻¹³ [2]. The time markers are meant to be accurate to better than a millisecond [2].

Several hands keep it that way. TDF runs the transmitter on behalf of ANFR and LNE-SYRTE, the time laboratory at the Paris Observatory [2]. LNE-SYRTE and a second laboratory, LNE-LTFB, provide the signal, and the two labs watch it together with France Horlogerie [2]. Their measurements go online in real time, and a monthly bulletin reports whether the accuracy targets were met [2]. The September pauses come from one of those bulletins [7].

## A quieter mast every decade

The power figures tell the story of a service kept alive on a budget. Allouis was cut to 1,500 kW in 2011, to 1,100 kW in 2017 and to 800 kW in February 2020, each time to save money [1]. At that power the signal reached about 3,500 kilometres [2]. A first test at the new, lower level took place in 2024 [1].

When the news site Next reported the plan that October, it said ANFR wanted to lower the power to save energy while keeping the service available to about 200,000 devices [12]. That is a third fewer than the count of users quoted above.

The cut has now happened. Telesatellite's report of no critical fault over the winter months [8] is the best evidence so far that the quieter signal still does its job.

## Why a weak old signal still matters

Satellites give better time on paper. Dedicated GPS timing receivers reach an accuracy better than a microsecond, while terrestrial time stations manage a few tens of milliseconds [6]. So why keep a long-wave mast on air?

The answer France Horlogerie gave in March was security. Its statement called ALS162 hard to jam and independent of foreign technology, and so an asset of national sovereignty [9]. It pointed to recent GPS jamming and failure incidents as proof that a country needs more than one way to broadcast the time [9]. A press-agency report the same week framed the signal as a matter of sovereignty and cybersecurity, with GPS interference showing the risk of leaning on other countries' systems [10].

The state's own guidance makes a similar point. A 2024 guide on jamming from ANFR and DGITM, the state's transport directorate, names the Allouis signal, alongside NTP, as a time source that measurement systems can use instead of satellite navigation [14].

Jamming means drowning a signal in noise until receivers cannot hear it. Spoofing goes further: a fake signal makes clocks set themselves to the wrong time without raising an alarm. Both are the risks behind France Horlogerie's argument for keeping a second, home-grown source on air [9].

## What could take its place

Next listed the ways legal time can travel: satellite systems such as the American GPS and the European Galileo, the internet through the Network Time Protocol, NTP, possibly 5G base stations in future, and terrestrial radio like Allouis [12].

Some of that already exists. LNE-SYRTE serves UTC(OP) and French legal time to the public around the clock through NTP servers and the heurelegalefrancaise.fr website [13]. It also hosts a station of EGNOS, a European navigation system used mostly by civil aviation, whose data tie the EGNOS network time to UTC through the French time scale [13].

Each option asks something of the device at the end of the line. A traffic light or a parking meter on long wave needs only an antenna and a receiver. On NTP it needs a network connection. On satellite time it needs a view of the sky and an owner willing to live with the jamming incidents France Horlogerie cited [9].

## Eight years to choose

The extension [9] buys time for the railway, the grid, the airports and the hospitals that set their clocks by Allouis. What happens on the first day of 2035 is the open question.

That is the decision now facing the French state and the operators. The choice is between renewing a transmitter that has been turned down again and again since 2011, or asking hundreds of thousands of station clocks, traffic lights and parking meters to find their time somewhere else: from the sky, which can be jammed, or from a network, which has to reach every pole and platform.

Until then the pattern holds. Every Tuesday at eight the mast falls silent, and every Tuesday at noon France's time comes back on air [2]. The September bulletin counted five of those pauses [7]. For now, each one ends.
