Rig Fire

The county line road ran north out of the gas station lights, and then a gravel section road cut east, and the gravel was the wrong choice inside four miles, but it was also the only road that ran the way they needed to go.

Tobin had the schedule open on his knees and the map light taped to the dash, and he read the next run off it twice, because the first read was wrong. He had copied it Tuesday night at the strip office with the door locked, standing at the copier with his back to the window, and the numbers had come off the glass at an angle, so he read them to her carefully, the way you read torque specs.

"Twenty-three hundred launch," he said. "Target's the same pair. Co-op, forty-nine, ingress. Rack four out of the strip, southbound run up the inflow. That gives us about an hour and ten to get the rig in front of the pass."

"Which side?"

"West of the track. She seeds from the east and runs the pass into the base. If you're west of her line, you're behind the seed."

"Behind the seed is upstream," Wren said. "The silver goes in and the inflow takes it north. If I'm upstream of the flare line, I'm measuring what's coming to it, not what she put in it. I want the notch. The notch is where the inflow turns vertical." She looked at the sky through the windshield. "Where does she turn in?"

Tobin put his finger on the map and ran it north along the section grid to the second east-west road above the lease road, and then he took his finger off and put it back a half inch west.

"Here," he said. "It's a bend in the county road where it crosses a draw. Fence line on the west side, a stand of fence posts, a culvert. Nothing else. She doesn't seed there. She seeds off the strip. But the notch sits over that draw for about twenty minutes in the four-fifty window, because the draw funnels the surface flow."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I'm the one who drove the pump truck," Tobin said. "Four nights. I sat in the pump truck at that draw with the tablet and watched the notch come over me, and I didn't know what I was looking at until you people started talking about inflow. I thought it was wind." He looked out the window. "It's not wind."

The gravel ran straight and pale between two wheat fields cut to stubble, and the headlights picked up the stubble in ranks, and then the road bent and the draw opened on the left, a shallow thing, a dry creek bed with cottonwoods along it in a line like a fence that had decided to grow. The trees leaned. Wren rolled the window down and the wind came in warm and steady and full of the smell of wet dirt on a dry night, which is a smell that means rain is falling somewhere within fifteen miles and the ground below it is hot enough to sweat.

She stopped the truck on the road above the culvert and killed the lights and got out.

The storm stood up north of them and a little east, and it was not a storm anymore. It was a wall.

It was low and long and flat-bottomed, and it went from the western horizon to the eastern horizon without a break, and the whole south face of it was a smooth shelf with a skirt of ragged cloud hanging off the underside like fringe on a rug, and behind the shelf the cloud went up into a grey that turned green at the top and then black above the green. No lightning. The thing was silent and turning and lit only by the moon behind it, and the whole sky over the draw was a ceiling of moving cloud going one way, south to north, one direction, no argument.

"Wall cloud," Tobin said. He had come up beside her. "It's got the whole base rotating. That's forty miles wide."

"Forty miles of inflow," she said. "Which means the notch is big enough to stand in." She looked at her watch. Ten past midnight. "Twenty-three hundred launch and a pass after. We've got fifty minutes."

The first rain curtain came out of the west end of the wall and swallowed the highway.

It crossed the stubble in a line you could see, a dark grey front with a lighter grey under it where the drops were hitting ground, and it crossed the wheat field and hit the draw and then it hit them, all at once, no arrival, just weather. The rain was warm. It came down in sheets wider than the truck and flattened the stubble and turned the gravel to a slick and got inside Wren's collar and down her back in the first ten seconds. Behind the first curtain there was wind.

"Now," she said. "It's now or it's four-thirty."

They worked in the rain with the headlights on high, and the rain stood in the beams like a wall of moving sand. The fence line ran along the west side of the road on a low rise above the draw, and the rise was the highest ground within a hundred yards, so it was the right place, and it was the worst place, because the rise was on the inflow side, and the inflow side was where the rain came first and hardest and stayed.

Tobin had the rig out of the bed before she asked for it. He had the posts under one arm and the tripod under the other, and he turned to her in the light and said the first true thing he had said all night, the one the rain cut the manners off of.

"You built this for clear air," he said. "The mast. It's a clear-air mast."

"I built it for cells."

"You built it for cells in May," Tobin said. "This is a June midnight and it's wet and she's already running hot. The mast's going to arc off the fence."

"Then we ground the fence," Wren said.

She got the copper strap and they laid it along the wire to the culvert and drove a stake with the flat of the maul, and the strap went in and the water ran over it, and the two of them were soaked through in two minutes and stayed that way. She got the tripod up, three legs in mud, and the housing mounted on top, and the mast leaned into the wind and she guyed it out to two of the fence posts with cord and set the tether anchor. The rig had gone dark on the truck bed because the truck bed was wet. She dried the terminals with the seat cover Tobin threw her from the cab and got the display up, and the display came up at three bars and the bars held.

"Reading," Tobin said.

"Reading." She crouched at the housing with the laptop on her knee wrapped in the ground mat, and the screen glowed up at her through the rain. Temp twenty-two. Dew twenty-one and climbing. The surface flow up the draw was twenty-eight gusting thirty-four out of the south, and above that the low-level jet was forty on the beam, and the shear was there, the whole spin, the draw feeding the notch the way Tobin said it would, and the notch was over her.

And the silver. She ran the filter on the mast and the strip came live and the number was high. Higher than the field. Higher than the tower. Silver iodide in the rain itself, in the droplets hitting her hands, in the water running off the fence. Vance had made a pass, and the pass was in the cloud, and the cloud was raining the seed back down on her.

She said Odell's name into the weather and the weather took it, and then she remembered he wasn't in the truck.

He was eleven miles back at the gas station. He had said he was going to check the ignition rotor, and he had said it in a voice that meant he was going back to the truck for something and would not say what. They had left him at the pumps with the map out and Tobin on the seat and the schedule in her notebook, and she had not argued, because he had been right about the tower and right about the whitewash and right about the oiler, and she had decided somewhere on the county line road that if Odell Brandt needed to go back to the truck for something, he would come out the other side of it with the thing he needed.

She had fifty minutes and the pass and the number. She had the notch and the strip and the theory that had started on the co-op roof and had finally, on the ridge, in a stairwell, been handed a reason bigger than proof. She had everything but a way to fire it in the rain.

"Charge builds in the anvil," she said, working it out loud because out loud was how she seated it. "The trigger is a stepped leader that doesn't complete. I fly a conductive path up the inflow before it can disconnect, and it pulls the discharge early, into the ground, along the path, into the tether." She looked at the mast. "The tether is the path. I fly the rocket and the rocket pays out the tether and the tether anchors at the stake, and the stake's in the ground, and the leader takes the path."

"Early is good," Tobin said. "Why is early good?"

"Because the first strike of an updraft's life is the strike that drains the column's excess charge. Trigger it early and you pull the charge down before the column gets tall enough to build its own field, and the base loses its electrical anchor and it can't organize the same way. The updraft relaxes. The base widens. It doesn't lose forty inches of rotation, but it loses its structure, and structure is what makes it a tornado instead of a rainstorm." She got the rocket from the case and laid it along the fence and started the launch tube in the mud with the base plate.

"You've done this," Tobin said.

"I've done it twice. On a dry cell. On my roof." She did not say: and once in the wheat with Odell watching, and the second time the rig blew a fuse and the rocket never launched and the storm went on being a storm. She got the tube up. "Third time's the one."

"Third time's the one," Tobin said, and he did not sound like he believed it, and he was already at the mast foot with the wrench, tightening the guy anchors against the wind, and that was the part of him she had needed, the part that worked while the rest of him decided.

She checked the tether. Eight hundred feet of copper-laced line wound on a spool at the stake, and she fitted the spool to the housing, and then she connected the launch leads from the tube to the housing's trigger circuit, and the display showed the circuit live at two hundred joules and the capacitor stack charged to green.

"Charged," she said. "Fire in two."

The rain came harder. It crossed the draw in a second curtain and the wind gusted to fifty on her meter and the mast leaned and the two guyed posts held and the third guy line sang like a wire. A fence wire out in the dark west of them rang back, and there was a light in the wheat field, low and yellow, and another.

"Car," Tobin said. "That's a car in the field. That's not a car."

It was a flare. It came up off the west end of the draw and hung under the shelf for three full seconds, and then another flare and another, four in a row north and east, and the whole underside of the wall cloud went from black to a soft orange and showed its shape: the whole shelf turning, the whole base stacked, the wall cloud low and knife-hung and pointed west-northwest at the county line.

"Twenty-three hundred," Tobin said. "She's early."

"Which means the notch is over me now."

Wren fired.

The rocket went up off the tube into the rain and the tether paid out off the spool behind it, and the display showed the rocket's altimeter climbing, and the tether stood up out of the mud and the stake, a thread of copper going up into the darkness, and the rain came down it and ran off it, and for four seconds the whole thing was working, and she saw the display count the leader in the column: an electric field that fell before it rose, the little heartbeat lightning makes before it decides.

Then the mast arced.

It happened at the base, not the top, which was the thing she understood too late. The mast base was in the mud at the fence and the water ran down it, and the copper ground strap ran off along the wire, and the wire was old and wet and connected to the culvert, and somewhere in the two hundred yards of wet fence the copper and the steel and the water found a voltage difference the ground could not carry, and the whole fence line lit for an instant and dumped back up the strap into the housing.

The mast base flashed white and threw blue and Wren was on her back in the mud.

She did not lose time. That was the first thing she noticed: no drop, no gap, she was standing and then she was sitting and the rain was falling and the whole rig was screaming.

The housing screamed. The main capacitor was venting, the case swollen and blown and the electrolyte spraying out of it in a fine hot line like a cut water hose, and the display went to a bar of red and then to nothing, and the launch circuit shorted, and the spool's brake let go and eight hundred feet of tether came back off the cloud in a long slack loop and paid out and fell, all of it, in coils, over the fence and into the draw. The rocket's light came down in the wheat a quarter mile west, still burning on the way, and went out.

The rig was dark in eleven seconds.

Wren stood up. She had the maul in her hand because she had been holding it for the stake and had never put it down; she did not remember picking it up. Her ears rang. The whole draw was rain and the wheat field to the west was rain, and the four flares hung under the base and moved west-northwest with the shelf.

"Grab the housing," she said.

"Wren."

"Grab the housing, Tobin."

They got it. The housing was hot and wet and the case was blown, and they cut the guy with the wire snips and the housing came down into their hands, and the two of them carried it off the tripod and put it in the ditch, because the ditch was the only place not on fire and the only place below the fence, and the housing was already dead, and the ditch was where you put a dead thing so it would not kill you on the way out. Tobin took the tripod. Wren took the spool on its side by the brake and the spool rolled on the ground and the tether came in behind it in wet loops, and they were three minutes getting the whole dead mess back up the rise to the truck bed. The wind was west of north now and the flares had gone out two miles west and the shelf was over the county line.

She loaded the housing into the bed and put the spool on top of it and slammed the tailgate, and then she stood with both hands on the tailgate in the rain and shook, and the shake was not cold.

Tobin came around. He had the flashlight and his coveralls were black through and there was mud up his side to the shoulder, and he stood beside her and he did not offer comfort and did not step back.

"You rushed it," he said, plain.

Wren said nothing.

"You got it in the rain and the rain was coming and the mast was wet before the base was set, so you grounded a wet mast to a wet fence, and you put the stamp of your foot on a circuit that was live to two hundred joules." He said it in his mechanic's voice, flat and ranked, not blaming, listing. "Torqued it hard before the threads were seated. You skipped the ground test. You had a clear-air rig on a night with a cell on it and you fired it inside three minutes because she fired flares." He put the light on the housing and the vented capacitor. "It ran hot and it let go. That's not the storm. That's you."

"I know," Wren said.

"You know."

"I know." She turned around and leaned on the tailgate and looked north, and the wall cloud was there, backlit by the moon, going west. "I had the notch and I had the number and I had the pass and I had forty minutes, and I spent all four on it at once. I put the stamp on it." The rain wiped her face for her. "I built the rig for a dry cell. I knew that. I took the number on the ridge and the number at the airstrip and the number twenty-four percent and I decided the rig would hold because the rig had to hold." She looked at the black display. "It held on the roof. It held on the roof in May and I told myself that was the same as holding here."

"It's not the same as holding here."

"No."

The rain kept on and neither of them moved for a while, and the water came off the truck's roof in a lip and broke on the ground, and out west a wire fence sang in the gust and quit. Wren crouched down at the tailgate and put her hand on the housing, on the cold part of the case, and stayed there, because her hands wanted to be doing something and there was nothing to do that would not make it worse.

"June had a thermos in the truck," she said, to the housing, not to Tobin. "It was on the floor of the cab by the shifter and it rolled under the seat when the bolt came in, and my mother has it on the shelf over the sink. It's got a dent in it from rolling under the seat. That's what's left of the truck. That and the licence plate and the seat cover." She wiped her hands on her thighs and stood up. "So when I say I know, I mean I know what it costs to be wrong about this. I have the receipt."

Tobin put the flashlight down on the tailgate and did not say anything for a while. Then he asked a question, which was what he did when he was not sure where to put his hands, and the question was the one she had been not answering since the day she had seen his look at the fuel bladder.

"What's the housing worth?" he said. "In cash. Because I can get the parts ordered, and I know a man at the co-op shop in Pella, and if it's cash I can have a capacitor in it by Friday. Not tonight. Tonight's done."

"It's not Friday," Wren said. "It's Tuesday and the storm's Thursday."

"I know what day it is."

"Then you know Friday doesn't matter." She picked up the housing and set it on the flat of the tailgate. "The main stack's gone. Two hundred joules out the fence. The trigger circuit's shorted and the altimeter board's wet, and I've got one housing and no spare, and the storm's on a heading of two-five-zero, and I've got"—she looked at the schedule on the seat—"three runs left before dawn. It's over the county line now."

Tobin picked up the flashlight and shone it into the housing. "Not over," he said, quiet. "The stack's blown and it arced the base, but the housing's plumb. The mast is standing. The tripod's in the bed and the stake's in the ground. What you lost is one part and one hour." He looked up. "You didn't lose the theory. You lost the part. Parts come. Ask your friend."

The headlights came up the road behind them then. One set, low, and then high, and then they dipped, and a dented nickel-colored Ford with a Wyoming plate slid off the gravel onto the shoulder and stopped behind Wren's truck.

Odell got out with the wipers going and a wooden crate under one arm and the map tube under the other, and the crate was nailed shut and the nail heads were rusted and the tag on the side was brown with age and had a 1977 date on it and a stamp that said what it was before the stamp faded.

He came up to the tailgate and looked at the rig in the truck bed, and he did not ask what happened. He had the answer already, the way he had the answer for the pencil flinch and the tower and the word seeding: he had read it off the drive up and off the one look at the black display and off the order the truck was parked in.

"Blown stack," he said. "That's the main capacitor, and that's a 1976 model. They had a hundred of them at the tower under the shelter, in a crate, because the tower's dish ran on the same stack and the tower never needed a hundred." He set the crate in the bed and got his knife out. "I gave them to the state in 2007, except I lied about the last crate, because the dish was still turning, and because I couldn't let a good machine die. That's every reason I give for things." He pried the crate open. "It's the same stack you need. The oiler didn't change and nothing out here changed. You can file that."

He pulled out a capacitor and handed it to Wren, and it was new in the sense that meant never used, and it weighed what it weighed, and the terminals were clean.

She took it and stood in the rain with it in both hands, and Tobin took the cover off the housing and shone the light in, and Odell stood beside the tailgate with the knife in one hand and the crate lid in the other and looked north at the wall cloud going west, and Wren saw his face do the thing it had done at the fence four days ago, which was to go still while it decided something it had already decided.

"You came back to the truck for a reason," she said.

"Two reasons," Odell said. "The crate was one." He closed the knife and put it in his pocket. "The other was to say a thing I should have said at the fence and didn't, and I'll say it now, because there are three runs left and you're going to be in the way of all of them."

He looked at Tobin, and Tobin took the cover and the light and went around to the far side of the truck and began, without being asked, to lay the tool roll out on the spare tire and sort the sockets by size.

"The matrix I designed in seventy-nine had a fault in it," Odell said. "Not the timing. The profile. It flared the flank at the boundary-layer height, because at the boundary layer the inflow vector is clean and the seed travels and the cell takes it. That's what the program wanted to be true, because it's cheap and it's safe and it looks like rain instead of weather." He put his hand flat on the housing. "The cell took the seed at the flank and it didn't fan. It deepened, because a supercell's inflow doesn't run at the boundary layer past the first mile. It runs up the column. And up the column the seed is not a fence at all. It's a layer in the stack, and the stack leans with everything else, and the more seed you put in, the more the column has to lean against."

"So the fence was never a fence," Wren said. "It was a food plot."

"Yes."

"And you knew that in the seventies."

"I knew it in the field the way you knew it at the rack." Odell took his hand off the housing. "I wrote the report the way it needed to be written so the office would close and the flares would pull. The report said the seeding was inconclusive, and the seeding was inconclusive in the sense that day at the tower, in that the state never published it. I have carried that sentence for forty years and I have never once been able to make it come out clean." He looked at her. "Vance has the old matrix. She has my timing, my height, my direction of run. She is not seeding a fence. She is seeding a feed, on a schedule, and the more of it she puts in, the more the storm leans, and the lean is aimed at a target she wrote herself, and the target is your elevator. She may believe the lean is the offense. She may believe the elevator is a fence. It doesn't matter. The numbers don't hold either way, and there is one thing on this earth that will break her grasp on it, and it is your instrument, and your instrument is the only thing on that trailer that uses her own signature as proof."

Wren stood in the rain with the capacitor in her hands. The night opened out into something with a shape: the strip, the schedule, the sodium light at the gas station, the four flares hanging under a shelf going west, and the pull of wind coming off the notch in long steady draws the way the storm breathed.

"Then we don't outrun the storm," she said. "We outrun the schedule."

She wiped the capacitor dry and Tobin brought the sockets, and the two of them sat on the tailgate with the housing between them, and Odell stood over them with the map light taped to a fence post, and they took the blown stack out of its cradle and laid it in the ditch and fitted the new one.

The work was slow, and part of what made it slow was that Wren would not go fast.

She checked the terminals twice. She ran the leads to the trigger circuit and tested continuity at every joint before she seated the board, and when Tobin offered her the wrench for the cradle bolts, she took it and torqued in sequence and felt each one seat before she took the next. The trigger circuit was shorted and she cut the short with the snips and reseated the lead and ran the meter down the path a fourth time, and the display came up green off the truck battery, and she stood on the tailgate and watched it and did not trust it for a long thirty seconds.

Then she killed the battery and set the housing face down in the bed and covered it with the ground mat.

"Test it dry in the cab," she said. "Ground test at the third stake, not the first. And we do not fire in the second curtain."

"Second curtain," Tobin said. "That's the one that's over us now."

"That's the one that's over us now." Wren got in the cab and started the truck. "The schedule has a flight at two and one at four-thirty and one at six. There's a gap in the middle of it where the inflow changes height, and the notch with it. That's the window. That's the notch I can stand in without drowning the rig." She looked at the dark. "She fires flares into a storm that's already feeding. I fire once into a storm that's between feeds. First one grounded. Second one aimed."

Odell put the crate lid back and set the crate against the housing in the bed and got in on the passenger side without a word about the map, and Tobin slid to the middle of the bench with the schedule on his knees.

The wipers came on and the headlights came up and the county line road showed itself in the rain, dark and straight and running east, and out beyond it the wall cloud went west with the flares under it and the moon behind it, and somewhere in the field to the west, out in the stubble, a seeding plane was warming on a private strip for the two o'clock run.

Wren put the truck in gear and pulled off the shoulder, and they went east into it, and the rig lay in the bed under the mat, dead and clean and checked and charged for the next thing, and the storm was over the county line now and still going west the way the schedule wanted, one pass closer to home.
