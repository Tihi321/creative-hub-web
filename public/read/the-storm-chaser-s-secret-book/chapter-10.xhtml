The Grave on the Ridge

The column came apart from the bottom, and from the fence line Wren watched it come apart the way she had watched it build for two days, in pieces and in order.

The funnel left the ground first. It did not lift the way the pictures showed, not folding up into the cloud like a hand withdrawn; it thinned from the bottom and roped, turning from a cone to a cord, and the cord walked north along the field for four hundred yards and then let go entirely and hung in the rain, spinning, going nowhere, and then there was no funnel at all, only a black wall cloud leaning the wrong way and raining on an empty quarter-section.

The wall cloud kept moving west because the whole storm was still moving west. That part did not stop. A supercell is a machine that does not turn off when its tornado dies; it goes on hauling warm air up its flank, and the mesocyclone high in the base went on turning or gave way to a new one closer to the storm's heart, and the anvil went on streaming east, and the rain on the wheat came down so hard the truck rocked on its springs.

But the lean was gone. The base had been pulled apart at the altitude where the rotation lives, and the track had stopped breathing, and the core was now putting its rain on ground that had nothing on it but fence posts and a dead culvert and one red heifer standing against the wind, alive, blinking, unbothered.

Two miles short. Wren did the arithmetic standing in the rain with the sledge still in her hands: the funnel had been on the ground for eleven minutes and had covered the field north of the draw and lifted at a point two miles and a quarter from the Coyote Flats city limits sign.

She had read the number off the display, and the display was a box of aluminum in a fence line with a smoking stake and a spool of copper going up nowhere, and the copper was still hot enough that the rain hissed on it.

"Get down off the bed," she said.

Odell was already down. He had his hand on the spool shaft and his head cocked, listening, and what he was listening to was the radio in the cab, which had gone from the spill channel to something else, something clipped and flat and repeated.

"Say that again," Tobin said into the mic from the cab.

The reply came through the open door, small and tinny and clear: "—units, all units, be advised, four vehicles northbound on County 14 at the draw, no plates, refusing to yield, stop sticks authorized at the PELLA 6 junction, repeat, stop sticks authorized—"

Tobin put the mic down and looked at Wren through the windshield.

Wren looked east, where the four dark trucks had been running south and were now, by the sound of it, running into a roadblock that had their description.

"He gave them our coordinates," Tobin said.

"No," Odell said. "He gave them mine."

Wren turned around. Odell had come around to the tailgate with the spool in his arms and the tether looped around his elbow, and he was standing in the rain with the water running off his hat brim and his face gone very still and very old, and he was doing what he always did, which was telling her the hard thing after he had told her everything else.

"I called the state inquiry line from the gas station at eleven last night," he said. "Not 911. The inquiry line, the number on the back of the 1979 report, the one with the box nobody ever opened. I gave them a location and a heading and a time and a name, and the name was mine, and I told them to look east on County 14 at the draw at two-forty in the morning." He shifted the spool in his arms. "I did that before I knew whether you would fire. Before I knew whether I would help you fire. I wanted the road closed behind us either way, because whichever way it went, somebody was going to come out of that field with a laptop and twenty-two empty flare racks and I only get to testify once."

Wren stood in the rain and understood it, all of it, the whole shape of the man: the eight months of correspondence, the flinch at the word seeding, the drive back from Wyoming, the confession in the stairwell and the confession in the motel room and this one, which was the last one and the heaviest one and the one he had been carrying the longest.

"You set yourself up," she said.

"I set the schedule up," Odell said. "I built the matrix. The flares that came off those racks tonight came off a sheet I wrote in 1979, and I put the sheet in a drawer and the drawer was never gone, it was just closed. Somebody has to say that out loud in a room with a stenographer." He looked past her at the plain where the funnel had been and where the rain now was, and the rain was ordinary rain, the kind that makes farmers sleep. "The crew's not going to run past a stop stick with the road wet. They'll ditch at the junction and walk, and the patrol will find four trucks full of paraphernalia and one very angry woman standing next to the number one truck, and she will tell them her filing is current and her authority is real, and she will be right, and none of that will matter, because her filing did not cover the operation and her authority did not cover Coyote Flats."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I wrote the filing," Odell said, "and I know what I didn't put in it."

They drove back to the motel with the rig in the bed and the tether coiled and wet, and by the time they got to the room the rain had gone east and the county had gone quiet in that specific way that comes after a storm has failed to kill anybody, which is to say it went quiet like a held breath let out.

The news came in pieces over the next six hours, the way news came in Coyote Flats: from the radio, from the scanner, from a deputy Wren woke at four-forty by calling the non-emergency line and asking who had jurisdiction over a supercell. By dawn she had most of it.

Four vehicles, plates out of state, stopped at the PELLA 6 junction at three-twelve in the morning. Two crew members declined to be interviewed and were detained. The flight officer was detained separately after the stop sticks took her lead truck's front tires at the crossbar and she got out and stood in the middle of the intersection with her hands in her pockets and told the patrol that the operation had been filed under the High Plains Augmentation Study, that the file was active, and that the state patrol had no standing over a permitted weather-modification program. The patrol had radioed it in and been told to hold everybody, and had held everybody.

Marisol Vance had not apologized to anyone. She had given her name and her license number and the location of her aeroplane, and had asked whether the shelter in Pella had taken anybody in, and had been told it had not, and had asked whether the school had taken anybody, and had been told the school had taken forty-one and the highway junction was clear and nobody had been hurt anywhere on the track.

Then they told her about the discharge, and about the two miles, and about the fence line.

Wren got that part secondhand, from the deputy at the junction, who told it to a reporter with the tape running and did not know he was telling it to Wren at all. The storm had missed Pella because the storm had collapsed, and the storm had collapsed because of a strike recorded at a fence line nine miles west, and not because of anything the crew had seeded.

She didn't know that yet when she sat down, the deputy said. But she was told that at three-forty, and she stayed where she was.

Three-forty in the morning, in the rain, on the tailgate of a truck with its front tires blown out, Marisol Vance was told that her town had been spared by accident. Wren read it in a wire story on the third day, with her own name misspelled in the fourth paragraph, and put the paper down, and went back to work.

She published everything.

The log set was the easy part. She had two years of differential voltages and four months of clean calibration and a timestamped record of every peak her rig had ever logged, and she assembled it into a single sequence with the columns matched and the offsets noted, and Odell read it and found three errors and she fixed them and sent it to the archive at the state school, which was where a sixteen-year-old published when no journal would take her calls.

The isotope data was harder, because the isotope data was the state's now, seized from the number six rack head and the number two pump and the barrels in the wheat field, and there was a chain-of-custody letter to write and a signature on a strip of paper to prove Wren had taken her own sample in front of the operator before anything was seized at all. She wrote the letter at the co-op office desk in the coldest room in town, with the space heater on the fritz and her mother's coffee going cold on the corner, and she made four phone calls and lost her temper twice and apologized once.

The third thing was the story, and the story was what the papers wanted: a storm steered, a program that would not die, a town that had died in 1979, a girl on a fence line with a rocket on a tether, and a supercell that came apart two miles short of the co-op elevator because it lost the charge it was standing on. That third thing hit the wire on the sixth day, and by the eighth day there were cars from two networks at the end of the elevator lot and a woman from the state bureau of investigation in the co-op office with a recorder and a list of questions, and a man from the county attorney who told Wren, kindly, that she should have a parent in the room for the next conversation.

Her mother sat through it. Her mother sat through the whole interview and did not say a word except to ask, at the end, whether her daughter had been placed in the path of the storm by anybody, and the woman said that was one of the things she was there to establish.

The inquiry opened the following Tuesday at the county courthouse, which was a building with a bad roof and a good furnace and a courtroom that held ninety people if nobody breathed. Wren testified in the morning: the rig, the schedule, the isotope comparison, the two-five-zero heading and the eleven-mile fix and the words co-op, 40.9N, ingress in a hand that was not hers.

Odell testified in the afternoon.

He was in the chair for two hours and eleven minutes. He named the High Plains Augmentation Study and the 1977 tower and the matrix, the August ninth schedule in 1979 and the seeded cell that went off forecast. He named Catherine Duley and her two boys, Emory Prasky, and Ana Reyes, who had been on the south road on her bicycle because her mother had been told the storm was going the other way, and he read all six names off a single sheet of paper in a voice that did not shake and did not hurry and did not stop. He told the panel that the program had worked better than it had ever been given credit for, and that burying a working program had been the crime, and that the crime was his, and that the man who had called him three months ago about the 1979 numbers was dead in a plane crash in June, and that he had no evidence about the crash and would not speculate under oath.

He gave the panel the crate. The crate had a tag on it, and the tag had a faded stamp on it that said PROPERTY OF HPA STUDY — DO NOT REMOVE FROM RIDGE, in letters that had gone yellow, and he had lied about it to a state inspector in 2007 and would like that on the record too.

The panel asked him why he had come back.

He said: because the schedule was mine, and somebody was running it.

The panel asked him if he had been involved in the seeding of a supercell over Coyote Flats.

He said: no. I stood on a fence line and handed a girl a tether, and I called in a roadblock I did not know whether I would get, and I did everything I could to put myself between that matrix and the town it was aimed at, and I did it forty years too late.

They arrested Marisol Vance on the eleventh day, on the courthouse steps, in the middle of the afternoon, on three counts of unauthorized weather modification and one of reckless endangerment, and she went down the steps with her head up and her hands cuffed in front and gave the cameras nothing.

She learned the rest at eight that evening, in a holding-room with a barred window and a lawyer who was still in a car somewhere south of the county line. There is one recording of what happened next, made by a reporter who had talked his way past a desk sergeant with a press pass and a thermos, and it is eleven seconds of a woman not answering a question, and then a voice low and even, saying, I'm not going to answer that.

The other sentence came three weeks later, in a plea document, in paragraph four, in the passive voice, one line long, buried where no camera would ever point.

Tobin stayed.

He did not say he was staying. He just did not go. He had nowhere to go: the crew was impounded and its operator was on bail and he was nineteen and the state patrol had taken his name and let him walk, and he had walked back to the motel and slept on the floor of the room Odell had not paid for, and in the morning he had gone out to the truck in the rain and taken the scorched board out of the rig and put it on the tailgate and started soldering, which was the only argument he had ever made about anything.

Wren found him there at seven-thirty with the pencil iron plugged into the motel's outside outlet and the board half-bridged and three of her labels relabeled in his hand.

"You don't have to fix that," she said.

"It's an hour's work," Tobin said. "Then it's a box of aluminum again instead of a box of evidence."

"Tobin."

"There's nothing for me in Pella," he said, and did not look up. "There's a lot in Pella. My mom, my aunt, the whole street where Ana lived. But there's nothing for me there, if that makes a difference." He set the iron down and looked at the board and then at her. "I came to the crew to find out whether the thing that did it still existed. I found out. I found out because of Ana, and because of the sheet, and because you're the one who took it apart. I'm staying with the person who took it apart."

Wren did not ask him a different question. She got her other screwdriver and knelt on the wet asphalt next to him and held the board while he finished the bridge, and they did not talk about Pella again for a long time.

She went to the ridge on the fifth Sunday, which was the first clear day after the front, and she went because her mother said the two of them were going up and it was not a question.

The ridge graveyard sat above the flats on the east shoulder of the only hill in the county, and the tower stood above it on the crest, eighty feet of grey lattice with its south leg out of plumb and its radome catching the afternoon sun like a lens, and the road up to the tower was the same road that ran along the fence of the graveyard, and you could see, from June's plot, the whole of Coyote Flats laid out west and flat: the elevator, the co-op bays, the streetlight on Depot Road, the wheat going out to the horizon in long brown sheets, and the town drawn small and inevitable under the sky.

June's headstone was grey granite with a wheat band carved along the base and a single line under the dates, and Wren had read it maybe forty times and had never once gotten through it without wanting to argue with it.

SHE WAS DRIVING HOME.

Wren's mother carried a fold-out chair up the hill and did not sit in it. Odell walked up last, and stopped at the level ground a dozen paces back, and stood with his hands behind him and his hat in them and his eyes on the wheat, and said nothing, and did not need to, and the not-needing was the part Wren only noticed afterward.

"It was a cell with no name," Wren said. "That's what the record says. No watch, no warning, no box on the map. A cell thirty miles wide with one bolt in it, and the bolt came down on the county road east of town at four in the afternoon and it went into the truck and through the frame and it took her out of a moving vehicle on a dry road with the sun out."

"I was on the phone with the co-op," her mother said. "I heard it on the scanner while I was on hold."

"The bolt was a strike out of the anvil, twelve miles from the rain, and there was no model that put convection over the county road, and I have read every line of that record four hundred times." Wren put her hand flat on the top of the stone and left it there. "And I built a box to prove that you can take a strike that's coming anyway and finish it early, on purpose, where you want it. That's what the box does. That's what it did on the fence line." She looked at the wheat and the town and the tower. "It doesn't bring anybody back. It just makes the sky answer on the record."

Her mother was quiet for a while.

"You know what I was afraid of," she said. "The whole time. Not the storm. You. You were fourteen and you had stopped eating supper at the table because the radar loop was up, and you had stopped saying her name, and there was a year where I did not know whether you were chasing the weather or chasing the truck she was in." She put her own hand on the stone, next to Wren's, and her hand was smaller and older and steady. "And then a man from Wyoming drove into my lot and you started saying her name again, and I have been trying to decide since March whether I should have thrown him out of the county, and I have decided I should not have. Filing it. That's all."

Wren took her hand off the stone and looked at the horizon, which was doing what the horizon here always did, which was show you the weather an hour before it arrived.

There was a storm coming. She could see it from the ridge, a long low shelf of cumulus eighty miles out with the anvil pushing east, unseeded and unscheduled and moving on its own tracks, and there was no leak in the shape of it and no lean in its base and no reason in the world for her to go and look except that she wanted to.

"Go," her mother said.

Wren looked at her.

"You have been standing on this hill for two years with your hands empty and your logbook in the truck," her mother said, "and if you stand here one more minute doing arithmetic on a tombstone I'm going to sit in that chair and wait you out. Go and look at the storm. Not for her. Not for the town. Not for the paper." She nodded at Odell. "Take the man. He needs something to look at that isn't a courtroom."

They went down to the road.

Odell stopped at the truck box and looked back up at the ridge and the tower and the small grey stone in its wheat band, and then he got in on the passenger side and shut the door and put his hat on the dash, and said nothing about June, which was the last courtesy.

Wren drove down the ridge road east and then north on the county line with the window down and the radio off and the sky opening ahead of her like a place she had never been, and the storm was eighty miles out and it was nobody's but its own, and she was not going to fire at it and she was not going to prove anything, and she was going to stand in the wheat and watch it build and come west and rain itself out over an empty quarter-section, and take a note or two, maybe, if her hands got bored.

The road ran straight. The sky did the rest.
