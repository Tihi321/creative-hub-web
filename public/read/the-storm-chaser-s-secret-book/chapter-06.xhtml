Aimed at Home

They drove back west on the section line with the laptop shut on the seat between them and the comparison in the notebook in Wren's lap, and neither of them said anything for six miles. The six miles were the flattest six miles in the county, wheat stubble on both sides all the way to the horizon, one windmill turning slow against a sky gone the color of a bruise.

At the county line Wren said, "Two-five-zero."

Odell said, "I know."

"Say it anyway."

"Two-five-zero is the heading to the co-op elevator. Eleven miles east of Pella. Eleven-point-two by the road."

"And you knew that when I ran it."

Odell looked out the passenger window at the dark coming west. He had the map tube between his knees and his hands folded on top of it, and he had not opened it since the field, and he had written one line on the back of it in pencil and shown it to nobody.

"I knew it before you ran it," he said. "I knew it at four o'clock, standing at that fence, when she said the word grass. There isn't eleven miles of grass west of that field, Wren. There's nine hundred people and a co-op and a shelter with a sump pump in it."

"Then why didn't you say it?"

"Because I didn't want it to be true," he said. "Which is the whole history of me. Which is why I'm going to tell you the rest now, and you're going to drive, because we're going to be on the road anyway and it's a forty-minute story and we have forty minutes."

He told it flat, watching the white line, in the voice of a man reading a logbook he had written himself forty years ago and had not opened since.

The program had a name. He said the name once and it sat in the cab like a tool on a bench. The High Plains Augmentation Study, funded out of a state water board and a defense grant with two purposes printed on it and one purpose in the back room, and the back room purpose was not rain for wheat. The back room purpose was control: whether a man who understood a supercell could put it where he wanted it, and whether that could be done with a rack of flares and a schedule and a young engineer who was too good at his job to ask why.

"I was twenty-nine," he said. "I designed the matrix. Not the rockets. The matrix. The flare timing against the inflow vector. I found the window myself, the six-minute one, the one that looks like a fence if you're the kind of person who wants it to look like a fence."

"You told me it worked."

"It worked. That's what I've been trying to make you hear. It worked and it was aimed, and the aim was the asset, and in August of seventy-nine we had a cell that was bigger than the matrix was built for, and the man who signed the flights signed them anyway, because the test he wanted was a big one, and the cell took the seed and stepped off the forecast and went thirty miles in a direction no paper in that room had on it, and it came apart over a town called Baird." He said the name and then said it again, quieter. "Baird. Thirty miles west of the ridge. Four hundred and ten people and a grain elevator and a school with a gym floor we'd refinished in the spring, because the county had put the program office in the back of the school and the school had put the program office in the back of the town, and that's how it works out here, everything in the back of everything else."

Wren drove.

"They hit the shelter in eighteen minutes," Odell said. "Four hundred and ten in the town and forty-one at the elevator and the gym floor held the rest. Six died. Six is not a massacre. Six was enough. The state investigator came out and asked what had seeded the cell and the man who signed the flights looked at me across the table, and I looked back, and then I wrote the report that said the program had never been conclusively shown to work." He turned his hands over on the tube. "I wrote it carefully. I wrote it well. I wrote it so that it was true, technically, which is the worst kind of true there is. They closed the office and pulled the flares and I moved to Wyoming, and every year since I have opened the paper in August and looked for Baird, and every year there's less of it, and last year there was a co-op and a church and a woman who runs a post office out of her front room."

"Why now," Wren said. "Forty years. Why Thursday?"

"Because three months ago a man I used to sign flights with called me to ask whether the matrix was still valid, and whether I had the seventy-nine numbers, and whether I'd come out and look at a schedule." Odell looked at her for the first time since they had crossed the county line. "He's dead now. Plane crash in June, single-engine, out of a private strip. But the schedule is real and the schedule is mine, Wren. It's my matrix on that placard. Somebody dug it up and put a fence around a town with it and called it saving lives, and the somebody is Vance, and Vance believes it, and that's the part that frightens me worse than the lying."

They came up out of the low ground and Coyote Flats stood against the last light, the elevator white and square and the roofs flat and the streetlight on Depot Road already on, because the photocell in it had been dead four years and it ran on the timer now, and it had been on since noon.

She pulled into the co-op lot at twelve-thirty and killed the engine and sat with her hands on the wheel.

The lot was empty the way it was every night, her mother's trucks in their bays and the flatbed with the bad axle still blocking bay three, the streetlight buzzing over Depot Road, the cornices of the co-op office window catching the one bulb the whole town kept. Twelve-thirty. She had left Coyote Flats at seven-forty that morning and driven east into a storm's formation point and stood at a fence and watched a woman say the word grass, and the clock on the co-op wall, visible through the office window, said the day had not even turned over yet. It felt like a week. It felt like the same day, because it was: the storm that would arrive on Thursday had started its approach on Tuesday morning, and every hour since had been the same hour happening again a little closer.

There was one other vehicle in the lot. A county pickup, engine still ticking as it cooled, parked in the far corner with its flashers off and two men in it, and neither of them got out, and Wren did not recognize the truck and did not have time to.

She went inside and turned on the co-op's desk lamp and sat down in the coldest room in town and pulled the phone off the wall, and she did not need the book because her mother's number was memorized.

Her mother answered on the third ring. "It's late and the storm's coming and I don't want to argue."

"I'm not arguing," Wren said. "Mom, listen. Don't talk. I need you to get the elevator crew and whoever's still in the office out of the building tonight and put them somewhere off the lot, and I need you to open the basement for whoever comes, and I need it done before dawn."

The line went quiet so long Wren could hear the refrigerator.

"What did you do," her mother said.

"I didn't do it. I found it. There's a crew seeding that storm, east of Pella, on a schedule, and the schedule has the track coming off Pella and onto a heading of two-five-zero, and the heading points at the co-op. Not at the county. Not at the grass. The elevator."

"Wren."

"Check the radar yourself. The co-op has the feed on the office computer and the feed says the same thing. The track bent at the county line at ten-fifteen and it bent west, and it's still bending."

Another quiet. Then her mother's voice, and it had changed shape, gone flat and practical the way it did in storm season when the sirens went.

"The elevator crew's off at eight on Thursdays. It's Tuesday night. There's nobody in the building but us."

"Then get out of the house tonight. Stay at the shelter."

"I'm not sleeping in a basement in August over a forecast."

"Then sleep at Aunt Ruth's in Wilcox, it's twelve miles east, and Mom—" Wren stopped herself and then did not stop. "I'll come get you in the morning. I'll come get you before the storm either way, I promise, but I need you out of the house tonight."

"Why tonight if the storm's Thursday?"

"Because the seeding runs overnight," Wren said. "It's the inflow. They seed at night when the inflow's clean. That's the schedule. That's what she's got in that trailer and that's what the plane's for, and Odell says the seventy-nine cell took two passes in the dark before it stepped off the forecast, and if it steps off the forecast tonight I want to know you're not under it."

Her mother was quiet a third time and then said, "You said 'she.'"

"Vance. Marisol Vance. She's out there in a wheat field with four trucks and a fuel bladder and she told me to my face that she's doing it, and she gave me a sample off her own rack, and I measured it, and the measurement says it's making the storm instead of turning it."

"Wren." Her mother's voice was very level. "Come home tonight."

"I can't. I have to go back out to her first."

"Then don't." A pause. "Wren, don't."

"I'll be at the shelter before it hits," Wren said. "I love you. Get out of the house."

She hung up before her mother could do anything else with the sentence, because if her mother said her name one more time in that voice Wren would turn the truck around and drive home and watch the storm from the kitchen window with her hands around a mug, and that was not a thing you could do with a schedule in your notebook and a comparison on a laptop and eleven miles of heading on the map. She sat with her hand on the receiver for a second, then stood and killed the lamp, because the office was a place somebody might come looking in, and she had learned in four days to keep her light off.

Odell was on the tailgate with the map open on his knees and the pencil line he had written at the field face-up now, and he had written four words: under-flare the flank.

"That's what I wrote," he said, before she asked. "That's the whole difference between seventy-nine and now. We flared the flank and it just made the base deeper. Vance is flaring the base on purpose, because she thinks the base is what you aim." He folded the map. "It's what you feed. You can't fence a thing with its own food. That's the sentence I should have said at the fence and didn't."

"Say it to her," Wren said. "Get in the truck."

They caught the crew at the gas station on the county line, which was the only gas station between Pella and the Flats and the only lights for eleven miles, and there were two of the dark trucks on the apron with their boxes still open and a flatbed at the air hose and eleven crew members standing around in the sodium light like a work crew at the end of a shift.

Vance's truck was not there. Wren checked and checked again and it was not there.

What was there was the flatbed trailer with the racks folded down and the generator gone off the trailer and set on the concrete, still humming, and a boy in coveralls standing at the pump island with a five-gallon can in one hand and something rolled in the other.

Tobin Reyes looked at Wren across the pumps and did not look away this time.

"I've got ten minutes," he said. "She's east. She took the plane truck and two racks to the strip for the eleven o'clock, and she left the rest of us to fuel and move south to the lease road by the airstrip. There's a flight plan in this." He held up the roll. "It's the whole schedule from here to dawn and it's got the next run's coordinates on it and it's got the target on it in her own hand, and when I read the target this morning I stood at the trailer for a while and then I put it in my coveralls."

"Why?"

"Because I'm from Pella," Tobin said. "And she told all of us, every one of us, on Tuesday, that we were keeping it off Pella, and I believed her until I saw the sheet. And because you're the first person who came out here and asked a question instead of agreeing with her." He held the roll out. "It's not the original. I copied it at the strip office Tuesday night. She doesn't know. Nobody knows."

The other crew members were watching. One of them, an older man with a clipboard, had taken two steps toward the pumps and stopped, and was standing in the light with his arms at his sides, deciding.

Wren took the roll and untied it on the hood of the truck, and Odell came around with the flashlight, and they read it under the sodium light with the moth at the bulb and the pumps ticking.

It was the schedule in the run-state form, and it was better than the fragment she had copied off the placard. Flights at 23:00, 02:00, 04:30 and 06:00, launch and target fixed, and on the target line, in a hand Wren recognized from an initial on a rack strip, a coordinate pair and three words: co-op, 40.9N, ingress.

"That's the elevator," Wren said. "That's our elevator. That's not a fence. That's a target. She wrote it as a target."

"That's what I said," Tobin said. "She wrote it as a target because to her it is one. It's the thing you aim at to get the bend you want." He looked at the pumps, then at the road east, where the storm's anvil had swallowed the last of the stars. "She called it a fence for four days and then she wrote it as a target on her own sheet. I don't know what that means except that she's going to do it."

"It means the fence and the target are the same coordinates," Odell said, "and she has always known that, and she is not lying to you about the storm. She is lying to herself about the town."

Wren rolled the schedule and put it in the notebook beside the tower strip and the rack strip and the field strip, four pieces of paper in one binding now. She stood in the gas station light with the smell of gasoline and the pumps ticking and the wind coming up from the east and did the arithmetic the way you do it when the arithmetic is finally the only thing left. Eleven miles and a heading of two-five-zero and a co-op elevator with a basement that held everybody. A schedule with four more seeding runs before dawn. Twenty-two flare passes and a woman with a filing and a plane and a certainty she would not move off of, on a night that had already bent the track west past the point where any of them could unbend it.

"Start the truck," she said. "We go west, not east. I'm not going to find her. If she's flying the eleven o'clock, she's off the ground and I can't reach a plane. The rig's on the truck and the rig is the only thing on this road she can't outrun."

Odell looked at her across the hood.

"That's the decision," he said.

"That's the decision," Wren said. "I am not going to stop the seeding. I can't. I'm going to put the rig in the notch west of her pass line and I'm going to fire into the base of her own feed, and either the discharge pulls the separation out of the column and it ropes out before it reaches the county line, or I'm wrong and I lose the rig and she loses the town. There's no third thing. I'm going to the fence line above the draw and I'm going to sit in the inflow and wait for the gap, and I'm going to fire once, and I am not going to run it back to the county first to ask permission, because there's no time and no phone and no deputy who'll believe a comparison off a laptop. If that makes me something, it makes me something."

"It makes you the only person on the road who's actually read the matrix," Odell said, and opened the door.

Tobin had not moved from the island. He stood with the gas can in his hand and his eyes going from Wren to the road east to the other crew, who were still watching, still not moving, the older man with the clipboard still two steps out and still deciding.

"Go on," Tobin said to them, loud, all at once, into the sodium light. "Get in the trucks. Get south to the lease road and fuel up and be where she told you to be. If she asks, I loaded the racks and I'm coming behind you."

The crew moved. It took them a slow minute and then it took them fast, doors and engines, and the two dark trucks pulled off the apron and swung east and south and were gone, and the older man with the clipboard got in the last one and did not look back.

Tobin put the gas can down on the island and walked to Wren's truck and got in the back seat of the bench without being asked, and set his hands on his knees, and looked straight ahead through the windshield at the west road.

"She's going to ask where I was at eleven," he said. "I'm going to have to give her an answer."

"You're going to have to choose a side," Wren said.

"There aren't sides," Tobin said. "There's a schedule with my aunt's name on a page in Baird and a set of coordinates in a girl's handwriting, and you're the one who took it apart." He looked at her in the mirror. "I'm staying with the person who took it apart. That's the whole answer."

Wren put the truck in gear and pulled off the apron and turned west onto the county line road.

The wind came over the lot as they left it and took the moth off the bulb, and behind them the gas station lights stayed on with no one at the pumps, and ahead of them the storm's whole west flank stood up in the dark like a wall being built on purpose, one pass at a time, aimed at the last lit window in the county, and the schedule lay on the seat between her and Odell with four pieces of paper folded inside the notebook binding and a coordinate pair written in a small firm hand that was not hers.

She drove into it with the gas pedal down and the odometer ticking and the wipers coming on, and behind her to the east the road to Pella ran back under the rain to a shelter that was full and a school that was opening and a plane climbing off a private strip with its lights on, and none of that changed the heading and none of that changed the instrument.

The rig lay in the bed under the mat, dead and clean and checked and charged for the last thing left to do.
