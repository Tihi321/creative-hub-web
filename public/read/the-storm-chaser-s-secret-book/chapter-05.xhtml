The Crew in the Wheat

The section line ran east into the storm's flank and the tar gave out at the first fence and became dirt, and the dirt was the color of the wheat stubble on either side, and Wren drove it at fifty because the convoy had driven it at sixty and the ruts were fresh and deep.

Odell had the map on his knees and his thumb on the section corner. "Two miles and there's a lease road north," he said. "If the strip's east of Pella they'll stage south of it, in the field, where the wheat's been cut."

"How do you know it's cut?"

"Because it's August and this is wheat country and the co-op cut it three weeks ago," he said. "And because a seeding crew does not park four trucks in standing crop. They park in stubble. Stubble's flat and open and nobody owns it in August except the bank."

They came over the rise and the staging ground was already there the way a thing is there when it has been there for days. A cut field west of the strip, three hundred yards of stubble gone pale in the afternoon, and along the fence line four dark trucks with their boxes open, a flatbed trailer with the seeding racks folded down, a generator running behind a windbreak of hay bales and humming the low dull hum a generator hums when it has been on since Tuesday, and a fuel bladder staked to the dirt with a hose coiled over it.

Wren counted nine people, then eleven, then stopped, because two of them were walking the rack with a hose and one of them was crouched at the generator and one of them had a clipboard and was standing at the edge of the field with her back to the trucks, facing the storm.

Wren stopped her truck at the fence and did not cross it.

The woman at the edge of the field turned around when the engine died, and she looked at the rig on the rack first, and then at Wren, and she walked the whole hundred yards across the stubble with the clipboard under her arm and stopped on the other side of the fence. She did not put a hand on it.

"Wren Kalisz," she said.

Wren did not ask how. She had the laptop open on the seat beside her and the rig head still clamped to the rack and the strip from the tower in her back pocket and the fold of new pages in her notebook, and she had been seen coming up this road for two miles, and any crew that stocked a shelter by the flat since Monday had been watching this road since Monday. She asked the thing that mattered.

"Vance?"

The woman looked at her for a second, and then did something with her face that was not quite a smile and was close enough.

"Marisol," she said. "And yes, before you ask, and yes, this is the only one, and yes, we filed, and no, the filing does not cover what we're doing out here. Come through the gate. There's coffee."

"I'm not coming through the gate."

"Then I'll come through it."

She came through it. She had the clipboard and a jacket tied at her waist and her hair back, and she was in her forties and she moved like a person who had spent a career walking toward things she did not want to look away from, and she stood on the road side of the fence and folded her arms and waited, and the waiting was the thing that made Wren's hand want to go to her notebook.

"You've been east of Pella all week," Wren said. "You stocked the shelter Monday. You had county emergency management in town Tuesday, before the models showed anything. You have a plane on that strip and a rack of flares on that trailer, and your load is in the boundary layer, and I've had it on two instruments since Sunday."

Vance listened to all of it without moving.

"Which instrument?" she said.

"The clear-air tracer signature off my differential array," Wren said, "and a second one twenty miles west, four days apart, same isotope set, same size distribution. I'll give you the load in numbers if you want to write them down."

"I don't need to write them down," Vance said. "They're my numbers. I wrote the flight schedule that produced them."

She said it the way a person said the date.

Odell had come around the truck and stood at the fence with his hands in his pockets and had not said anything yet, and Vance looked at him for the first time and did not offer a hand.

"Brandt," she said.

"Marisol."

"You're the one who wrote the report that closed the program."

"I'm the one who wrote the report that closed the program."

"And now you're here, and you've brought a witness, and you think a witness changes the arithmetic." She looked back at Wren. "You think I'm going to deny it. I'm not going to deny it. That's the part everybody gets wrong about this. I'm not going to stand in a field and tell a sixteen-year-old that water's dry. I'll tell you the truth, and I'll tell you why, and then you'll decide what you decide, and you'll do it with your own numbers, which are good, by the way. Are they yours? The rig?"

"Yes."

"The differential's yours?"

"The whole rig."

"Then you built a better instrument than the state had in nineteen-seventy-nine," Vance said, and it was said flatly, as a fact, and it landed in Wren like a fact, and she did the thing she always did with a fact, which was take it and hold it and not spend it yet.

"You're steering it away from Pella," Wren said.

"Yes."

"Away from Pella and toward open country."

"Eventually."

"Say the second part."

Vance did not look away. Behind her, the rest of the crew had stopped what they were doing. The one at the generator had stood up. The storm was behind Vance's shoulder now, filling the whole east, the base stitched and the green under the edge gone dark, and the anvil was already west of them, which meant it was already west of Pella, which meant it was already over the flats and the road home.

"Pella has nine hundred people and one shelter and one road out," Vance said. "The Flats has nine hundred and one shelter and three roads and a co-op with a basement that holds everybody. The storm's on a track that intersects one of us. It's going to intersect one of us. The question is which of us. I have twenty-two flare passes left and a schedule to put them where the inflow will take them, and if the array holds, the track comes off Pella and goes west across the grass and out the other side of the county, and no one dies."

"And if it comes off Pella and the track goes west and the grass is empty until it's not, because my town is eleven miles further west than your model thinks this county ends."

"Then I'm saving nine hundred people to lose nine hundred people," Vance said. "And I'm going to do it anyway."

She said it kindly. That was the part. She said all of it kindly, in the voice of a woman explaining a thing to a colleague, laying out her arithmetic and her confidence as if the arithmetic were a favorite tool she was proud to show you, and the kindness did not waver, and Wren stood in the road with the fence between them and felt the thing happen in her chest that happens when someone is not quite lying.

"You don't get to call it open country," Wren said, "when there's a town on the far end of it."

"I get to call it the direction the flare matrix will take," Vance said. "The town's a consequence of the direction. It's not the direction."

"It's the direction if it's the direction."

"Yes," Vance said. "It is. Are you going to stop me, or are you going to take your sample?"

There was a boy at the fuel bladder.

Wren had counted him the first time without really seeing him, because he had been bent over the pump head with his back to the road and his coveralls dark with grease to the hip. Now he straightened up and looked at the fence. He was about the age of a senior in high school and a year past, nineteen maybe, and he had a wrench in his hand and grease on his forearms, and he met Wren's eyes across three hundred feet of stubble and held them for two seconds and then looked away.

Wren took the reading. It was not a weather reading, and she filed it anyway.

"Why are you telling us this?" Odell said.

Vance looked at him. "Because you know the program and you'll check the plan against the old one, and you'll conclude what I concluded, which is that the old matrix under-flared past the flank and that's what killed your town and not the seeding itself, and then you'll either help me or you won't, and either way I don't have to worry that you didn't understand."

"Nineteen-seventy-nine the seeding was the storm's feed, not its fence," Odell said. "We know that now."

"We do."

"And you're feeding this one."

"I'm feeding the base. Not the updraft."

"Those are the same things," Wren said, and both of them looked at her. She had the laptop open on the truck hood and the rig head up and was already copying the schedule off the rack — how many flares, at what altitude, on what heading, from the array numbers visible on the trailer's placard.

"They're the same thing if the timing's wrong," Vance said. "They're the fence if the timing's right. That's the entire point of a schedule."

"Then prove it," Wren said. "You have a load on the board and a plane in the air and a plan. I have the other half of your numbers. Give me the flight line and let me run the isotope comparison against the inflow, and if your array is a fence, the comparison will show it and I'll put it in the notebook and I'll sign it, and you can tell the state I said so."

Vance was quiet for a second, which was the first time she had been quiet.

"You want the flight line," she said.

"I want the flight line. And I want to take a sample off your own rack while it's loaded, so there's no argument about what I measured."

One of the crew started to say something behind her and Vance stopped him with a hand, not looking back, and the hand was the only quick thing she had done.

"Fair," she said. "You'll take it from the rack, you'll take it in front of me, and you'll take it while the plane's on approach, so the comparison isn't a comparison of two different flights." She raised her voice, not much. "Tobin. Bring the number six rack head and a fresh filter."

The boy with the wrench looked up, and looked at Wren again, and this time he did not look away for a beat longer than he needed to, and Wren would remember that beat later and not know yet what it was.

He came across the stubble with the rack head in both hands and a face like a person who had been deciding something for three days and had not finished.

Wren took the sample at the rack, on the trailer, in front of Vance, at four-forty, with the plane reporting a two-mile final on Vance's handheld and the generator humming under everything.

She uncapped her filter housing and seated the media and ran the pump off the truck battery for six minutes, and the readout on the strip went up the way it had gone up east of Pella and the way it had gone up on the tower roof, and the peak was the same peak, and she wrote it down and timestamped it and let Vance initial the page, which Vance did, in a small firm hand, without comment.

The plane came over the field low, left to right, and dumped a pass into the base of the updraft three miles east, and the base of the updraft did a thing that Wren had never seen and did not have a word for. It pulled the pass in and got deeper, not looser, the whole wall of it tightening around the silver the way a fist closes around a coin.

Wren watched it and did not say it.

Odell wrote it down too, on the back of the map, in pencil, one line, and did not show it to her and did not show it to Vance.

Vance let them go at five-fifteen, off the field and up the lease road, because she said it plainly: the next pass was at seven-forty and she did not want a witness watching the launch with a notebook in her hand. She said it to Wren directly, as a colleague, and told her where the plane would be at nine and what heading it would fly and what she expected the array to do.

"You have two hours on the road before the next one," she said. "You'll have your pump running by six-fifteen and your comparison by eight. That's how I'd do it. Which means it's how you'll do it."

She went back across the stubble to the trucks and did not look back.

Tobin was at the trailer with the rack head, closing it up, and when Wren got in the truck he was still at the trailer and his hand had stopped on the latch and he was looking at the road, not at her, and she started the engine and let it be.

They drove west and did not talk for four miles.

"You could have called the sheriff from the field," Odell said, finally. "You had a name and a rack and a plan on a placard."

"With four trucks and eleven people and the plane in the air," Wren said. "And the deputy comes out from the county in a half hour and they're gone in ten, and the only thing left in the field is my word and your word and a filter I took from their own rack, which is exactly why I took it from their own rack, and it'll be in the notebook in my hand no matter which way this goes." She checked the mirror. "And because the comparison's the thing."

Odell did not answer that.

They set the pump on the tailgate in a stubble field west of Pella, past the last fence before the county line, where the storm's inflow would pass over them before it reached anything built. Wren had the rig head on the pole and the pump running and the laptop closed on the seat, and she had the tower strip and the rack strip and the field strip side by side in the notebook, and she had the plane's schedule in her right hand in her own shorthand, and she sat in the dirt with her back against the truck tire and watched the sky arrive.

The storm came west all evening. The anvil went over them at seven and the shelf went over at eight, and the rain held off, because the storm was feeding and the feed came from the south, and the south was where the inflow was and the inflow was where they had put the rig. The plane made its pass at seven-forty off the east and again at nine off the northeast, and at ten to ten Odell went to the truck and came back with the thermos and neither of them drank any of it.

At ten past ten Wren ran the comparison.

She ran it on the laptop off the pump logger with the strip pinned under the keyboard so she could look at both, and the software was rough and the software was hers, and she stood in the wind at the tailgate with Odell beside her and the screen lighting the two of them, and the comparison ran and stopped and did not need the second run.

She read it out loud, because she did not trust her own eyes to say it silently.

"Silver burden in the inflow is up twenty-four percent on the pass. Not down. Up." She scrolled. "And the updraft velocity derivative — Odell, the derivative is positive over the same six minutes. The seeding is in the feed. The base is ingesting the silver like a nutrient. It's making her taller. It's making her hold." She looked at the next page, and the next, and the radar trace she had pulled off the tower feed at seven, and she did not need the third read.

She put her finger on the storm's track and ran it forward.

Odell looked where her finger was and did not move.

The track had been east of Pella at four. It was over Pella at nine. It was west of Pella at ten-fifteen and pointed at the county line, and at the county line the track bent, and the bend was not a bend. It was a heading. Eleven miles down a heading of two-five-zero was the co-op elevator in Coyote Flats, and the storm was already on the heading, and it was on it early.

"She's not steering it away from Pella," Wren said. "She's aiming it."

Odell said nothing.

"The schedule's not a fence. It's a sight," Wren said. She closed the laptop and stood in the wind with the strip in her hand and the four pieces of paper in the notebook and the number written small in her right hand in the corner of the page, where the readings went. "And it's aimed home."

She started the truck and turned the wheel west into the dark, and behind them the storm went on feeding, and the feed was her town.
