The Choice in Pella

The county line road ran east out of the draw into country that had gone flat and blind, and Wren drove it at forty because forty was as fast as the wipers would let her see. The rain came off the wall cloud in curtains now, one behind another, and the road showed itself between them in pieces: a mile of gravel, a cattle guard, a culvert, an intersection where a white sign on a leaning post said PELLA 6 and nothing else.

She had the schedule on her knees and Tobin between her and the door, and Odell in the back with the crate because the bench was full, and the display in the bed was dead and the housing was under the mat and the new stack sat in its cradle like a thing asleep.

"Two o'clock run," Tobin said. He had the map light on the schedule and he read it with his thumb under the line. "She flies out of the strip and runs the pass north up the inflow. The strip's behind us now."

"Then the pass is ahead of us," Wren said.

They came over a rise and the country opened and the storm was there on the right, not behind and not over but alongside, running parallel to the road like a train on a track laid north-south, and Wren saw for the first time the whole of what Vance had built. The wall cloud had gone low and smooth and it was no longer a shelf. It was a collar, a ring around a base that had begun to lower, and under the collar the rain fell in a column that was almost still, and the whole assembly moved west at the speed of the road, fifteen, twenty, and the lightning finally started. It started in the anvil, out of the top where the ice was, and walked down the outside of the column in long steps, and the thunder came a count of four behind the light and then a count of two and then no count at all, thunder without interval, a sound like a floor being built overhead.

Wren slowed to thirty.

At the intersection where the sign said PELLA 6, a truck was parked across the road.

It was one of theirs. A dark crew truck with the box behind the cab, parked broadside, nose west and tailgate east, lights on and flashers off, and its driver was out of it, standing on the far side, waiting. The road was a county road with a ditch on either side and a fence beyond each ditch and no other way through for eleven miles, and Wren measured the gap in one look the way she measured everything: four feet at the tailgate, soft shoulder, wheat.

She stopped a hundred feet short.

"Vance," Tobin said.

"I see her."

Marisol Vance came around the tailgate of the crew truck and stood in the road with the rain running off the shoulders of her jacket. She did not wave and did not advance and did not go back to her vehicle. She stood where the two roads met, at the center of the intersection, lit from behind by her own taillights and from above by the anvil, and she waited the way she had waited at the fence, arms folded, weight on one leg, a person who had spent a career walking toward things.

Wren put the truck in park, left it running, and rolled her window down.

Vance walked to the window and stood at a distance that was too close for a stranger and about right for a colleague, and the rain ran down her face and she did not wipe it.

"You fired at the draw," Vance said.

"Yes."

"On the clear-air rig, in the rain, without the ground test."

"Whoever's on the plane called it in."

"The pump truck saw the arc." Vance looked past Wren into the cab, at Tobin, and at Odell in the back, and she did not react to either of them. "You're lucky the stake held. If the ground had floated you'd have put the whole leader up through the cab."

"I know what I did wrong," Wren said. "I'll say it back to you if you want. Ground test at the third stake. Fire in the gap. I've got the stack replaced and the trigger reseated and the continuity run four times, and the rig's in the bed under the mat, and it's going north with me whether you block this road or not."

"Where north?"

"Into the notch west of your pass line. Where the inflow turns vertical, in the gap between two and four-thirty, which is the gap in your schedule and you know it is."

Vance stood in the rain for a moment with that, and then she did something Wren had not planned for. She nodded.

"Good window," Vance said. "If the draw funnels the way it did Sunday, that's the best ten minutes on this road tonight." She looked at the storm. "You've read the shear off the tower, you've got the inflow vector, and you've got Odell, who wrote the matrix I'm flying. So let's not do the part where I pretend you don't know and you pretend I'm a fool." She put one hand on the window sill, which was a gesture Wren did not like and did not stop. "The part where we do something is the part where you tell me what you want."

"I want you to stop seeding."

"The storm is at the county line and the county line is six miles west of Pella," Vance said. "Do you know what's under the base right now?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the point." She pointed north with two fingers, at the collar, the lowering base, the column of rain that hung almost still. "Two hours ago that was aimed at Pella on a track my matrix calls for and my schedule funds. The seed went into the flank at the height Odell designed and the flank deepened and the whole storm leaned, and the lean is two-five-zero, and two-five-zero out of that formation point is not Pella. It's the flats. It is your town and your elevator and your mother's house, and I know it is, and I did it anyway, and I did it because the alternative was Pella, and Pella has nine hundred people, one shelter, and one road out, and the road out is the road we're both standing on, and if this storm comes up that road Pella is thirty years of nobody and one church basement and a shelter board that stocks water by the flat, and half of them won't leave, because the ones who leave already left."

"So you chose."

"I chose. And I'd choose it again right now, and I want you to understand that I hate that I'd choose it again right now, because that's the part you're here for." She wiped her face with the back of her hand and the rain put it back. "The flats have three roads and a co-op with a basement that holds everybody and a mother who has a truck and a daughter who reads the sky. That's the math. I've done it a hundred times. It's the only math there is."

Wren reached over Tobin and took the notebook off the dash and opened it to the strip and the numbers, and she held it out the window and the rain hit the page and smeared the pencil and she held it anyway.

"Your math is right and your numbers are wrong," Wren said. "Here's the isotope comparison from ten past ten. I took it off your own number six rack head with your initials on it, four minutes before your seven-forty pass, and I ran it against the field sample from Sunday on the same instrument, same filter, same size distribution. Silver burden in the inflow up twenty-four percent after the pass. Not down. Up. Your seed is not fencing the flank and it is not leaning the storm away from Pella as a fence. It's going into the column and the column is eating it and getting taller, and the taller it gets the more it leans, and the lean is a heading and a heading is not a choice and you cannot aim a heading. You can only choose who's under it."

Vance took the notebook.

She read it in the rain with the taillights on the page, without hurry, her lips moving on the numbers, and when she got to the comparison she stopped and went back and read it a second time, and then she handed the notebook back through the window.

"That's Odell's fault line," she said. "The flank flare at boundary height. He told you."

"He told me."

"He wrote it up inconclusive."

"He wrote it up inconclusive." Wren closed the notebook. "And you found the fault in his matrix and you kept flying it anyway, and that's not a defense, that's a confession."

Vance looked at Odell in the back seat for the first time and said, "You could have called."

"I called," Odell said. "In 2009 and in 2014 and three months ago. You stopped taking the calls."

"I stopped taking the calls because you stopped believing in the work."

"I stopped believing in the work when it killed six people in Baird," Odell said.

The rain came down on the roof of the truck and ran off the windshield in a standing sheet, and in the north the lowering base had begun to turn, slow and definite, the whole collar rotating around a point that was not yet a funnel and was deciding whether to be one, and the lightning walked down the outside of the column in a rhythm now, one-one-two, one-one-two, the anvil strobing so that the wheat on both sides of the road showed and hid and showed.

Wren watched the base instead of Vance, because the base was the thing that was true, and because the base was doing exactly what the isotope comparison said it would do: it was tightening. The rain column under the collar had narrowed in the eight minutes they had been stopped. The rotation had come down out of the shelf and into the base. Somewhere inside that dark, a wall cloud was growing a tail.

The radio in Vance's truck crackled.

It was a loud radio on the dashboard with a fist mic on a coiled cord, and the mic had been left keyed to the wrong channel, which is why they all heard it. It was a woman's voice, calm and county and slightly bored, the voice of someone who had been reading a spill call off a clipboard since midnight.

"—all units, shelter status. Pella south shelter at capacity, repeat, Pella south at capacity, we have one hundred ten in and we are turning people to the school and the school has no generator. Repeat, Pella south shelter is full and we are turning people. Do you copy, east unit?"

There was a pause with the carrier open, and then another voice, younger, and it was not bored.

"Copy, dispatch, we're turning them to the school. We need somebody at the highway junction, we have two cars stacked and people walking."

"We'll send the deputy."

"Dispatch, we're going to need more than the deputy. It's raining sideways on the junction and it's dark and we have older folks out there."

"We'll do what we can, east unit."

The carrier stayed open for a second and then clicked off, and the rain came down on the two trucks and the radio was silent and the base turned.

Vance had not moved. She had her hand on the window sill and her face turned toward the intersection where her truck was parked, and she was looking east, down the road she had come up, toward the town that was six miles behind her and full of water.

Wren did not say anything.

She wanted to. She had the sentence ready — your math just made a decision for you, and it's my town, and you can't unmake it by standing in this intersection — and she did not say it, because Vance's face was doing the thing Odell's face did at the fence and at the tailgate, going still while it decided something it had already decided, and Wren had learned in four days that the still face was the only part of these people that was ever going to be true.

Vance looked at the storm. Then she looked east. Then she looked at the storm, and the storm's base was lower and narrower and closer to a tail than it had been two minutes before, and the rain under it had gone from a column to a rope.

"Dispatch will not move them fast enough," Vance said. "The shelter board will not open the school, because the school has no generator and no water and it's a liability, and the board will stand on the shelter door and argue while people stand in the rain, and the junction will stack three more cars and the deputy will get there and there'll be nobody to read the sky." She said it flatly, reading it off the radio the way Odell read a log. "The storm is twenty minutes from the junction on the two-five-zero heading and the junction is the road out of Pella."

"It's forty minutes from the flats," Wren said.

"I know how far it is from the flats. I know where it is to the minute. I've been reading it off my own matrix all night and my matrix is a liar, and I know that now, and it doesn't change where the storm is." She took her hand off the window sill and stood back from the truck. "I have nineteen flare passes left. Nineteen. If I put them in the flank they deepen it and it comes west. If I put them up the column it does what your comparison says it does and it deepens the column and it comes west faster. There is no pass that turns it. There is no pass that saves anybody. There is only a pass that decides who gets twenty minutes."

She looked east again, down the road to Pella, and then she looked north at the base, and the order she looked in was the answer and everybody in the truck saw it.

"I'm going to seed the junction," Vance said.

Tobin said, from the middle of the bench, quiet, "That's east. That puts the flank over the junction and the junction's upstream of the shelter."

Vance said, "Yes."

"That feeds it east."

"Vance," Odell said from the back, and then did not finish it.

"I know what it does." Vance was already walking back to her truck. "It puts a flank over the junction and the junction is the road and the road is where the people are, and the flank will put down rain and cut the lightning and buy the deputy thirty minutes to move them to the school, and then the storm will take the seed out of the flank and put it back up the column and eat it, and the lean will go west, and the flats will take what the flats take, and I'll have chosen." She stopped at her door. "That's the last time I do the math out loud. Get off the road."

She got in her truck and started it and pulled it off the intersection, nose west, tailgate east, out of the way, and she sat there with the engine running and the wipers going and she did not look at Wren.

Wren put the truck in gear and drove through the intersection and past Vance's window, and Vance did not roll it down, and Wren did not stop. Tobin had the schedule and the map and he was already working the next gap with his thumb. Odell was in the back with the crate and he was watching Vance in the mirror, and Wren watched her too, in the mirror, until the road bent north and the intersection was behind them and the rain took it.

"Two o'clock run is off the board," Tobin said.

"Why?"

"Because she's getting on it." He held up the schedule. "That's her plane. She put it up when she walked back to the truck. The run's re-tasked east, and she's flying it herself."

Wren looked north, where the base was turning west.

"Then the gap opens early," she said. "She's spending her pass east and the column's going to be hungry and the next gap is ours. Rig in the bed, stake in the ground, third stake, and we fire when the notch comes over the fence line, and we do not fire into the second curtain. If she wants to buy the junction thirty minutes, that's what she buys and it's for her town, and it doesn't change the heading and it doesn't change the instrument. That's the shot. There's no other shot after it, so that's the one we take."

She said it out loud because saying it out loud made it a thing she had chosen and could not take back, and then she took the county road north at forty-five and the wipers were losing and she did not slow.

They came up the long grade out of the county line and the notch opened ahead of them where the draw cut the fence, and the lights of the seeding plane swung east behind them and climbed into the flank of the storm with its landing light on, and somewhere in the dark above the wheat a woman was putting nineteen flare passes into the road out of Pella, buying her town half an hour with a matrix that did not work. Wren drove north with the storm on her right and the stake unloaded and the tether coiled and charged, and the road ahead ran straight into the gap in the schedule where the storm had left a door open for exactly ten minutes, and the base went on turning in the dark over the road home, and the tail of it came down.
