What the Models Say

The co-op office at six in the morning was the coldest room in Coyote Flats. Wren had learned that at twelve, when her mother first let her open the north bay alone, and she had learned it again at sixteen, sitting at the desk with her hands wrapped around a mug of the bad coffee Odell had brought in a steel thermos instead. Outside the window the town was still grey. The streetlight on Depot Road was still on. Somewhere east the dry cell from last night had died and the ground had cooled and the wheat had gone back to being wheat.

Odell put his glasses on and laid her logs out across the desk.

He did it like a man dealing cards. He did not stack them, did not sort them, just spread the printed pages edge to edge until the desk was covered and the fluorescent light lay flat across the columns. Wren had printed them out on the co-op's dot-matrix printer, because she did not own a printer and the county library charged eleven cents a page and she had better things to spend eleven cents on, and so every number she had ever collected was on tractor-feed paper with perforated edges, and Odell Brandt looked at the perforations first, and then at the numbers, in that order, the same way he had looked at her rig the night before.

He read for an hour. He read the way a musician reads a score — not line by line but in groups, three or four pages at once, the whole shape of the thing coming off the paper into the air between them. Every dozen pages he would stop and tap a column and say a thing out loud. The humidity sensor ran two degrees warm in the afternoon because the aluminum spine held heat; whitewash the spine and reflect it. The charge amplifier had a cold solder joint under the second stage and it would bite her when there was rain in the airstream. Her May runs were clean; everything before May was calibration and did not count. She wrote each one down in the corner of the day's page, in her right hand, small.

At ten fifteen he cleared the desk.

"Paper charts," he said.

"Do people still use those?"

"I use those." He went out to the truck and came back with a cardboard tube and unrolled a USGS quad sheet across the whole desk. He set a roll of drafting tape on the corner and produced a pencil from his shirt pocket. "You've got the grid, I got the map. You read me the numbers and I'll write them down."

They worked the storm the way cartographers worked the coast. Wren called the forecast parameters off her notebook, fast, and Odell wrote each one at the point on the map where it mattered, in a small engineer's hand that set the dry line in red and the shear axis in blue and the low in a firm black circle south of the panhandle line. The blank quad sheet filled up east to west. A formation point east of Pella, at the junction where the dry line would set up on Thursday. A track arrow, drawn slow and deliberate, aimed west across the county road, across the wheat, across the whole of Coyote Flats and out the other side.

Wren drew the town as a rectangle by hand, because the quad sheet did not show Coyote Flats at all, only the fields, and she felt the town needed a rectangle.

"Formation," she said, tapping the point east of Pella. "Two, three in the afternoon. It'll build fast. Shear's already there and the moisture's coming back up from the gulf behind the backdoor front. Mature by six."

"Mature by six." Odell wrote 1800.

"Seven hours of mature storm, if she holds."

Odell drew a thin arc across the track from the formation point to the rope-out and labeled the arc with the word WINDOW, because he was a man who labeled things.

Her mother came to the doorway at noon with two plates balanced on one wrist, and did not come in.

"Wren," she said.

"We're almost done."

"You are not almost anything. You're shipping your rig around a county map." Her mother set the plates on the counting table and did not sit. "Mr. Brandt, I need to say something to my daughter."

Odell stood up politely.

"Sit," her mother said. He sat. She looked at Wren for a long moment. "The supercell on Thursday. It comes west over us. You mapped it. I saw."

"It's a forecast. Forecasts are wrong. That's why I built the rig."

"Oh, Wren." Her mother rubbed her thumb against the edge of the plate, round and round. "The last time a storm was forecast to come over us and didn't, you came home and told me the forecast was wrong and the rig would fix it. Twelve times, maybe, I've heard the same sentence in this room." She looked at Odell. "You worked the tower."

"I did."

"Then you know what I mean." She turned back to Wren. "It's not the storm I'm scared of. It's the year you get it wrong and you're not in this kitchen when it does."

The diesel engine rolled out in the bay. Somebody laughed and Wren recognized the laugh and could not place it and did not want to.

"I'll be careful," Wren said, which was what she always said.

Her mother stood a moment longer, and then she went back out, taking the plates with her, because her mother had never learned to yell and had never learned to cry in front of her, and so she left, and that was the whole conversation, and it had gone the way it always went.

Odell looked at the door for a second after she was gone. Then he picked up the pencil and said, in a voice that did not ask anything of her, "Where were we."

"Where I was," Wren said, "was seeding."

It was an idle word, at the desk, with the maps open. She had read the papers; she had read about the old experiments, the hail-suppression programs and the flare lines and the silver iodide and the way they had all been judged inconclusive and closed. It was a word you used the way you used perigee, to show you had read the thing.

Odell's pencil stopped on the paper.

She saw it a half-second late, which for Wren was a long time, and by then his hand was moving again and the line was clean, and the whole image of what she had seen was still behind her eyes. The pencil had stopped on the word WINDOW. Exactly on the W.

"I've read the seeding papers," she said, evenly. "The hail ones. Inconclusive. That's the word the whole literature uses. Inconclusive."

"Inconclusive," Odell agreed, and drew the line out the other side of the W.

"They stopped doing them in the eighties, mostly."

"Mostly." He rolled the pencil between his fingers and set it down. "You're asking whether I was in them."

"I wasn't asking anything."

"You were asking without asking," Odell said, and smiled a little. "Which is the right way to ask a stranger. Keep doing it." He looked down at the map. "A different question I'd rather you asked me was whether the low stays south of the panhandle line on Thursday, because if it doesn't, your formation point moves twenty miles north and Pella catches the first half."

Wren looked at the map. The low was there, a firm black circle drawn by a man who had drawn a thousand of them. She looked from the low to the formation point to the town rectangle she had drawn for Coyote Flats, and then up at Odell.

"Fine," she said, and picked up the ruler.

She filed the flinch away. She did not know what was in the file. She only knew that a man who had been to the tower and the weather and forty years of the world had stopped his pencil at that word and no other, and Wren had spent two years learning not to throw away a reading, and so she put it where she put readings, in the corner of the page, in her right hand, small.

When the chart was done she rolled it up and handed it over, and Odell slid it into the cardboard tube and drove off west toward the motel, and Wren stayed at the desk with the logs spread out and the cold coffee and did not read the four words in the corner of the map. She knew what they said. She had written it as a reading, not a question, because she did not yet know what she was asking.

Out the window, east of town over Pella, the sky was clear and the dry line was still four hundred miles away, and it was going to be four days before anything at all happened there.

None of it showed on the glass.
