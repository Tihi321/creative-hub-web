Silver in the Airstream

They left Coyote Flats at seven-forty in Odell's truck with the rig in its plywood case on the back seat and a five-gallon can of gas lashed in the bed. They took the county road east because the highway went north and looped, and the county road went straight at the storm.

Wren had the laptop open on her knees and the national radar loop running, and the loop was the same thing it had been at six and the same thing it had been at five. A line of towers going up on the dry line eighty miles east, small, growing, the whole line sliding west at eighteen knots while the cells on the southern end leaned and breathed and got their act together. The models had put the formation point east of Pella and the models were not wrong, because the models had never been wrong about where a storm would start. They were wrong about what it would do after. Anybody could see a storm coming. The county had been seeing storms come for as long as anyone had kept a record, and the shelf of land out here had been getting out of their way since long before there was a county at all. What the county could not see was what a storm would choose to do when it got here, because storms did not choose, and that was the part Wren had spent two years trying to pin down with numbers.

"You're watching the loop again," Odell said.

"I'm watching the loop again."

"Watch the low instead. The loop's already told you what it's going to tell you. The low hasn't."

She looked at the surface map. The low was a fat comma of pressure south of the panhandle line, exactly where they'd drawn it on the quad sheet, and it was nudging north. Six hours ago it had been south of the line by thirty miles. Now it was south of the line by twenty, and twenty was still south, and twenty was drifting.

"Low's north of forecast," she said.

"Say it like you mean it."

"Low's north of forecast." She looked up from the screen. "If it comes north of the line before the towers go up, Pella catches the first half."

Odell drove with his forearm on the sill and the wind coming in off the wheat, and the wheat was standing straight all the way to the horizon. Above the wheat the east sky did the thing the east sky did on a chase morning, which was nothing at all, which was a blue so clean it looked painted, which was the sky over a formation that had not happened yet.

"That's the thing about this county," Odell said. "The horizon shows you weather an hour before it arrives, and it shows it to you wrong. You see the towers and you think you have an hour. You have eleven minutes. The whole county lives on eleven minutes and calls it an hour."

They passed the first wheat town at eight-fifteen, a name on a water tower and a co-op and fifty houses and a school that had been closed since before Wren was born. Then the second town, smaller, a crossroads with a church and a gas pump that took cards only. Then nothing for twenty miles but the road and the wheat and the fence posts going by like the ticks on a ruler, every ten miles and every mile and every quarter. Wren counted the posts because she counted everything, and Odell let her, because Odell counted things too. He counted the mile markers under his breath and stopped when he noticed he was doing it.

Pella came up over a low rise at nine-forty, and Wren had seen it on the quad sheet as a rectangle and on the county map as a name and had never in her life come to it from the west.

It was a town the way a tooth is a tooth after it's been pulled. The elevator stood on the north side of the tracks and the elevator was the only thing in Pella taller than a house. The houses were there and the street was there and the street was tarred and the tar had cracked and been patched and cracked again. The whole town had the look of a place that had been fine and then had stopped being fine. It had decided, quietly, to keep the lights on anyway.

Odell slowed to fifteen. It was the kind of fifteen you drove when you were not sure you were welcome.

The shelter was on the south side of the tracks, a low concrete building with a steel door and a concrete apron and a sign that said EMERGENCY SHELTER in yellow letters with a date plate under it, 1962. The door was open, and two men were carrying flats of bottled water in through it and stacking them inside where Wren could see them.

She counted the flats. Six in, and six more on the apron, and a woman in the door writing on a clipboard, and a hand truck leaning against the wall with the paint worn off the plate.

"They opened it already," Wren said.

"They opened it Monday," Odell said.

"How do you know?"

"Because they're stocking water by the flat and not by the case," he said. "Case means today. Flat means the third day. People don't plan in flats unless they've been planning a while."

They parked in front of the diner because there was nowhere to park but in front of the diner, and the diner was open and had a hand-lettered sign in the window with the day's hours and a second sign below it that said STORM LINE OPEN — SHELTER IS OPEN — WATER IS FREE.

The diner was four booths and a counter and a pie case with two pies in it. The owner was a woman in her sixties with her hair pinned up and a rag over her shoulder, and she came out from behind the counter and filled two cups before she asked what they wanted, because that was how it worked in a town where the only customers of the day were the kind who came in off the county road.

"You're chasing," she said. It was not a question.

"Working the radar," Odell said. "We came over from the flats."

The woman looked at Wren's rig case on the floor of the booth, and then at Wren, and then at Odell, and the looking took its time.

"You're from the flats," she said. "Coyote."

"Wren Kalisz," Wren said.

The woman's face did something small. "Kalisz. There was a Kalisz girl. Two years back."

She had the cup halfway to her mouth and she did not put it down and she did not drink it. Wren took the reading the way she took readings and gave nothing back, and Odell, beside her, put both hands around his cup and let the silence sit, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for Wren in a long time.

"My sister," Wren said. "She was driving home from here."

"From the elevator," the woman said. "She used to pick up sample sieves for your dad's co-op. June. June Kalisz. Everybody here knew June." She set the cup down. "We knew it was her before the county called. My husband ran the wrecker that week. He said the sky stood still over the west road and he knew and he didn't say it out loud until he got home."

Wren put the cup down too. Her hand was steady and she noticed it was steady, and she noted the noting.

"Three days," Wren said. "You've been stocked for three days. The models didn't show a supercell three days ago. The models showed scattered cells until Wednesday night."

The woman looked at her for a second.

"Radio," she said. "And the boys from the county came through and talked to the shelter board and said be ready, and the shelter board put it up on the church sign and the church sign's our whole newspaper. We don't wait for the models out here, hon. We wait for the county to tell us." She picked up the rag. "And the county came through Tuesday."

Tuesday. Wren did the math in her head and the math was a problem: Tuesday was the day the models had still shown a dry line setting up west of the ridge, and the day before the four trucks had come up the ridge road, and the day before anything at all had been on the horizon over Pella but blue.

"Which county?" Wren said.

"Emergency management," the woman said. "Two fellas. Didn't know them."

Odell asked her, quietly, whether they'd left a number, and she said no, they hadn't left anything. He asked her what they drove and she said a pickup with a box on it and didn't know what kind. He thanked her and paid for both coffees and left the change in the tip jar, and Wren noticed the tip jar and noticed that he put the whole change in, and took the reading.

They set the rig on a dirt crossroad four miles east of Pella, where the county road crossed a section line and the section line went south into grazing and the county road went east into nothing. The nothing was the point.

The supercell was up on the dry line twenty miles east when they got the rig leveled on the tailgate. It was a single tower at first, raw, unorganized, the kind of cumulus that goes up and shears over and is gone by suppertime. And then it was not. The tower got a base under it and the base got a shelf on it and the shelf went rotating and the rotating went tight. In the space of about forty minutes the whole thing went from weather to a storm, which was the part Wren had come to watch, the part she had watched a hundred times in downloaded data and exactly twice in person.

The anvil was already spreading. It fanned out west with a low sheet under it, which was the overshoot hitting the tropopause and telling the whole eastern sky which way the storm was going to go. It was going west, the width of the county at Pella and straight on over Pella and out over the flats toward the ridge.

"She's leaning over us," Wren said.

"She's leaning over Pella," Odell said. "And the anvil's leaning over us. Two different things. Watch the anvil."

She watched the anvil. The anvil leaned over Pella. Wren did not say anything for a full minute, because the reading was a problem and the problem needed to be read.

She had the rig head clamped to the aluminum pole off the truck's ladder rack, elevated to eleven degrees, which was the angle she had learned from Odell over the tower. The logger was on the tailgate with a notebook on each side of it and a thermos wedged against the case so the wind didn't rattle it. She ran the differential every three minutes and wrote the number in the book. By twenty past ten the number had stopped being about the boundary layer and the wind and the sun and had started being about the storm.

The trace went up.

It went up the way a hand goes up. Not a squall line, not a gust front, not the ion hump of a dry thunderstorm cooking the air above it. It was a wave, and a peak, and a crest that kept cresting. The peak was not a fingerprint on the air. The peak was an aerosol load, and the mass distribution was wrong for every natural source she knew. It was right for one thing.

Wren put the pencil down. She got the strip from the back of the notebook, the folded strip from the tower roof, and laid it next to the new pages. She put her thumb on the peaks and lined them up, and the peaks were the same peaks. Two hours at the tower. Twenty miles east of the tower four days later. Same load. Same size distribution. And the new one was not a crest. The new one was rising, and it was rising as the storm leaned toward Pella, and the lean was the direction the anvil was going, which meant the seeding was not in the anvil. The seeding was in the inflow.

She had hidden the first strip. She had folded it once and slid it behind the July pages and told herself it was because a reading is a thing you hold, and that had been part of it and not all of it, and she had known the rest of it since the stairwell: he had told her a thing that had killed a town, and she had gone looking for the part he left out, and she had found it before he chose to give it, and you do not hand a man back his own buried crime on the first day he says the town's name. That was why she had hidden it. It had taken her until now, with the two strips lined up on the tailgate, to say the sentence even inside her own head. She had hidden it because some part of her had already decided that the man in the passenger seat might be a witness or might be something worse, and a reading was the only thing she had that he could not overwrite.

"Odell," she said.

He had been standing with his back to her, watching the storm the way you watched a thing that had been building since Tuesday.

"Give me the numbers first," he said.

"Silver iodide. Mass load above the boundary layer, consistent with an airborne source, size distribution narrow enough to rule out pyrotechnics on the ground." She said the number off the logger, and the number off the tower strip, and the difference between the peaks. "The tower was a pass. This is a job. There's a plane up there seeding the base of the updraft, and it's not one plane, because the load's too even for a single track and the pass has repeated twice." She held up the strip. "I've had this since the tower roof and I didn't show it to you."

Odell turned around.

He did not look surprised. He looked like a man at the bottom of a stairwell who has heard the door close above him and not the door close behind him.

"I know," he said.

Wren waited.

"I had it before I got to your co-op," he said. "A phone call, and a number on a screen, and a motive. I could not see the load. I can see the load now." He looked back at the storm and put his hands in his pockets. "It's the same isotope set as seventy-nine. Down to the isotopic ratio, as near as makes no difference. I brought the old numbers with me. I didn't bring them to show you. I brought them to check against."

She turned the second strip so the two peaks faced the same way, and she saw the flinch again, the pencil stopping on the W of WINDOW, and she named it for what it was. That had been the first reading she had ever taken of Odell Brandt, four days before this one, and it had come back the same as this one, in kind if not in degree.

"You came back to stop it," she said.

"I came back to stop it."

"You've known since before you drove up my driveway."

"I've known since some Tuesday of somebody's," Odell said, "and I drove up your driveway because I needed a reason to be in this county that the county would believe. A retired engineer, riding shotgun with a kid who writes him emails about radar." He met her eyes. "And I needed the kid, because the kid has the one rig in this county that can read a load the way it has to be read, and because I do not have a way to look at a storm anymore except through a machine, and my machine is dead on the ridge and yours is on your truck."

The anvil was spreading west. The shelf cloud had tightened and the base had gone green at the edge and the storm was doing the thing Wren had read about and had never seen. The lower half of it had begun to look stitched, the way a quilt looked stitched when the thread was pulled tight, and the tightness was in the direction of the road, and the road ran west.

"Who," Wren said. "I've told you what I've got. Now tell me what you've got, or tell me why you won't."

He was quiet for a moment, and the quiet was an answer Wren did not like.

"Because I don't have the name," he said. "I have a schedule and an operator and a filing that shouldn't exist, and I do not have the hand that put the filing in. If I give you a name I don't have, you'll call it in, and it'll be two people's word against four trucks and a signed state form, and one of the two will be a kid who sat on a reading for four days and never showed it to anybody." He said that last part without cruelty, which was worse. "If you want the name, we have to go get it. That means the strip, and the load, and a landing."

Wren took that and held it the way she held a reading. It was not an answer. It was a direction.

"Then we get more numbers," she said. "If there's a plane, there's a strip, and the county only has three strips that'll take a twin, and I can log the load on every pass until the plane has to land, and when the plane lands the paperwork is a fact, and a fact is a thing you can hand a sheriff."

Something moved behind Odell's eyes. It was not agreement yet. It was the beginning of the thing that comes before agreement, which was respect, and Wren did not have time to enjoy it, because the sky had changed.

The storm pulled the road up into it.

It was not coming west on its own yet. It was sitting over its own formation point and feeding, and the anvil was reaching, and the base was dropping, and between the base and the ground there was now a curtain of rain with a vertical edge on its south side. That edge was the storm's flank, and the flank was aimed at the section line.

Then the convoy came over the rise behind them from the west.

Wren heard it before she saw it, four engines loud and out of tune with each other, the third one missing a cylinder. She turned and there they were on the county road, the same four dark cabs, one through four stencilled small on the boxes, the same flatbed with the tarp and the same trailer with the coil of lay-flat hose. They came up the road at speed and did not slow for the crossroad where the rig was.

The lead truck passed within fifteen feet of the tailgate. It was going sixty on a tarred county road with the windows down and the driver had one hand on the wheel and the other on a radio mic clipped to the dash. In the second between the truck and the rig, Wren saw the box behind the cab had a door in it, and the door was open two inches, and what she could see of the load was steel canisters racked in foam.

The second truck passed. The third. The fourth, and the trailer with the coil, and the last trailer wheel threw a rock off the shoulder and it put a chip in Odell's windshield the size of a dime.

The convoy went east, into the storm's flank, toward the section line, and did not stop.

Wren picked up the logger and set it in its case. She unclamped the head and threw the mast down onto the rack and did not fold the covers.

"They're going in," she said.

"They're going to the strip," Odell said. "There's one east of Pella and it's the only one they can launch from."

"I know where it is."

"I know you do."

Wren latched the tailgate and got in the truck and started it, because she had the key in the ignition before Odell had walked around the hood.

He got in and pulled the door and looked at the windshield chip once and did not mention it.

"You understand what this is," he said. "You follow a convoy into a living storm with a rig on your rack and a notebook full of a reading, and you are not a chaser anymore. You're a witness."

Wren put the truck in gear and pulled out onto the tar and turned east, and the storm filled the windshield from the quarter to the quarter, and the green under the base was going the color of wheat at dusk. The odometer was ticking on the dash and Wren counted the ticks.

They went east at speed, and the storm leaned over Pella, and behind them the town held its water by the flat and waited for the county to tell it what to do next.
