The Tower That Talks

The ridge road was nine miles of gravel that the county graded twice a year whether it needed it or not. Odell drove it at twenty-five, because he said the washboard would eat the truck's shocks if you gave it the chance, and Wren believed him, because the truck was from Wyoming and had the rust to prove it had earned his respect.

They had left Coyote Flats before seven. She had told her mother she was taking the rig to the tower to calibrate against the old dish, which was true. Her mother had said fine in the voice that meant I know what you are doing and I am choosing not to fight it today, and Wren had taken the sandwich and gone.

The tower stood on the only hill in the county. Wren had seen it her whole life, a grey lattice column with a white radome on top that had gone the color of old teeth. She had never been up it. She had the specs and the 1977 maintenance reports and the schematic that had his name in the corner, and she had never put her hand on the steel.

Odell parked at the base and killed the engine and sat one second with both hands on the wheel.

"Built in seventy-seven," he said. "You know that."

"I know that."

"Nobody's regraded the fill under this pad since about ninety-five, so the south leg's out of plumb." He got out. "You can feel it in the stairwell going up, the way the treads pull you toward the rail. Walk the center line. And the lock's on the housing door, not the tower gate. The gate's been open since I was young and it will be open when I'm gone."

Wren took the rig off the back seat, the sensor head and the logger and the spare battery in its plywood case, and followed him to the base.

The tower was four legs and a lattice that went up eighty feet to the equipment shelter, and the radome above that. From the ground it looked small, the way a thing looks small when you have seen it from every road in the county and never stood under it. Up close the steel was the color of concrete and the bolts were rusted and the whole structure hummed in a way that was not wind, because there was no wind, because it was a still grey morning and the wheat was standing straight.

"You hear that," Odell said.

"I hear it."

"That's the dish." He put his hand flat on the nearest leg, the way you put a hand on an animal. "The old antenna on the roof of the shelter. It tracks when the wind's doing nothing at all. It hunts. The bearing's worn and the drive motor's got a dead band, and every so often it decides it's off azimuth and swings and comes back. You'll hear it every few minutes going up. Don't let it worry you." He looked up the lattice. "It worried me for eight years."

They went up.

The stairwell was a switchback bolted inside the tower, and Odell was right about the south leg. The treads pulled you toward the rail on every other flight, and Wren walked the center line and felt the pull anyway, and counted the flights, because counting was what she did when the floor wanted to move. Her boot rang on the grates. The rig case knocked against her hip. Below them the truck got smaller and the co-op's white elevator stood up over the town like a finger, and beyond the town the fields ran west in long grey-gold stripes, and the wheat went on and on and did not end.

On the third landing Odell stopped and put his hand on the rail.

"You're going to want to ask me about the program," he said.

Wren had not said anything. She had been counting. But he had read it off her, the way he read everything.

"I was going to ask about the program," she said.

"Let's get up first."

The equipment shelter was a steel room with a plywood floor, and it smelled of dust and hot metal and the ghost of cigarettes smoked in it forty years ago that had never quite left. Odell opened the door with a key off a ring he kept in his jacket and not on his belt, which Wren noticed. The console inside was a wreck of it. Two decades-old CRTs sat dead in their racks. A desk had been bolted to the deck and had swelled around the bolts. A logbook lay on the desk, and Wren opened it and found the last entry was 2009, in blue ballpoint, in a hand that slanted left.

"That's not yours," she said.

"Mine's above it. Find the 1979 entries."

She turned the pages back. The paper got browner and the ink got bluer and then got blacker, and somewhere in the middle, in a hand she already knew from the corner of a radar schematic, was his name at the top of a page. O. Brandt, 1400 CST.

"Fourteen hundred," she said.

"That's when the shift changed."

"You kept a log."

"You keep a log when you don't trust the machine," Odell said. "This machine, you didn't trust it on Tuesdays and you didn't trust it in July and you didn't trust it in any month with an R in it, and so you kept a paper log so that when it lied you'd know exactly when and how." He sat down on the edge of the desk like a man sitting on his own porch. "And Tuesday, in July, is the whole reason we're up here."

He walked her through the tower the way a man walks you through a house he grew up in and cannot live in.

The dish was a flat plate array on the shelter roof, and it had never been a Doppler. It had been a modified search set with a single polarization and no velocity channel, which meant it could see the shape of a storm and not the spin inside it. Odell pointed at the receiver rack and told her that in humid air the waveguide filled with condensation and threw a ghost return at nine miles, dead north, every time, and that the ghost had been Wren's first obsession when she found the 1977 schematic, because her own rig threw the same ghost when she ran the humidity sensor too warm in the afternoon.

"That's the same fault," she said.

"That's the same design," Odell said. "Whoever built your rig and whoever built this tower read the same datasheet, because there was only ever one."

The real tell of the tower was none of that. The real tell was the elevation motor. The azimuth drive hunted, but the elevation drive was dead honest to within a tenth of a degree, and when you canted the dish up and swept the low horizon east of the ridge, you could read the entire column of a distant storm in one vertical slice, bottom to top, and if you knew how to read the slice, you could see the updraft lean before it leaned, and the day before it leaned, and the hour before it leaned.

"That's how you could forecast old storms," Wren said. "From the vertical. You didn't have velocity, so you inferred it."

"You infer it," Odell said. "You take a slice every six minutes and you draw the slant of the column on a sheet of tracing paper and you put the sheets on the light box and you watch the column pretend it isn't turning." He smiled, and it was the first smile she had seen on him since the day went grey. "It's the same thing you're doing with your rig, except you've got electronics and I've got a light box."

She set the rig up on the shelter roof, on the flat of the dish mount, and ran her cable down to the logger, and started the battery. The tower's dish threw its ghost. Her rig threw its ghost. She took the ghost off with the humidity housing whitewashed, like he had told her, and the trace went clean, and for the first time in her eight months of schematics and logs and morning emails she and Odell Brandt were looking at the same sky through two machines three generations apart.

"Now," he said. "I'll tell you what I did here."

He told it standing up, at the desk, with his back to the console, and he told it the way he mapped storms. Landmarks first, weather second, and the hard thing left for the end.

"In seventy-four, a drought took half the wheat in this county. In seventy-six, a hailstorm took the other half. There was a program, and I'll say it flat because you already knew it. There was a program, run out of the weather mod office, to put silver iodide into the clouds over these plains and turn the hail to water. It was called cloud seeding and it was published as hail suppression and the local paper ran it on the front page in July of seventy-six and called it science." He put his hand on the logbook. "The tower was the eye. This tower. It was new in seventy-seven and I was the young engineer who wired the return from the dish to the shelter, and I sat in this room for eight years and watched every storm in the county that could be seeded, and I gave the crews their timings, and they fired the flares on my word."

Wren did not move. She had a reading for this. She had a reading for this and it was not going to help.

"It worked," Odell said. "Most of the time. We turned the hail to rain and the wheat stood up and the county stocked the shelter with canned goods and everyone slept fine. And in seventy-nine, in August, we seeded a cell that was bigger than anything the models had ever shown us, and it was the wrong cell, and it went against its own forecast and stepped off the line of every paper we had." He stopped. He looked at the floor. "It killed a town thirty miles west of here on a Tuesday I was standing in this room."

He did not name the town. Wren waited for the name and it did not come.

"We buried it," he said. "The program, I mean. Not the town. The town got buried by other people." His voice had gotten very level, which was worse than shaking. "That August we closed the office and pulled the flares and I helped sign the paper that said the program had never been conclusively shown to work, and I moved to Wyoming, and I did not come back for forty years because I could not think of one good reason to look at this ridge again."

The dish above them hunted. Azimuth, dead band, swing, catch. It ran its little cycle and stopped and waited and ran it again, and Odell listened to it the way Wren listened to the trace on her logger, taking a reading.

"You told me on the map it was inconclusive," Wren said. "The literature."

"The literature is inconclusive," Odell said. "I helped write the literature."

"Then how did you bury it if the science said it never worked."

"Because it was inconclusive and we knew it worked," Odell said. "That's the whole burial. You can't close a program that works. You close a program that never conclusively worked. You take a working program and you write it up as a failed one, and then you can walk out the door, because a failed program is a shame and a working program is a crime." He looked at her for the first time since he started. "You asked me on the map why my pencil stopped. It stopped because you said the word for the crime."

Wren got the logger off the dish mount at noon, packed it into the case, and checked the trace one more time before she wrapped the head in the wool blanket Odell kept in the truck bed.

The trace had a reading on it she had not asked for.

For most of the morning the differential had run the way it ran in dry air, low and quiet, the numbers she had spent two years learning to trust. Somewhere in the hour when Odell had been talking about the flares, the rig had logged a rise in the ion count. A wave, then a peak, then a long flat crest. Not the hump of solar heating. Not lightning. She knew the shape of lightning; she had chased the shape of lightning across a hundred and forty log pages. This was a peak on a flat base with no thunder behind it, and there was no thunder anywhere in the county, because the radar the national service ran out of Amarillo still showed nothing east of the ridge but blue sky and wheat.

She ran the numbers twice.

The mass was consistent with silver iodide. Not the trace you would get from a firework or a road flare or a farmer's legacy crop duster. This was an aerosol load with the right size distribution and the right rise, delivered high, delivered against the low-level wind so it could only have come down from a source above the boundary layer. If the source was above the boundary layer and east of the ridge, there was a plane up there. Wren had spent eight months reading the schematics of the man beside her, and she knew exactly who in this county had the chops to read a trace like this.

She did not say it.

She folded the strip of paper once and slid it into the back of her notebook, behind the July pages, behind the boring ones. She would spend the next two weeks trying to explain to herself why she had done that, and she would never get an answer that satisfied her, and the first thing she would ever say to Odell about it, four days later and twenty miles east of the tower, would be the sentence she should have said on the roof: I've had this since the tower roof and I didn't show it to you. That was the whole reason and it took her until the isotope comparison to find it. He had told her a thing that had killed a town, and she had gone looking for the part he left out, and she had found it before he chose to give it. She did not know yet what she was asking. She hid the strip because a reading is a thing you hold, and because you do not hand a man back his own buried crime on the first day he tells you the town's name.

She wrapped the head in the blanket and carried it empty-handed down the stairs.

The drive down the ridge took eleven minutes, and they had been on the gravel for six of them when the convoy came up the road the other way and passed them.

Wren counted because she could not help counting. Four trucks, dark, unmarked, cab-high boxes with the paint scuffed to primer along the rocker panels, and no county plates and no logo on the door and no sign at all except a number stencilled small on the driver's side of each box, one through four. The third truck had a flatbed with a tarp over it and the tarp was lashed tight to what looked like a generator, and the fourth truck was pulling a trailer with a coil of hose on it and the hose was the yellow-white of ag-grade lay-flat. They were headed east, up the gravel, toward the ridge, toward the tower behind them, toward Pella beyond that.

Wren watched them go by. She watched Odell watch them.

He kept his eyes on the road and his hands at ten and two and his speed at twenty-five, and the trucks passed, and the dust came up off their wheels and hung in the cab, and Odell Brandt did not say one word.

He did not say a word at the crest. He did not say a word at the hairpin. He did not say a word at the cattleguard, or the county line, or over the four miles of straight road after that.

They went a full mile in silence.

Wren sat with the rig case between her knees and her notebook on her lap and her thumb on the folded strip of paper in the back of it, and she watched the road and the man and the mile go by, and she took the reading the way she took readings, in her right hand, small. A convoy of four trucks, unmarked, heading east toward the supercell's formation point four days before the supercell formed. A man who would not turn his head. And behind her, in the notebook, a hair-thin peak in a strip of paper that said silver iodide and had no thunder to explain it.

"Odell," she said.

"Not yet," he said. "Let me get to the pavement."

They got to the pavement. He still didn't say it.

The town came up over the rise in its grey-gold rectangle, the elevator white over the wheat, and the tower standing small on the ridge behind them, hunting, turning into the wind that did not exist, and Wren watched the mile tick over on the truck's odometer and did not ask again. She kept her hand flat on the notebook and the notebook closed and the strip of paper in its fold, where it was a reading, and a reading was a thing you could hold.

Then he said it.

"Four trucks went by us on the ridge road," Odell said, "four days before a seeded supercell is forecast to form over Pella, and one of them was pulling a lay-flat hose trailer, and there is one thing on this earth you pull a lay-flat hose trailer for, and it is not wheat."

Wren did not answer that.

"You saw it before I did," he said. "You counted them in the mirror and you counted the stencil numbers and you counted the trailer, and you took the whole thing down in that notebook before I said a word. Good. That's the reading that matters." He kept his eyes on the town. "I want you to understand what it means before you do anything with it. Those trucks belong to a program that does not exist. The paper it ran on was pulled in 1979 and the office was closed and the flares were collected. If somebody is running four trucks up the ridge road in August, then the only way for it to happen is for the program to have been picked up again by whoever kept the schedule, and the schedule is a thing somebody compiled." He slowed for the co-op turn. "I don't know whose. If I did, I'd say it. What I know is it's a program, and a program has crews, and crews have a chain of command, and the top of the chain has a name I have not earned the right to guess at on nine miles of gravel."

Wren took the turn, and then she asked the thing the beta reader would ask, out loud, because she was sixteen and she had spent two years learning not to file a reading that mattered.

"Tuesday," she said. "The trucks were on the ridge on Tuesday."

"They were on the ridge on Tuesday," Odell said.

"That was the day the models still had a dry line setting up west of here. There was nothing over Pella on Tuesday but blue sky. There was nothing for the national service to flag, and the state doesn't seed off models, the state seeds off a filing, and a filing off a filing is a thing with a paper trail." She parked in the co-op lot and killed the engine and looked at him across the bench. "Which means whoever's running this didn't move on the storm. They moved before it. They knew where it was going to be before the models did, or they wrote the models. Say the second one is worse."

Odell looked through the windshield at the elevator for a long count of seconds. The windshield chip was still there, a dime of white in the passenger glass from a rock off some other road on some other day, and neither of them had ever explained it to anybody.

"The second one is worse," he said. "And the second one is the one that fits the truck count. A crew that size doesn't stand up on a rumor of weather. Somebody told them Tuesday. Which means somebody is reading the same thing we read two days before we read it, and I would very much like to know who."

He got out of the truck and did not slam the door, because he never slammed doors.

Wren followed him into the office and hung her jacket on the nail by the door and turned on the bad fluorescent light. The logbook was on the desk where she had left it, spread open to July. The map with the WINDOW label was in the tube by the window. And in the back of the notebook under her arm, behind the July pages, the folded strip of paper was still folded, and still a reading, and still, for now, hers.

She got the coffee on, because that was what you did in the cold room, and then she sat down at the co-op's desk with the July logs in front of her and the strip of paper in her back pocket and the four stencil numbers written small in the corner of a page, and she started the arithmetic that would take her until Thursday.

Off to the east the sky was clear and the dry line was still four hundred miles away and nothing at all was going to happen over Pella for four days. None of it showed on the glass.
