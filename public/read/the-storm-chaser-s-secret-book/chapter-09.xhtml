The Conductive Tether

They came off the county road at the motel before eleven because the motel was the only place with a roof and a door and a light that ran on a line that was still standing. Wren backed the truck up to the room, and they carried the housing and the crate and the spool inside in two trips and put the mat down and laid the rig on it, and Odell shut the curtains and Wren turned on every lamp in the room, and the room became a shop.

It was a bad shop. It was a bed and a chair and a television bolted to a swivel and a bathroom with a fan that did not work, and the carpet was the color of old wheat. Wren put the housing on the bed and the crate on the chair and the spool on the floor, and she knelt on the carpet and took the top plate off the housing with the screwdriver from her back pocket, and the room smelled like rain and hot dust and the ozone off the dead stack.

She had fired once and lost. She was not going to lose twice for the same reason.

"Hand me the meter," she said, and Tobin handed her the meter.

The capacitor was bad. She had known it before she opened the housing and she knew it worse with the plate off. The 1976 can had taken the arc and swelled at the seam, and the dielectric had vented out the bottom onto the board in a brown stain, and the board under it was scorched at two of the four terminals. She had expected the can. She had not expected the board.

"Board's cooked," she said.

"At the second terminal," Odell said. He was sitting in the chair with the crate open between his knees and a rag in his hand, cleaning a salvaged connector barrel with the slow circular motion of a man who had done exactly this in exactly this kind of room a long time ago. "It's a through-hole board. You can bridge it."

"With what?"

He reached into the crate and held up a short coil of tinned wire.

"You kept the wire," she said.

"I kept the whole crate," Odell said. "I told you I lied about the crate."

She took the wire and bridged the two dead terminals and soldered them with a pencil iron that plugged into the lamp socket and a strip of solder she bit off with her teeth. Then she seated the 1976 capacitor in the cradle and tightened the strap and ran continuity from the can to the ground lug, and the meter read what she wanted it to read. Then she ran it again, and a third time, and a fourth, and the fourth read the same as the first three, and only then did she take her hands off the housing and sit back on her heels.

Continuity was the easy thing. Continuity was the thing that had been true all four times at the draw, right up until the mast arced.

She looked at the rig. It was a box of aluminum with a lid and a stake stand and a spool of tether, and the tether was the part she had been working toward for two years without once being able to say out loud that she would need it. The method was not hers. The method was older than she was, older than Odell, older than the tower. You send a small rocket up into the electric field with a conductor trailing off the tail, and the conductor makes the path the leader is looking for, and the discharge comes down the path early, on purpose, into the ground. What the sky was going to do in its own time, it does in yours.

Triggered lightning. The wire goes up. The wire vaporizes. The next strike follows the ion trail the first one left.

She had read every page of it she could find for two years, and she had never once put a rocket on a tether in a real updraft, because a real updraft is a thing that will kill you if you are wrong, and she had not had a reason to be wrong until now.

"The rocket," she said.

Tobin brought it. It was a solid-fuel motor the length of her forearm with a nose and four fins and a channel down the side, built to trail a line, and the line was the whole disagreement.

"We splice the tether to the rocket's shock cord at the nose, not the tail," she said, laying the two of them out on the mat. "The main line goes off the nose so the rocket flies with the wire behind it and the wire doesn't whip and doesn't foul the fins on the way up." She laid a length of copper along the rocket's body and measured it by eye against the fins and cut it and stripped an inch of jacket with her thumbnail. "And we do not, at any point, let the tether touch the frame of the truck while the motor's loaded."

"Wren," Odell said.

"Not now."

"Now," he said, and put the rag down.

He got up out of the chair and crossed the room and knelt on the mat across from her. His knees made a sound going down, and he looked at the rig for a while the way he had looked at the tower from the county road, which was the way a man looks at a house he used to live in and then burned.

"The town was Baird," he said.

She did not stop working. She kept stripping the jacket off the tether, an inch at a time, because her hands were the only part of her that could be trusted right now, and she let him talk.

"It's thirty miles west of this ridge," Odell said. "There's a grain elevator there and a school and a rodeo grounds and a one-lane bridge over a creek that runs twice a year. Six hundred people on a good day in 1979. I gave the crews their timings out of the tower logbook and their flare orders, and on the ninth of August I gave them a schedule I had built on a matrix I had built, and the cell came up off the line exactly where I said, and the seed went in exactly where I said, and for four hours it did exactly what I said it would do." He turned the connector barrel over in his fingers. "Then the boundary layer went neutral at the wrong hour. The matrix didn't have a term for it because I hadn't been taught one. The updraft stopped venting and the column went down on itself and turned, and it moved west instead of east, and it went over Baird."

Wren cut the last strip of jacket and laid the copper bare.

"Six people," she said.

"Six people," Odell said. "A woman named Catherine Duley and her two boys, who were in a car on the bridge because the road report said the storm was going the other way. A man named Emory Prasky, who was seventy-four and lived in the low house by the creek. A girl named Ana Reyes, who was out on her bicycle on the south road because her mother had been told the storm was going the other way." He said the last name and stopped.

Wren's hands stopped.

"Reyes," she said.

"Reyes," Odell said. "Tobin's grandmother's sister. He knows. He's known since he was thirteen. His mother told him when he asked her why the family name was in the inquiry record and the inquiry record had no page for it. He came to Vance's crew to find out whether the program still existed. He found me instead."

At the door, Tobin leaned on the jamb with his arms crossed and his eyes on the middle distance, and he had not moved since he came in with the crate, and he did not move now.

"Did you write the names," Wren said.

"I wrote them in the report," Odell said, "and then I wrote the report up inconclusive, and the inconclusive report went into a drawer, and I moved to Wyoming, and for forty years every time it hailed I got up and looked west." He set the connector barrel down on the mat between them. "That's the whole confession. There isn't a second one. I've spent forty years deciding whether the crime was the program or the burying of it, and I decided it was the burying, and I decided it too late to be any good to anybody. Which is why I came back. Not to be forgiven. To be in the way when it happened again." He nodded at the rig. "And now I'm in the way, and you want me to help you splice a tether onto a rocket in a motel room."

"Yes," Wren said.

"Then let me tell you what I think about that."

"Tell me."

"You're sixteen and your theory is sound and your instrument is good and your timing is off," Odell said. "You fired once tonight and lost a capacitor and an hour. If you fire again from the wrong place in the wrong minute, you lose the rig and the truck and the bench seat and possibly you. A triggered strike into a rotating updraft at the base does not care that you're right. It goes where the potential goes. You have one shot and no ground test and no second stack and no generator that isn't the truck, and the storm is going to notice you."

"I know."

"Do you."

"I know that a triggered discharge is early," Wren said. "It's early by design. It empties the charge that was going to collect and release in its own time into a path that goes straight to the ground. A rotating updraft that has lost its base charge is a rotating updraft that has lost its separation, and the column's separation is the only thing holding the column up. Pull the charge out of the base before it organizes, and the updraft loses its footing and it collapses from the bottom. A supercell that collapses from the bottom ropes out and dies in its own rain. That's the theory. I didn't invent it. It's the theory in every paper ever written about triggered lightning, and the papers are about research, because nobody's ever done it into a storm like this, because nobody ever had a reason." She held up the bare copper. "I have a reason."

Odell looked at her for a long moment.

"Then splice it," he said, "and I'll do the nose ring, because your fin knots are terrible."

They worked for forty minutes. Odell spliced the tether to the nose ring with three turns and a whipped end. Wren rigged the trigger circuit and checked continuity at the stake lug and ran the display through its boot cycle twice. Tobin tore the cardboard off a case of water and flattened it and drew the notch on it with a marker from the front desk, and the rain hammered the roof and the lights flickered twice and held.

At the top of the hour the radio somebody had left on the nightstand, tuned by accident to the county spill channel, said: "All units, Pella south shelter remains at capacity. School is opening. Request all available at the highway junction, we have people walking."

At ten past the hour the wind came around southwest, and the rain stopped on the roof like a hand being lifted.

Wren went to the door and opened it.

The air had changed. It was not cooler; it was a different kind of air, pressure-heavy and still and full of a smell she knew from the roof of the co-op, the smell of a column going up somewhere close, ozone and wet iron and cut wheat. The sky to the north had gone from black to a black with a lit edge on it, and under the lit edge there was a shape.

She got the binoculars out of the truck.

The wall cloud had lowered all the way and made a tail. The tail was a funnel that was not yet on the ground and was turning hard, and under the funnel the rain had gone green, not a trick of the light but the real green of a hail core lit from inside, and even at four miles the truck's frame hummed and the screws in the motel door plate buzzed against her fingers.

"Tornado," she said. "It's down on the two-five-zero heading, five miles north of here, and it's coming west at the speed of the road."

Odell came out on the step and looked at it and said nothing.

Tobin came out and looked at it and said, "The convoy's heading back."

The trucks were up the county road with their lights on, four of them, coming south, and over the sound of the storm she heard the pulse of a small plane east of the rain, and the whole four miles of the inflow lit once from ground to anvil.

"Final pass," Tobin said. "That's the four-thirty run pulled forward. She's flying it east. When she seeds east, the column takes it and the lean goes west."

"Then we're out of gaps," Wren said.

"No," Odell said. "We're out of scheduled gaps. This is the unscheduled one." He looked at the funnel and at the trucks and at the funnel's track, and he did the math out loud the way he had once done it in a logbook above a ridge. "She seeds in the next four minutes. The plane's on its final pass and the storm surges on the seed, and the surge pushes the core over the section line, and the core is where you want the rig. Get in the truck. We're going north into it."

They loaded the rig in fifty seconds flat. Odell took the spool into the bed and set the brake off and kept his hand on the shaft. Wren drove. Tobin rode in the middle with the meter on his knees and the toggle run up to the cab so he could fire from the seat if the radio failed.

The county road ran north and the storm filled the whole right side of the world. The funnel was on the ground and moving, and the rain curtain that pushed ahead of it was a wall of water vertical, and the water moved sideways at the bottom where the inflow came in, and the wheat in the ditch went flat before them and got up behind them and went flat again.

Wren stopped the truck at the fence line where the draw opened and the notch was, in the gap between two runs of fence and the culvert, which was the spot Tobin had driven to four nights running. She got the stake in with the sledge, two hands and no ceremony, and set the tripod and leveled it and put the housing on the mount, and she drove the third stake at the end of the guy and grounded the frame to it and ran the meter down the path one last time. The meter read true. She seated the rocket in the launch tube and led the tether off the spool, and she put her hand on the housing to feel for the vibration the way she had felt it on the co-op roof nine days ago, and the housing was steady, and the housing was the only steady thing in the county.

The inflow found them.

It came across the wheat at the speed of a train, the air of the whole column feeding down into the core. It took her hair straight back and took the flattened cardboard off the truck bed and took the map with it, and the temperature dropped nine degrees in six seconds, and she felt the pressure come down through her sinuses, and over everything the scrape of the funnel going by a mile west with hail in it.

In the east, the plane's lights crossed the top of the wall cloud and went out.

"She's fired," Tobin said. "Count."

Wren watched the anvil.

One, two, three—

The base of the storm flash-lit from within and the whole lower cloud went wide and low like a bell being struck, and the rotation above the funnel came down and the funnel got fatter and the rain under it went from a rope to a cone, and the storm surged.

It surged the way a fire surges when you turn air on it.

"Rockets," Odell said from the bed, flat and urgent, and Wren was already at the trigger. The display showed the field in the notch go negative and hold negative and step, and the notch came over the fence line and the radio crackled as the charge built in the cab, and Wren heard her own voice in her own head saying the theory the way she had said it on the roof, over and over, to nobody, for two years. Early is not breaking it. Early is finishing it on purpose.

She put her hand warm on the trigger and looked once at Odell. He had his hands out of the way and his eyes on the funnel, and he nodded, once, and said what he had not said since a logbook on a ridge in 1977.

"Send it."

Wren fired the rocket.

It left the tube with a hard flat crack and went up and out into the inbound air, and the tether paid off the spool in a wire of copper going up and over the fence line into the rain, and the rocket climbed into the notch and the fins began to whistle against the fin wash and the tether stood up behind it, and the display counted the field step by step and then the notch went quiet, which was the sign.

The conductive path was up.

The sky leaned down it.

The first strike followed the tether exactly, straight down the wire, and hit the stake in the ground at the fence line with a flash that went white and silent for one whole second. The second strike followed the ion trail the wire left, and the third followed the first two so close behind that the thunder from the first had not finished arriving. The whole fence line lit, and Wren felt the discharge go through the soles of her boots into the ground under the wheat, and the display read what she had spent two years promising it would read: the field in the inflow notch dropping as the base emptied, the separation gone negative, and up in the rotating base the wall cloud stopped leaning west and hung.

The storm held.

And then, a mile north, the funnel left the ground and took the rain with it, and the whole column came apart from the bottom.
