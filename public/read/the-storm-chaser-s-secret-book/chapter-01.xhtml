The Rig on the Roof

The rig came to life on the third try, and Wren Kalisz allowed herself exactly one second of relief.

Crouched on the grain co-op roof with her knees aching against the shingles, she watched the first column of numbers crawl up the small screen she had bolted to the rig's aluminum spine. Wind speed: nine. Temperature: eighty-six. Relative humidity: thirty-four percent. Charge differential: climbing. She had spent seven months welding this thing out of scrap conduit and a gutted weather radio. Tonight it was finally reading a dry thunderstorm the way she had promised it would. Not the rain. Not the wind. Just the static, the part of the storm most people never noticed.

The cell sat thirty miles east, over open ground. No lightning yet. That was the point.

Wren tapped the housing twice with her knuckle. The numbers held.

"Okay," she said, to nobody. "Okay."

She sat back on her heels and let the roof take her weight. From up here the co-op looked like a thing built by someone who had never seen a storm, all flat roofs and open bays, and beyond it Coyote Flats went on in its long low line of gravel and tin and one streetlight already buzzing against the dusk. The elevator cast a shadow clean across the lot. Past the lot the wheat lay down flat in the evening wind, all the way out to the county road and the road's pale line running east into nothing. You could see the weather an hour before it reached you, if you knew how to look.

Wren knew how to look. It was the only thing she had ever been sure she was good at.

The gust hit and the mast leaned, and she leaned with it, one hand flat on the rig's spine, feeling the vibration come up through the aluminum the way you felt a truck's engine through the floorboards. Charge differential ticked up one, then two. The storm was still just building, still roiling over a dry field with no rain shaft under it, the kind of storm that spits lightning at the ground because it can. That was the kind that had killed June. Two years in May, a cell that no model had flagged, that had come up out of the panhandle at midnight and put down a single bolt on the county road where June had been driving home from a friend's house in Pella. One bolt. That was the whole thing. Doppler radar could pick up a storm as it formed and track its path, but the warning that followed covered only the next hour or so. Wren had read every forecast for that night, twice, the way she read everything twice now. Nothing called it. Nothing at all.

And so the rig. Seven months of scrap metal and solder burns and one trip to the emergency room she had not told her mother about, the scar on her thumb still pale and puckered. Because if you could not forecast the bolt, maybe you could learn how to fire one on purpose. Early, on your terms, into the part of the storm that mattered. The updraft had to be choked. Triggered lightning did that. She had the papers to prove it, printouts folded into the cover of her notebook. She had a rig to prove it with. She had nobody at all who believed her.

Below her, the co-op's lot was empty except for the trucks her mother would need at five in the morning and the flatbed with the bad axle that had been blocking bay three since March. Coyote Flats went to bed early. The diner closed at eight. The gas pumps went to card-only at nine. By ten the only light in town was the streetlight on Depot Road and whichever porch light someone had forgotten, and the sky above all of it going from blue to grey to a deep bruised violet that the screen said meant charge and the eye said meant nothing yet.

Wren watched the numbers for the space of ten breaths. Then a pickup's headlights swung into the lot below and washed across the bays.

She looked down. The truck was not her mother's.

It was wrong in three ways at once. Too clean, no dust on the fenders and no caked red clay in the wheel wells, and everything out here had clay in its wheel wells by June. Too old, a dented Ford the color of an old nickel, the kind that ran on nothing but stubbornness. Too foreign, a Wyoming plate, and nobody came to Coyote Flats from Wyoming unless they were lost or selling something.

The driver parked in the lot's far corner, well clear of the co-op's trucks, and got out slowly, the way old men did when their hips had opinions. He stood looking up at the roof for a long count of ten, as if he had known exactly where to find her.

Wren had known his name for eight months. She had typed it into the top of a message thread that ran back to last November. That thread was longer than any conversation she had ever had with a human being in person.

The thread was titled odin_brandt_radar — 1977 tower specs.

She had found him the way she found everything, sideways, in a scanned bureau archive that had never been digitized and probably never would be. His name was on a 1977 maintenance report for the radar tower on the ridge east of town. His email address was still live. She had written him a message that was four paragraphs of questions and one sentence of introduction. He had answered the next morning. He had answered every morning since.

Wren closed the screen on the rig and started down the ladder.

Halfway down, she stopped and said it flat across the lot. "You're Odell Brandt."

He looked at her for a moment. Then he said, "You're Wren Kalisz," with a small, tired smile, as if the introductions were a formality they had both already skipped. "I know your handwriting."

"How?"

"Your drawings. The rig. You send me schematics with your left hand, and you write your logs with your right. That's how the slant goes." He put his hands in his pockets. "I've been reading your weather for eight months, Miss Kalisz. I know a good deal more about you than a stranger should."

Wren came off the last rung and landed in the gravel with her hands still grey from the roof tar. She did not offer to shake his hand. She was sixteen and he was old and she did not know the rule for this. He just stood there with his collar turned up and looked at the rig's antenna above her head, and then at the lot, and then at the ridge, in that order, the way a person checked a room for exits.

"You've seen the models," she said.

"I've seen the models."

"So have I."

"Then we have something to talk about," Odell said, "and I'd rather not do it standing in a parking lot."

Her mother found out inside four minutes.

The co-op office had one window and one fluorescent light and a coffee maker that had been broken since before Wren was born. Wren sat Odell down at the table and brought the rig inside and plugged it in, because she was not going to stand in the dark and trade riddles with a stranger. The screen lit. The numbers came back. The office smelled like paper and diesel and the peppermint her mother kept in a tin by the till.

Odell put his glasses on and read the screen the way a person read a score. "Nine at the surface, eighteen aloft," he said. "You've got shear. That's a storm with a spine."

"It's a dry cell," Wren said. "It won't do anything. It's just — I needed something to test against."

"No," he said, "you didn't."

She looked at him.

"You've been watching this thing cross the county for ninety minutes," he said, "and you packed up the moment it showed charge. You're not testing against it. You're rehearsing." He tapped the screen, gently, like it was a person's shoulder. "That's not a criticism. That's a compliment."

Wren had run out of a comeback and was still sorting through the drawer for one when the door opened.

Her mother came in with a dish towel over her shoulder and the whole weight of the county in her face. The fluorescent light buzzed over the sink. The clock on the wall said eight forty-one, and Wren noted it, because noting the time was easier than noting the look on her mother's face.

"Who's this?" her mother said.

"Odell Brandt. He's a radar engineer. He and I have been—"

"You're the man she writes to," her mother said, and put the dish towel down on the table like she was laying down a hand of cards. "I've seen your name on her screen."

Odell stood up. "Ma'am."

"You're the one with the tower." Her mother's eyes went over him once, top to bottom, the way she priced cattle. "I've heard about you."

"Not from me," Wren said.

"From the internet," her mother said. "He's on the internet. There was a—" She stopped. Whatever it was, it was filed away where she kept the things she did not say in front of Wren. "There was something."

Wren watched her mother decide something, and watched her decide not to say it, and watched the towel stay on the table between them.

"Supper's on," her mother said, to Wren. "There's a plate for you and a plate for your guest, if he's staying. And then we're going to have the conversation about the chasing again."

"We already had it."

"We have it every time, and you don't hear me, so we have it again." Her mother looked at Odell. "You'll eat first. I'm not sending a man out on an empty truck."

Supper was chicken and cold bread and the good gravy, because her mother did not know how to be angry without also feeding you. Odell Brandt ate two helpings and asked about the co-op's moisture meter and the state of the road east and whether the diner in town still did pie. By the time the plates were cleared it was almost warm in the room. Almost.

Wren ate one roll and pushed the rest around. She watched the two of them the way she watched a radar loop, trying to see where the motion was going before it got there. Her mother was doing the thing she did with strangers, the polite thing, the thing that kept the door open and the lights on and the questions out of the room. Odell was doing a thing Wren had not seen before. He was letting her. He answered every question with an answer three sentences longer than it needed to be, and did not ask one back.

And then, because Wren had never once in her life let a silence sit, she told him about the models.

"They've been running the same pattern for six days," she said. "A supercell forms east of Pella on Thursday. Big shear, dry line backing west, the whole setup. CAPE's in the four thousands by afternoon and the hodograph's a textbook curve and the LCL's low enough that anything that drops stays dropped." She said it fast, and the numbers came out the way they always did, in a run, and her mother's jaw tightened a notch and did not open. "It's the one."

Odell wiped his mouth. "The once-a-decade storm."

"You've seen it."

"I've seen the models." He put his fork down and turned his coffee cup a quarter turn, lining it up with the napkin edge, and Wren understood immediately and completely that he had seen more than the models. "I'd have to drive back out to the tower to be sure of anything. The ridge radar's been half-blind since '09." He paused. "But yes. It's the one."

And that was when Wren's mother said, very quietly, "Wren. No."

Wren set her fork down too. "It's not chasing to stand on a county road and take a reading. It's fieldwork."

"It's the same thing, and you know it's the same thing, and I have one of you left." Her mother's voice did not rise. It never rose. It just got quieter, which was worse. "Every time you go out there, I sit in this kitchen and I think, and then I wait. I am so tired of waiting, Wren."

The kitchen clock ticked. One. Two. Three.

"June didn't die because she chased," Wren said, and her own voice was tight now, technical, the numbers coming up behind her teeth. "She died because nobody saw it coming. Because the warnings were wrong. Because a single-cell storm formed and moved and died inside of half an hour and there was no lead time. That's the problem I'm trying to fix. I can't fix it from this kitchen."

"You're sixteen."

"I know how old I am. I built the clock that counts it."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. Wren held the look, because holding it was the one thing she had. The data did not hold eyes. Only the girl did. Then her mother got up, took the plate Wren was done with, and put it in the sink, and that was the whole resolution of the argument, the same as every time. The faucet ran. The light hummed. The plate went into the rack and the water went on running over it.

Odell Brandt sat very still and did not pretend he had not heard.

When her mother had gone up the stairs, Wren turned and looked at him flat, and said, "The radar on the ridge. You worked it."

"I did."

"You help me read it Thursday, and I'll let you ride."

"Let me," Odell said, and for just a second something moved across his face that Wren could not name. "All right. I'll be let."

He walked out to his truck with her afterward. The storm was still sitting east of town, still dry, still spitting the occasional thread of light from cloud to cloud, and the wheat stood up silver in the last of the dusk. The air had gone cool the way it did when a storm decided whether it was going to commit. Off to the north a dog was barking at nothing. The streetlight on Depot Road came on with a click Wren could hear from here.

"I came here to ride shotgun," he said.

Wren stopped walking. "Excuse me?"

"The supercell. Thursday." He leaned against the truck's dented fender, not looking at her, looking east, at the storm he could not possibly have been able to see and somehow was looking at anyway. "You've got the rig, you've got the theory, and you've got nobody who's actually read a radar return in the field since the Reagan administration. I have. I read the old tower's paper every day for eight years and I helped wire it in the first place." He folded his hands. "Your instinct is good, your data is clean, and your truck is going to need a second driver, because Thursday is a seven-hour storm and you can't watch the sky and the road at the same time."

Wren's pulse was going, fast, the way it went when the numbers lined up. "You left the plains forty years ago."

"I did."

"Why now?"

He looked east for another second. The clouds there were still lit and still dry and still not raining, and the charge was still building, and Wren's rig was still in the office ticking up its numbers where no one was watching.

"There's a thing I used to say to the crew," Odell said, at last. "Updraft's a sum, not a sentence. Everything that happens in it is the total of what went into it, and the column doesn't care who you are. If you can read the sum, you can read the storm." He straightened off the fender. "I can still read the sum. And I'd rather be reading it out there than sitting in a motel room in Wyoming watching it come at me."

She should have asked why a man who left the flats forty years ago would come back for one storm. She would ask, later, and the answer would take until the sixth chapter to get out of him, and by then the storm would be somewhere it had no business being. But it was Thursday's storm, and it was hers, and he was right. She could not watch the sky and the road at the same time.

"Six a.m.," Wren said. "Bring your own coffee. My mother's is terrible."

Odell Brandt laughed, once, like a man putting down a long-carried weight. "Six a.m."

He got in the truck and drove off west. Wren stood in the co-op lot a while longer. The dry cell pulsed east, one thin thread of light going groundward for the first time all night and snuffing itself out before it got there, and over the ridge the sky went on doing exactly what the models had promised, on the exact clock she had counted.
