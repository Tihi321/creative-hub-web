What the City Ows

The loading arch, dawn

Ambience: water lapping under stone, a motor idling just over idle, a radio's papery hiss

Grey dawn comes across the flooded street and into the high stone arch. The rain has thinned to mist. Inside the dry lip of the arch a chair stands beside a crate, and Dell sits with his bad leg out.

On screen: Dell (brown canvas jacket, cap off, grey stubble, radio on his knee), Sol (in the works boat, sodden coat, bag against her knee), Ife (standing, line over her shoulder), The municipal engineer (at the tiller, helmet under his arm)

DELL You're wet.

SOL I've been in the water a while.

DELL Sit down before you fall down. Somebody brought me a chair. I haven't earned it.

SOL Grille was seized. Your hidden screw was clever for about a decade and a nuisance ever since. Boy had a stub wrench. The woman with the bailer held the tin lid over the fuse so it stayed dry.

SOL Bracket had ridden off the wall. It's shimmed, the nut's home, new cartridge, and I waited out the tick and it came back quiet. It wants a survey before the month's out and I want to be on it.

DELL All of that. In the dark, on a ladder, in the flood.

SOL I did it by the water. The book was a liar, you told me so on the roof.

IFE He's been on the air all night. Ozzy's been handing him the channel every hour to say the district's holding.

SOL I heard.

The loading arch, dawn

Ambience: a channel hiss, a queue of voices far off at the tower

Ozzy's voice comes out of the handheld at Sol's hip, warm and rapid, the fine hiss under it of a whole delta listening.

On screen: Sol (handheld in hand), Dell (looking at his hands), The municipal engineer (folder closed, on the arch step), Ife (arms crossed)

OZZY Port Alaric. It's coming up light and the rain's off. On the line I have the municipal engineer of works, and I'm not letting him speak in section numbers. The city's had a long night.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER That's fair.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER The hospital district is lit. Wards lit, lifts running, the blood fridge cold, and Barrow Row reading its own meters. I checked it myself off the substation dials.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER I did not do it. An apprentice electrician did it, and four neighbours on a parapet, and a woman in a barge, and a radio operator, and a retired man with a bad leg. I'm recording all of that.

OZZY And the lawbreaking.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER Both switches are unrecorded. The pumping house island switch and the section link on the district wall. Installed by Dell Marchetti, off the survey and off the drawings.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER The code has something to say about that and it isn't kind. His license will be reviewed. The district may send its word to it. I said so on the parapet.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER But hear the other half from me, not scraped together out of the air.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER My men have been rebuilding the outfall feeders from a drawing Mr. Marchetti made at twenty. We could do it because he drew carefully. If he hadn't, we'd have no hospital either.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER That's not in the code, and I'll not be the only man to say a good word for him tonight.

DELL (quiet) That's a kind thing from a man who doesn't have to say it.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER It's a true thing. I don't like it any more than you do.

The loading arch, dawn

Ambience: the channel hiss, a hoarse voice close to a microphone

The arch fills with dawn. Dell's hand rests on the radio on his knee, white at the knuckle, and he has not pressed the key.

On screen: The man from Kirk Row (not seen, voice only), Sol (on her feet now), Dell (still, looking down), The municipal engineer (listening)

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW I'll say my word. I'm from the flats on Kirk Row. Water in my kitchen since three and my mother in her chair.

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW I heard your man say he hid a switch in a wall so nobody could catch him at it. So let me ask him while the city's listening.

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW What else has he hid? How many other switches has he left off the paper that'll come up next storm and get somebody killed?

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW I don't care if he lit the hospital. He lied to the city about the city's own wires for thirty years. That's my word.

SOL I'll answer him.

DELL Sol—

SOL I'll answer him.

SOL This is Sol Vieira, the apprentice off the district wall. Kirk Row has asked a fair question and he isn't wrong, and I won't pretend he is.

OZZY Say it. The whole of it.

SOL Dell Marchetti hid two switches. He hid them because he put them in where they were needed and the survey wouldn't pass them. Then he was ashamed, and a man with shame in him keeps a thing quiet a while.

SOL That's a fault in the man and a fault in the record, and it wants putting right.

SOL But you've a mother in a chair. Hear what held her up all night. It was the link he's scared of. The one off the paper. It kept the pumps pumping while the survey sat in a dark office.

SOL You asked what else he's hid. Six boxes in this city have had my hand in them since the surge.

SOL The waterworks. The market hub. The depot's third, where the tags were wrong and the drawing upside down.

SOL The depot's in-line, the shunt at the stone bridge.

SOL The pumping house island switch, the one that was the fourth, off the drawing since he was twenty.

SOL The wall link besides. Six, and every one's written in my notebook.

SOL If there was a seventh I'd have found it and put it in the book and told the city. That's what the notebook is for.

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW How do you know it's only six?

SOL Because I know the sound of a house. I've been listening to this one since before I could read.

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW That's the girl that went down the wall in a flood.

OZZY It is.

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW Well. Then I'll not say the old man lied to the city about the wires. I'll say he lied to the survey.

THE MAN FROM KIRK ROW And I'll send my complaint to the review, and I'd like the girl to put a line in it that says what she just said. My mother's chair runs off a socket and the socket was live all night. I'd like that in the record.

SOL It'll be in mine. I'll write it tonight. And if your socket's damp, make it safe before you sleep. If you can't, tell Ozzy and I'll send someone who can.

The loading arch, dawn

Ambience: a tired exact voice, a rough plain voice, a radio's papery breath

The queue at the tower comes to the microphone one at a time. The dawn has gone from iron to a rinsed pearl. Sol stands with the handheld loose in her hand and does not sit down.

On screen: The doctor off the ridge (not seen, voice only), The old woman at the culvert bridge (not seen, voice only), The sister's boy (not seen, voice only), The woman on the roof (not seen, voice only), Sol (listening), Ife (arms crossed at the arch step)

THE DOCTOR OFF THE RIDGE This is the doctor from the ridge. The wards are steady and the blood fridge never went warm. The power kept my patients alive, not one old man's pride.

THE OLD WOMAN AT THE CULVERT BRIDGE I've a complaint drafted and I'll read it. The law did not light a lamp. The law did not bail my culvert. The law did not boil my sister's kettle. That's the list and it's not finished.

THE SISTER'S BOY I held the light. Nobody was in charge. It worked.

THE WOMAN ON THE ROOF A line thrown from a barge saved my boy, and I still have the forty feet of good line, and I'll bring it down myself in the morning.

IFE (under her breath) Finally.

The loading arch, dawn

Ambience: the channel clicking quiet, water under the arch

The queue is done. Dell puts his hand flat over the radio on his knee and leaves it there.

On screen: Dell (hand on the radio, face lined), Sol (beside him), Ife (watching), The municipal engineer (folder under his arm)

DELL I'll say my word at the review. Not on the air. I've done my saying on the air.

DELL I'll sit in the chair and take what comes and be civil, and I'll not say the law was wrong. It's a good law and I knew it when I broke it.

DELL But I'll say the one thing the law has no box for. A city's wires and a city's people are not two different circuits. You can't survey one and leave the other dark.

SOL Your father would have said it shorter. And then he'd have gone and made the socket safe.

DELL Aye. That's the job.

THE MUNICIPAL ENGINEER Mr. Marchetti. The second boat's going downriver. The review will be easier if the man can walk to it.

DELL I've a great many things to say about reviews that won't go on the record.

The loading arch, morning

Ambience: an oar knocking a step, gulls, a motor turning away

The engineer's boat goes downriver with the old woman and the sister's boy. The old man comes along the parapet with Sol's coat over his one good arm.

On screen: The old man in the vending machine (dry, stooped, coat over his arm), Sol (turning to him), Ife (on the arch step, jaw set), The municipal engineer (at the tiller, pushing off)

THE OLD MAN IN THE VENDING MACHINE Your coat. It stayed dry in my one good arm.

SOL The wrench. When the market's lit I'll come down for it myself, and that's the last time I say it.

THE OLD MAN IN THE VENDING MACHINE Good.

Ife stands on the arch step with her arms crossed until the second boat is out of earshot.

IFE The tram.

SOL The tram.

IFE The water's going down on the yard. The salvage rights are mine and I've been up all night earning them.

IFE I'll take the engineer's man and the old man at the corner and the woman at her window as witnesses, and the dawn's a witness. Say it.

SOL First claim on the tram's salvage at the Drowned Tram Depot. The rest of the night split. And the board grievance settled by me speaking to Dell and the hall, not the radio.

IFE Then we're square on that.

IFE The engine comes out this morning and goes back in this afternoon, and it's four hundred pounds of old iron with the yard in it. I'm not pulling it alone.

SOL I'll pull it with you. I've never pulled a barge engine out of a barge and I'd like to.

IFE You'll be wet and your hands will be cut and I'll shout at you.

SOL That's a bargain I've heard before.

Drowned Tram Depot, morning

Ambience: water running off cobbles, a starter coughing and failing, gulls

The yard has drained enough to show the cobbles. Sun lies hard and bright on the wet city. The shed stands, the barge sits low, and the motors under the shed are dry.

On screen: Sol (kneeling at the engine casing, ear to the iron), Ife (bent over the sump plug, wrench in hand), The man with the tobacco tin (sandy work coat, wrench at his belt, at the shed door)

SOL It's full of the yard. Intake took water, sump's full, water standing in the cylinders.

SOL We pull the injectors, turn it over slow by hand, let it blow the water out. Drain the sump, new oil in. No starter till the cylinders are dry.

IFE And the gearbox?

SOL There's a note in it I don't like.

IFE There's a note in it I've never liked.

Sound: water spraying out of a cylinder, a wet hiss

IFE Ah, it's got me on the arm.

SOL That's the first one.

IFE Say it like a person, not a meter.

SOL That's the first one, and it was wet.

IFE You're the man with the feeder in the fish gutter.

THE MAN WITH THE TOBACCO TIN I'm the man with the feeder in the fish gutter. It's grounded and safe. I came to bring the tin back and say so.

THE MAN WITH THE TOBACCO TIN And to ask if there's work for a man with a wrench who's finished his job and doesn't want to go home to a dark flat.

He holds out the tin, empty now, the lid shut on nothing.

THE MAN WITH THE TOBACCO TIN My fuse went down the chain to the wall and I'm glad of it. But the tin's a good tin and it comes back where it came from.

THE MAN WITH THE TOBACCO TIN I've a coil of clean wire in my coat, if the shop wants it.

IFE The shop'll take the wire. The tin goes to the girl, because she likes tins.

IFE And there's a gearbox wants two men and a torch and half the morning, if you're not particular.

THE MAN WITH THE TOBACCO TIN I'm not particular.

He comes in off the step and gets his wrench out, and the three of them put their heads down over the gearbox.

SOL Thin feeler and a good torch, and I'll hear it running with the cover off before I believe it.

Sound: a feeler blade sliding, a slow breath held

SOL There. It wants a shim and it wants a man who's done it forty years.

THE MAN WITH THE TOBACCO TIN Then it wants me.

Drowned Tram Depot, afternoon

Ambience: a motor turning clean, water dripping off a shed roof

The sun is high, the puddles steam, and the market's low streets are lit while its upper streets stay dark. A radio in the yard carries Ozzy reading the city street by street.

On screen: Sol (in the sun on the yard wall, notebook in hand), Ife (below, wiping her hands on a rag), The man with the tobacco tin (at the engine casing, torch in his teeth)

OZZY (over a radio, small) —the lower market is lit. Barrow Row reads its meters. The upper market and the flats above the tram line stay dark until the survey. I'll read the list again at the hour.

SOL It isn't all of it. Streets without meters. A man on Kirk Row with a socket of his own to make safe. A review to go to and a grievance at the hall.

Sol crouches and puts her knuckles to the yard stone. Under her boots the city comes back up note by note, block by block.

SOL It's a circuit. Somebody closed it. A great many somebodies, hand to hand.

IFE (calling up) The gearbox. What's it want?

SOL It wants a person who can read a motor.

IFE That's you, if you've finished looking at the view.

SOL I've finished looking at the view.

Sound: a starter turning, a clean catch, an engine settling into a heavy even note

She gets down on her knees in the shed beside Ife and listens until she can hear the wrong note in the gearbox the way you hear a wrong note in a hymn, and says so.

SOL Saturday, then. The review. I'll have it all written before the hall sits.

IFE Not all of it.

SOL No. Not all of it.
