Half-Life Drift

Observational Core, Monday, early morning

Ambience: low hum of life support, cold air through the vents

The round chamber is dark except for cyan decay curves floating on the black glass walls. Dust turns slowly in the projector beams. It is cold enough to see breath.

In the centre, one worn chair. A rack of twelve sealed ampoules glows faintly blue beside it, each with its own small counter ticking.

On screen: Aris (charcoal coat buttoned to the throat, slate glowing in his hand, hollow-eyed)

ARIS (muttering) Cold. Good. Cold keeps me honest.

He breathes on his fingers and taps the slate awake.

ARIS Life support. Fifth below middle C. Same note since I was a boy.

He tilts his head and listens.

ARIS Except it isn't, this morning.

He taps the slate. A note joins a column of a thousand other notes.

ARIS A quarter tone flat. Logged. Everything gets logged.

ARIS (to himself) Somewhere in the logs there's a message. There always is, if you keep enough of them.

He turns to the ampoule rack. Twelve small glass vials, a gram of graphite in each, each watched by a counter that ticks at every blue flash of decay.

ARIS Good morning, carbon-fourteen. Fifty-seven hundred and thirty years, give or take forty. The most boring number on the ship.

ARIS You were boring when we left Sol. You were boring when my grandmother was born on Ring 2.

He pulls up the overnight counts. He stops.

ARIS (frowning) And you. What are you doing?

Close-up: The carbon-14 assay screen

The screen reads: HALF-LIFE ESTIMATE 5,729.83 YEARS. DEVIATION FROM REFERENCE: -0.003%. ALL TWELVE CHANNELS.

ARIS Not one channel. All twelve. Same size. Same phase.

ARIS A dirty detector does one channel. A warm housing does the top row. Not all twelve. Not in step.

He pulls the photomultiplier diagnostics, the thermal logs, the calibration history. One by one he swipes them away.

ARIS (faster now) Not the detector. Not the housing. Not heat. Not the clock.

He checks the time on three separate clocks. Three times he runs the numbers. Three times they agree.

ARIS (very quietly) Eleven years. Eleven years this assay has been as boring as a stone. And this morning it moves.

On screen: Aris (very still in the cold, the slate's light on his gaunt face)

He sits down in the worn chair and does not move for a while. His breath clouds and clears.

ARIS Three thousandths of a percent. A rounding error. A nothing.

ARIS Except nothing has moved in this number since before the ship was built.

He stands and drags the whole eleven-year trace onto the wall. Thousands of daily counts unroll across the black glass like a tide line.

ARIS Show me where you moved before. Show me the little ones I missed.

He writes a script. Then a longer one. Then a loop that checks every period from one day to four thousand.

ARIS (pacing) Come on. Come on. You don't wake up one morning and move. Nothing does. You've been practising.

Sound: a soft chime from the slate

A list of numbers appears in the air in front of him.

ARIS (reading) Two. Three. Five. Seven. Eleven. Thirteen. Seventeen. Nineteen.

He stops breathing for a moment.

ARIS Primes.

Picture: Aris standing alone in the Observational Core, a column of glowing prime numbers floating in front of his face in the blue dark.

ARIS (whispering) The gaps between the drifts are primes. In order. And each one a little shorter than the last.

ARIS Like something ticking down.

He checks them against everything he can name: the rotation of the ship, the reactor shield cycle, the counter calibration, the cleaning schedule of the vents.

ARIS The ship turns every ninety seconds. No. The shield cycles every six days. No. The vents, eleven days. Close. No.

ARIS Nothing matches. Nothing natural ticks like this.

He presses both palms flat on the cold console and laughs once, short and ugly.

ARIS Twenty years I said it. In papers. In meetings. In my own head at three in the morning. A world this finely set has a setter.

ARIS (eyes bright) And a setter leaves fingerprints.

He looks at the comm panel. His hand is already on it.

ARIS Vera. Vera, are you up? Of course you're up. I need you to look at something.

Bio-Integration Lab, Monday, morning

Ambience: incubator fans, the bubble of hydroponic trays

The long lab is bright and warm. Green light glows from the incubators. Assay plates shine pale yellow under a hood, and the air smells of agar and warm salt water.

Racks of sample tubes line the white benches. Along one wall, plants grow in hydroponic trays, their roots trailing in clear water.

On screen: Vera (sleeves rolled, blue-green stains to the elbow, stylus behind her ear, not looking up), Aris (on the wall screen, slate held to the camera, unshaven)

ARIS (eager) Two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, seventeen, nineteen.

VERA (pipetting) Morning to you too, Aris.

ARIS Two, three, five—

VERA I heard you the first time. Spacing of what to what?

ARIS Interval start to interval start. The gaps between drift episodes follow the primes. It's a signature.

VERA It's a fit to a sparse series.

ARIS Vera—

VERA (flat) You fed eleven years of counts to a sequence checker until the sequence checker found a sequence. How many periods are hiding in eleven years of daily data?

ARIS That isn't the—

VERA A lot, Aris. You found the one you went looking for.

ARIS (bitter) You're not listening.

VERA I'm listening perfectly.

She sets the pipette down and turns. Her stained hands rest on the bench. The stains have not come off in twenty years.

VERA I'm telling you I have my own anomaly. And mine has a mechanism.

On screen: Vera (turned to the screen, arms folded, serious), Aris (on the wall screen, leaning in)

She swipes the wall. A grid of enzyme curves replaces his face for a moment, then shrinks to share the screen with him.

VERA The eight hydrolases. The enzymes the gene banks use to turn stored food into food. Their curves have been flat for thirty years.

ARIS Boring.

VERA (nodding) Beautifully boring. I check them every Monday and I'm bored every Monday. It's my favourite part of the week.

VERA This week three of them speed up. Parts per million. Every replicate.

ARIS That's tiny.

VERA It is. And the DNA repair error rate in the tissue bank went up with it. A few more failed proofreads in ten million.

ARIS The kind of thing that takes a century to matter.

VERA A century isn't what worries me. The slope worries me.

She taps the curve. It glows where it bends.

VERA If enzyme rates drift, the way molecules hold on to each other is drifting. If that drifts, the forces holding proteins in their folds are drifting.

ARIS (finishing for her) Which means a constant has moved.

VERA Which means a constant has moved.

ARIS (triumphant) Which means someone moved it.

She looks at him for a long moment, then picks her pipette back up.

VERA (dry) It means the room changed temperature. Bodies adapt to that. So does software.

ARIS You don't even want to know why?

VERA I want to know how far. Why is a luxury for people with time.

ARIS That's the saddest thing a scientist's ever said to me.

VERA Then you don't spend enough time with scientists.

She goes back to her plates. Then she stops, pipette in the air.

VERA (thinking aloud) If the forces are shifting down at the particle level, where would it bite first?

ARIS The reactor.

VERA The blanket. The lithium. The neutron economy is the most sensitive scale on this ship.

She pulls up a feed from the reactor ring. Orange numbers march across the bench screen.

Close-up: The reactor blanket telemetry

A single line, highlighted in orange: LITHIUM-6 CAPTURE CROSS-SECTION: DRIFT +0.0021% (24H). FLAG: UNEXPLAINED.

VERA (softly) There you are.

ARIS (hungry) That's the same week. That's the same week, Vera.

VERA I can see the calendar, Aris.

On screen: Vera (staring at the orange numbers, still), Aris (on the wall screen, smiling for the first time in days)

VERA The enzymes, the decay rate, the blanket. Three scales that have never agreed on anything. All moving together.

ARIS Now do you want to know why?

VERA (not answering) Do me a favour. Don't tell anyone about the primes until I've measured how big this is.

ARIS (not quite answering) You'll want to see the full trace.

VERA Aris.

ARIS I'll send it over.

VERA That's not what I asked.

The wall screen goes dark.

Command Node, Monday, midday

Ambience: soft fans, a slow pulse of status lights

The compact room is deliberately warm and deliberately dim. The great white-line schematic of the Keres fills one wall. Rings 3 and 7 glow amber.

On screen: Kaelen (navy jacket, amber stripes, hands clasped behind his back, earpiece in, calm)

KAELEN Cortisol in the return air. Ring 3, up fourteen percent on the week. Ring 7, up nineteen.

WATCH OFFICER (off) Sleep efficiency's down in both, Chief. Corridor loiter time is up. Two hundred metres of Ring 7 have gone quiet on the acoustic monitors.

KAELEN Quiet because they're whispering.

WATCH OFFICER (off) About what?

KAELEN Doesn't matter yet. A body knows it's sick long before it can say where it hurts.

WATCH OFFICER (off) The security subsystem calls it early civil disorder. It recommends a visible patrol.

KAELEN Decline it.

WATCH OFFICER (off) Chief?

KAELEN (patient) A patrol is a signal of threat. Threat raises cortisol. Cortisol raises aggression. You don't put out a fever by heating the room.

He pulls the environmental map up over the schematic. Pressures, vent flows and light bands shimmer across the rings.

KAELEN Rings 3 and 7. Raise ambient pressure by one and a half percent.

KAELEN Slow the light cycle. Stretch the evening band by forty minutes. Cool the spectrum down toward four-eighty nanometres.

WATCH OFFICER (off) The blue, then?

KAELEN The blue for 7. Move the agricultural vents so Ring 3 gets the lavender banks.

KAELEN And bring up the oxytocin analog. Low draw. We already carry the stock.

WATCH OFFICER (off) The subsystem says—

KAELEN (unmoved) The subsystem says punish. I say modulate.

He watches the amber on the schematic.

KAELEN The rings are inflamed. It's an immune response, not a crime. We bring the swelling down. If it's still hot tomorrow, I cool the light further.

KAELEN I don't send in sticks.

On screen: Kaelen (one hand raised to the map, eyes on the cortisol curve)

On the map, Ring 3's corridors turn soft lilac. On a corridor camera, a crowd that stood shoulder to shoulder drifts, without knowing why, a little apart.

A man sets down a bag he has been gripping. A woman sits on a bench and rubs her eyes.

The cortisol curve bends.

WATCH OFFICER (off) It's working.

KAELEN (to himself) Hours. Not days.

He checks the chem-bank reservoir. It has dropped four percent in one watch.

KAELEN Log the reservoir draw. Every hour.

WATCH OFFICER (off) It's in the automatic logs, Chief.

KAELEN Log it by hand. A number I write down is a number I can't pretend I didn't choose.

He looks at the curve again. Something about it is wrong in a way he cannot name.

KAELEN (frowning) Thirty years I've read this chemistry. It has never once lied to me.

WATCH OFFICER (off) Is it lying now?

KAELEN No. It's just reading back like it was worked out in a room a quarter percent off from mine.

Sound: a two-tone alert from the reactor panel

WATCH OFFICER (off) Chief. Reactor reports a neutron flux anomaly.

Kaelen closes his eyes.

KAELEN (quietly) Of course it does.

KAELEN Tell them to log it. And tell the physics office I want a plain answer by end of watch. Not a paper.

He moves the lavender markers one ring inward and does not look at the reactor line again.

KAELEN (low) By end of watch.

Observational Core, Monday, night

The chamber is darker than in the morning. The decay curves have been replaced by one long trace and a column of primes running down the black glass.

On screen: Aris (coat open, collar loose, slate in both hands, alone)

ARIS (reciting) Two, three, five, seven, eleven, thirteen, seventeen, nineteen.

A message from Vera blinks on the slate: DON'T POST IT YET. I MEAN IT.

ARIS She means it.

He sets the slate down. Then picks it up again.

ARIS Physics office wants a plain answer by end of watch. The Chief wants a plain answer.

ARIS (bitter) Plain. As if it were plain.

He files the analysis in the physics office log, with his confidence intervals attached, the way the regulations say.

ARIS Filed. Correct. Proper. Nobody reads the physics log.

Then he opens the ship-wide board. Thousands of names glow in a list, the chatter of a normal Monday: a hydroponics swap, a choir rehearsal, a lost cat on Ring 4.

ARIS (softly) They have a right to know someone is out there.

ARIS Or they have a right to know I think so.

His thumb hovers over the screen.

On screen: Aris (lit from below by the slate, face hard and hopeful at once)

ARIS Eleven years of data. Twelve channels. Prime gaps, shrinking.

He types a title. Deletes it. Types another.

Close-up: Aris's post on the ship-wide board

The title reads: THE CONSTANTS ARE BEING EDITED. THE GAPS ARE PRIME. SOMEONE IS WRITING TO US.

ARIS (to the empty room) Patch notes. That's all they are. Somebody's patch notes.

He presses send.

Sound: the soft click of a message going out

For one second nothing happens. Then the first reply appears under his post. Then ten. Then a hundred, faster than he can read them.

ARIS (whispering) There. Now someone has to answer.
