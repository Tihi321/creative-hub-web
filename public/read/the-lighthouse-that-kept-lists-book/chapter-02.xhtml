Boats That Did Not Come Back

The machine typed at the speed of a careful old man, sixty words a minute if that. Mara read each line as it climbed out of the platen.

GALEB, 1961.
DVA BRATA, 1974.
SV. NIKOLA, 1983.
LJUBICA, NOBODY KNOWS WHEN.
MARICA (WENT TO ITALY).

The carriage returned. Paper advanced twice. The green lamp died, then the red, and the room held only the bora and her own breathing.

She had been eight. He had carved the boat from a piece of pine the length of her forearm, painted it blue, cut a sail from a tea towel and written MARICA on the stern in white — what he called her when nobody was listening. She had sailed it off the causeway on a string. The bora took the string. She cried until she was sick on his shoes, and he crouched down, hands on her shoulders, and told her very seriously that the boat had gone to Italy and would send a postcard.

Three weeks later, a postcard came. Ancona, a picture of a church, an Italian stamp. ARRIVED SAFELY. FOOD IS ALL RIGHT. MARICA.

She had never told anybody. Neither, as far as she knew, had he.

"Signal-to-noise," she said to the empty room, and found she had nothing after it.

Dino came at seven for the inventory and found her asleep sitting up on the crate, the paper in her lap. He stood in the doorway and cleared his throat until she woke.

"You stayed the night in a lighthouse."

"I stayed the night in a lighthouse."

"That is not on any form I have." He held out his hand. She gave him the list — no point pretending she hadn't been reading it all night.

He read standing. She watched him reach the third line and stop. His thumb moved over the paper, the way you smooth a child's hair.

"Sveti Nikola," he said.

"Did you know her?"

"She was my father's." He held the paper further from his face, as Ivka held the phone, though Dino didn't need glasses. "Twelfth of December. Bora. He went out for the nets because the nets cost more than the boat. They found the nets." He folded the strip along its length, carefully, then unfolded it because folding it had been a mistake. "The inquiry form was four pages. I've read it many times. It has a box for the cause and somebody wrote 'weather' in it."

The wind leaned on the door.

"Dino, I'm sorry."

"It was a long time ago." He wrote something on the clipboard — she was nearly certain it was nothing, just a line to give the pen something to do. "For the record, I am now assisting this investigation."

"Is there an investigation?"

"There is now." He looked at the last line. "Who is Marica?"

"A boat."

"Which went to Italy."

"It sent a postcard."

He looked at her. She looked at the receiver. He let it go, which she would remember later as the first nice thing anybody had done for her on Galebnjak.

The logbooks sat under the desk in a wooden crate stencilled DNEVNIK, forty of them, black oilcloth covers, one for every year from 1979 to the year before he died. Dino lifted the crate onto the desk and the desk groaned.

Each page a day. Wind, direction and force. Sea state. Ships that passed, by name when he could read it and by colour when he couldn't. Bulbs changed. And in between, lists. FIGS FOR KATICA. RING THE DOCTOR ABOUT KATICA. MARA'S BIRTHDAY, WHAT DOES A TEN YEAR OLD WANT, ASK IVKA. THINGS I SHOULD HAVE SAID TO MY FATHER, one item, crossed out so hard the pen went through.

"He wrote everything down," Dino said.

"He said if you write it down you don't have to carry it."

"Did it work?"

She turned a page. "Look at the size of this crate."

The last book was thin. The final entries leaned in a shakier hand, capitals propping each other up. On the inside of the back cover, in pencil, boxed twice:

HOME LINK.
45.45 BD. 170 HZ SHIFT.
TEST WITH I.

And a frequency in the two-metre band, which she copied onto the back of her hand.

"Forty-five point four five what?" said Dino.

"Baud. Symbols a second. Old teleprinter speed. Slow. And a hundred and seventy hertz between the two tones." She heard herself — talking the way she talked in meetings when somebody senior had made a mistake. "It's a private link. He set the receiver here to listen to something of his."

"Something where?"

"That's what I'm going to find out."

"And I?"

"You're the harbour master. You're Dino."

"Not me. I." He tapped the pencil. "Test with I."

"I don't know." She closed the book. "Could be anyone. Could be Italy."

Afternoon found her set up on Ante's kitchen table among the bin bags: laptop, a radio dongle the size of a lighter, a length of wire stuck to the window with masking tape. The waterfall display ran blue noise down the screen, nothing in it. She went through the drawers while she waited. Eleven screwdrivers. A jar labelled BUTTONS (OTHER PEOPLE'S). A steel tape measure, five metres, with BASIC scratched into the case.

She looked at the tape measure for a while. Then she went out to the shed for PVC pipe and hose clamps.

When it was done it was two crossed lengths of pipe with three pieces of tape measure clamped across them, cut with Ante's tin snips — which she was fairly sure he would have approved of in principle and objected to in practice. It looked like a fishbone made by somebody who had only heard fish described.

Dino arrived at half past nine with his clipboard in a plastic bag against the spray. He looked at the fishbone on the table.

"What is that?"

"A directional antenna. Hears best where it's pointing. If the signal's close, we point it, we listen, we go where it's loudest."

"It is a tape measure."

"It's three tape measures now."

He sat down, which she noted. She made coffee. At ten the waterfall lit up.

Two bright lines appeared side by side, a hundred and seventy hertz apart, and the level meter jumped so hard she reached to turn the gain down. She checked it. Checked it again.

"That's not Split," she said. Split through a wire on a window would have been a whisper. This was shouting. Whoever was sending was close enough to hit with a stone.

She could have decoded it. Software on the laptop, and she watched the two lines dance and did not start it. She wanted to read it on the paper, the way he had meant it to be read.

"Last night it ran about five minutes," she said, already pulling on the raincoat. "Most of that's tuning at the start. Bring the fish."

Dino held the antenna upright in both hands, solemn as a processional cross on the saint's day. She kept the handheld receiver. They ran down the steps to the riva. The bora came along the harbour in hard flat blasts, sea smoke blowing over the bollards like steam off a pot.

The receiver in her hand sang: two tones alternating, a sound like a very small bird being very insistent.

"Turn," she said. "Slowly. Left. Stop."

The tones swelled. Dino turned. They faded. He turned back.

"There. That way."

They went that way. Past Dino's office, dark. Past the café with its chained chairs. The tones grew stronger. A shutter banged somewhere above them like a gunshot, and Dino flinched but did not drop the antenna — which she thought said a great deal about him. The RYRYRY run ended and the data began, quick and burbling, the list spooling out along the causeway to the lighthouse with nobody there to read it.

"Three minutes," she said.

"I know where this goes," said Dino.

"Keep pointing."

He kept pointing. The fishbone came round, steadied, held, and the receiver screamed so loud she pressed it to her chest to muffle it. They had reached the end of the riva, where the alley ran up behind the only door on the harbour that ever had a light behind it.

The front of the shop was dark. Round the back, at the small window over the bins, a light was on.
