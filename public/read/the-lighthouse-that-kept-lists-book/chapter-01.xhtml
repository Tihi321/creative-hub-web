Bread, Lemons

The ferry left Split at two and reached Galebnjak at a quarter to five. Summer they called it the afternoon crossing. November it was just the ferry.

Mara stood on the open deck. The cabin reeked of diesel and somebody's sardines, and her grandfather's yellow raincoat swallowed her whole — sleeves dangling past her fingertips, shoulders collapsing. She rolled the cuffs twice. They unrolled themselves, the way his used to when he got going on an explanation.

The bora had blown two days straight. It came off the mainland in shoves, combing the sea into white stripes. The ferry took it broadside with a groan she felt in her back teeth. A man beside her with a crate of chickens said this was nothing. The chickens disagreed.

Galebnjak rose low and grey: harbour first, then houses stacked up the hill, then — at the end of the long stone causeway — the lighthouse, white as a tooth. Not lit yet. It switched on at dusk by itself, photocell and relay her grandfather had replaced in '97 and described to her every summer since.

On the riva the cafés had their chairs stacked on tables and chained together, as if the furniture might otherwise make a run for it. Nobody met her. She'd told nobody which day she was coming, which on Galebnjak meant everybody knew. Across the harbour, behind the one lit window, a curtain moved and fell still.

The house smelled of him. Pipe tobacco he'd quit in 2004. Oil. Oranges. Lists on every wall. On the fridge: BULBS 24V. COFFEE. RING MARA SUNDAY. By the door, the tide table annotated in his hand. Over the bed, in a frame, a list headed THINGS NOT WORTH SHOUTING ABOUT, fourteen items long. The first: The bora.

She'd brought a roll of bin bags, a roll of masking tape, the number of an estate agent in Split. Set all three on the kitchen table and looked at them. Then went to bed in the raincoat, because the stove needed wood and the wood was outside and the outside was the bora.

The knock came at eight Tuesday.

Dino Kovač filled the doorway, collar turned up, clipboard pressed flat to his chest like a breastplate. A big man the wind had made smaller. Salt in his moustache.

"Mara Bašić," he said, then checked the clipboard to be sure. "Granddaughter of Ante Bašić. Arrived Monday, sixteen forty-five ferry."

"Morning, Dino."

"For the record, I am here in my official capacity."

"Want coffee in your official capacity?"

He considered. "After."

He slid a strip of paper from under the clip. Teleprinter paper, narrow and yellowed, torn at both ends. Capitals punched into it more than printed, a little uneven — like a careful man typing with cold hands.

BREAD.
LEMONS.
ONE APOLOGY TO IVKA.

"Where's this from?"

"The lighthouse. The machine in the service room." He turned the clipboard round, showed her a form with numbered boxes. "I went at seven to do the inventory for Friday. Item fourteen. Teleprinter, one. Condition: I wrote 'dead'. Now I have to cross it out, and I hate crossing things out."

"So it's not dead."

"It is printing shopping." He studied her face. "Twenty years, Mara, that machine says nothing. Then you step off the ferry and the same night it wants lemons."

"You think it's me."

"I think you are a radio engineer."

"I was asleep. In a coat." She handed the strip back. "It's a prank. Kids. Somebody's found an old transmitter in a garage and they're winding you up."

"Kids." Dino looked up the street. Empty except for a cat and a plastic bag the wind walked like a dog. "The youngest person on this island is my nephew Luka. He is forty-one. He mends nets. His idea of a prank is doing my crossword in pen."

She made coffee on the gas ring. He drank it standing, as if a chair would commit him to something, and laid out Friday. A Plovput crew on the Friday ferry. A solar beacon on a galvanised pole, LED, twelve volts, serviced once a year from Split. The old lantern switched off, the old equipment trolleyed down the causeway and taken away. Unless the harbour master signed otherwise.

He said signed otherwise very quietly.

"And will you?"

"Why would I? The light is old. The machine is old. Now the machine is ordering lemons." He leafed through the clipboard to file the strip, forms flipping past: berthing, fuel, lost property, and one headed PROPOSAL FOR REGISTRATION OF A CULTURAL GOOD, every box empty, corners soft from carrying. He turned past that one fast.

"What's that?"

"A form. I have a form for everything."

"Ever filled it in?"

"Not yet." He rinsed his cup at the sink — her grandfather would have liked that — and set it upside down on the draining board. "Thank you for the coffee. Please don't print anything tonight."

She photographed the strip before he took it. Told herself it was for evidence.

Ivka's shop was the only door on the riva with a light behind it. A bell rang when Mara pushed inside. Bread and paraffin and dried figs strung on a cord behind the till. Rope, batteries, washing powder, three kinds of tinned fish and one kind of everything else. Ivka sat behind the counter in a black cardigan, reading glasses on a chain, doing nothing in a way that looked exactly like surveillance.

"Mala," she said. "You're thin."

"Same as August."

"You were thin in August."

Mara set a loaf on the counter. Then, because the strip of paper had lodged in her head like a jingle off the radio, two lemons.

Ivka looked at the lemons. Looked at Mara over her glasses.

"Who's drinking tea?"

Mara held out her phone. Ivka took it, arm fully extended, and read. Her lips moved on the last line. She handed it back screen down.

"Bread, lemons," she said. "Half the island buys bread and lemons."

"On a Monday?"

"Who said Monday?"

"It printed Monday night."

Ivka rang up the loaf on a till older than Mara. "Your grandfather bought bread and two lemons every Monday for thirty-five years. Then Katica died, and he bought bread and two lemons every Monday for fifteen more." She slipped the lemons into a paper bag and folded the top over twice, sharp. "I asked him once. He said she liked lemon in her tea. I said, Ante, she's not drinking tea. He said, you don't know that."

Mara looked at the bag.

"And the apology?"

"What apology?"

"One apology to Ivka."

"Many people owe me apologies, mala. I don't keep a list." She took Mara's coins and counted change out of a biscuit tin. "Eleven forty."

"Ivka. Do you know who's doing this?"

"Doing what?" Ivka was already looking past her at the window, where the bora threw spray over the bollards. "You'll want batteries. The power goes when it blows like this."

Mara bought batteries.

The causeway: four hundred metres of fitted stone with an iron rail on the sea side. In the bora, four hundred metres of being shoved. She walked it bent double with the bread under the raincoat. Salt dried on her lips. At the end — the rock, the tower, the steel door with its padlock. The key where it had been all her life, under the third flowerpot, in which nothing had grown since the eighties.

The service room was round and cold and smelled of hot dust. The teleprinter squatted on a steel desk: grey, the size of a sewing machine, keyboard and paper roll feeding up through it. Beside it stood the receiver, a cream metal box with one large dial and two small lamps, one red, one green. Under each lamp, a scrap of masking tape in her grandfather's capitals. MARK. SPACE.

A telephone socket on the wall, cable cut years ago and the end taped over. The teleprinter didn't need it. A cable ran from the receiver to a small box between the two machines, another climbed the wall and went up through the ceiling to the aerial on the gallery. Every switch on. Of course. Ante never turned off anything that might come in useful later.

She talked herself through it, because that helped.

"Two tones. Frequency-shift keying. One tone's a one, the other's a zero, five bits a letter. The box turns tones into pulses and the pulses drive the printer. Somebody somewhere has a transmitter on this frequency." Her voice too big for the curved walls. "And a sense of humour."

She went back to the house for the stove, a thermos, returned after dark. Above her the lamp went round and round, beam going out over the water and coming back. She sat on an upturned crate in the raincoat. Nine o'clock. The bora leaned on the door like somebody waiting to be let in. Nine thirty. She thought about the summer she was eight, when he'd shown her the two lamps and told her they were a married couple who had to agree before anyone could speak.

At ten the red lamp flickered.

Then the green. Then both together, steady and equally bright, and the teleprinter woke with a crash like a filing cabinet falling downstairs.

RYRYRYRYRYRY

The carriage slammed back. The paper jumped a line. Mara stood up so fast the crate went over.

BOATS THAT DID NOT COME BACK.
