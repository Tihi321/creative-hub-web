A List for Mara

"I'm coming," said Ivka.

"It's four hundred metres of wet stone," said Dino.

"I've walked wet stone for eighty years."

"Not that stone. Not at night. You're a resident in a high-risk category."

"Which category?"

He checked the clipboard. "Over seventy-five."

"Then I've less to lose than you," said Ivka, and that settled it, except she still had to sign for the life jacket.

The orange one from the harbour office, made for a fisherman twice her size. Over her black cardigan she looked like a very small, very cross buoy. She signed with a flourish, as if it were a treaty.

Luka got the back room. Forty-one, large, gentle, a crossword tucked under his arm. Ivka stood over him at the card table, the cassette marked LAST in her hand.

"Ten o'clock," she said. "Play. Stop when it stops. Don't touch the knobs."

"I know, teta Ivka."

"Luka. This means you."

The bora had blown itself down to a stiff wind by day, which on Galebnjak counted as calm. They walked the causeway three abreast, Ivka in the middle with a hand through each of their arms, the lamp sweeping them. Halfway, at the third step down to the water, Ivka stopped and looked at the sea. Then she went on without saying why.

At the door Dino bent, tipped the third flowerpot, and came up with the key, like he'd been doing it for years. Mara decided he probably had.

In the service room he righted the crate, folded his jacket on it, and Ivka sat like a queen on a fish box. Mara stood by the teleprinter. Dino planted himself by the door, in case someone needed stopping.

"What if Luka presses the wrong button?" he said.

"There are two buttons," said Ivka.

"He's my nephew."

At ten the red lamp lit, then the green, then both steadied and agreed, and the machine woke with its crash.

RYRYRYRYRYRY

Ivka flinched. "Every night it does that?"

"It's a test pattern. Works every part of the printer so you know it's all..." Mara heard herself and stopped.

FOR MARA.

The paper climbed. Nobody moved.

1. DO NOT SELL THE HOUSE TO THE MAN FROM SPLIT. HE WANTS TO PUT IN A JACUZZI.
2. THE LIGHT RELAY IS 24V. SPARES IN THE JAR MARKED RELAYS. NOT THE JAR MARKED BUTTONS.
3. THE STOVE DRAWS BETTER WITH THE WINDOW OPEN ONE FINGER.
4. MARICA DID NOT GO TO ITALY. I FOUND HER IN THE MORNING IN PIECES UNDER THE CAUSEWAY. I COULD NOT MEND HER AND I COULD NOT GIVE YOU PIECES. I PUT A STONE IN HER AND SANK HER BY THE THIRD STEP. THE POSTCARD WAS CAPTAIN BOZIC ON THE ANCONA BOAT. I DICTATED. SORRY. SAME AS IVKA.
5. EAT. IVKA SAYS YOU ARE THIN.
6. YOU CAN STOP EXPLAINING THINGS NOW. I KNOW HOW IT WORKS. I BUILT IT.
7. MONDAYS, BREAD AND TWO LEMONS. YOU DON'T KNOW SHE ISN'T.
NONO

The carriage returned. The paper fed up twice. The lamps died, green then red.

"Captain Božić," said Ivka. "I knew it. His wife said he had a girlfriend in Ancona."

Mara tore the paper at the bar. Her hands were steady. She noticed, and it seemed important, so she held the sheet flat with both palms and read number six again.

"The tape's a recording of the audio," she said. "He'd have generated the tones on the old terminal unit at the house. It's in the cupboard under the stairs. Recorded them onto the cassette through the line out. Then the recorder at Ivka's plays them into the microphone socket of the radio, and the radio sends them up the wire on her roof, so it's..."

"Mala," said Ivka.

"...so it's actually a very simple..."

"Mara," said Dino, and held out a handkerchief. Ironed. He'd brought it in a plastic bag with the clipboard.

She took it. The explaining ran out somewhere around the microphone socket, and what came after was wet and undignified and went on until the handkerchief was no longer ironed. The wind circled the tower. The lamp circled above it. Ivka studied the ceiling. Dino studied his clipboard.

When she finished she gave the handkerchief back, and he folded it and filed it away.

Then she reached up to the shelf over the desk. Two jam jars, side by side, labels in his capitals. BUTTONS. RELAYS. The buttons jar was full to the lid. In the other, wrapped in newspaper from 2009, four small black relays with 24V stamped on their cases. She held one to the bulb. Contacts clean, because of course they were.

"Good?" said Dino.

"Four spares. Clean."

Ivka got off the crate without being asked. Mara pulled it to the desk and sat at the teleprinter keyboard.

"Does it go anywhere?" said Dino.

"No. The receiver here can't send. It'll only print on this paper." She switched to local. The motor hummed. "Doesn't matter."

She typed with two fingers, slowly, the way he had, keys coming down hard, letters rising in capitals from the platen.

FOR NONO.
1. HOUSE NOT FOR SALE.
2. SPARES FOUND. FOUR. NOT THE BUTTONS.
3. WINDOW OPEN ONE FINGER.
4. I KNEW ABOUT THE POSTCARD. BOATS CANNOT WRITE. I WAS EIGHT, NOT STUPID.
5. SAME AS IVKA.
MARA

She tore it off and looked. Then she slipped it into the logbook crate, on top of the forty black books, where whoever looked next would find it.

Ivka had been reading over her shoulder, rustling in her life jacket. "Good," she said. "Now help me back over that thing. I want to sit down somewhere with a back."

The Plovput crew arrived on Friday's ferry: two men in orange jackets, a trolley, a galvanised pole lashed across the foredeck like a lance. The ferry swung against the ramp at a quarter to five. The light had come on twenty minutes before, by itself, its beam sweeping their heads as they stepped off.

Dino met them at the bottom of the ramp with the clipboard.

He'd been in his office since six. Mara knew because she'd been there since half past, in the yellow raincoat, signing where he pointed. PROPOSAL FOR REGISTRATION OF A CULTURAL GOOD. Every box filled. Name of the good: Galebnjak Lighthouse Radio Station. Description: lighthouse with historic radio-teletype receiving station, in working order, demonstrated. Responsible technician, volunteer: Mara Bašić. He'd sent it to the conservation office in Split at seven, with a request for preventive protection, phoned at eight to make sure someone had read it, at nine to make sure they'd read all of it.

"Preventive protection," said the older man, reading. "So we can't touch anything."

"Until it's assessed," said Dino.

"How long's that?"

"It's November," said Dino. "They're in Split."

The man looked up at the lamp sweeping the causeway, at the pole on the foredeck, at Mara in the raincoat. Then he took a pen from his jacket and signed the bottom of Dino's form. Dino took it back and clipped it at the front, where the berthing forms used to be.

The ferry turned at a quarter past five. That gave the crew half an hour in Ivka's shop, drinking her coffee standing up. Each left with a paper bag of lemons. Mara watched Ivka reach to the bottom of the box for them.

The pole stayed on the riva. Someone would come back for it in spring.

That evening Mara walked the causeway with bread under her raincoat. At the third step she stopped and looked down. The water was black and showed nothing, which she found she didn't mind.

In the service room she took a new notebook from her pocket, black oilcloth, from Ivka's shelf between the batteries and the candles. She wrote the date at the top of the first page, in capitals, because that was how it was done.

LIGHT ON 16.25.
WIND N, DROPPING.
TELEPRINTER OK.
BREAD. TWO LEMONS.
