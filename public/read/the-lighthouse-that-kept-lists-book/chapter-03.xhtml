The Back Room

The back door was not locked. Nothing on Galebnjak was locked except the lighthouse, and the key to that was under a flowerpot.

Mara pushed it open and the noise came out to meet them: two tones, high and low, squabbling through a small tinny speaker. The back room was narrow and warm and smelled of paraffin. Shelves of tinned tomatoes went up to the ceiling. A calendar from 2011 hung crooked on a nail. A paraffin heater ticked in the corner. On a card table under the window sat a silver cassette recorder, the kind with a carrying handle, and beside it a black radio with a microphone on a hook and a cable running from the recorder into the microphone socket. Another cable went up the wall and out through a hole drilled in the window frame.

Ivka sat on a kitchen chair beside the table in her black cardigan and a knitted hat. She had a cup of coffee in one hand. With the other she was holding the cassette recorder still, as though it might wander off.

She looked at Mara. She looked at Dino, and at the fishbone in his arms.

"Shut the door," she said. "You're letting the bora in."

Dino shut the door.

The tones went on. Mara stood dripping on the lino and listened to them as if she had never heard anything like them, though she had heard them every working day of her adult life.

"It sounds like a kettle arguing," said Ivka.

"It's frequency-shift keying."

"That's what I said."

The tones stopped. The tape hissed. Ivka pressed a button with one finger, firmly, and the hiss stopped too. She put the coffee down.

"You'll want coffee," she said.

There was a shoebox on the shelf behind her, between the tinned tomatoes and a box of candles. Ivka took it down and put it on the table, and Mara read the lid. It was in her grandfather's capitals, in black marker, underlined twice.

ONE A NIGHT, WHEN MARA COMES HOME.
NOT BEFORE.
PLAY. STOP. DO NOT TOUCH THE KNOBS.
IVKA THIS MEANS YOU.

Inside were four cassettes in a row, numbered on the spines. One, two and three had been turned round in their cases so they faced the wrong way, which Mara understood was Ivka's filing system. The fourth said LAST.

"March," said Ivka. "He came in for lemons. He was already thin by then, you saw him at Easter. He had that radio under one arm and this under the other, and he took over my back room for an afternoon like it was his own house. He put a wire on my roof. He told me it was for the television."

"Test with I," said Dino.

They both looked at him.

"In the logbook. Test with I." His neck went pink. "For the record."

"He made me press play," said Ivka. "Then he walked all the way out to the lighthouse in the rain and walked all the way back, and he was so pleased with himself he bought two lemons he already had." She folded her hands on the lid of the box. "Two buttons. Play. Stop. That's all he taught me. He said, when Mara comes home, one a night, ten o'clock. He said don't tell her."

"How did you know I'd come?"

"Monday, a yellow coat gets off the ferry." Ivka shrugged. "Who else wears that coat? You can see it from Italy."

"And why not tell me?"

"He said if you tell her, she'll explain it."

Mara opened her mouth and shut it again.

"So you've been sitting here," said Dino slowly, "every night, sending messages to the lighthouse."

"I have been sitting here with a cup of coffee pressing a button. What it sends, how would I know? It's a kettle. I never knew a single word." For the first time that week Ivka sounded offended, not with them but with the situation. "Eighty years on this island. I know who is in debt and who is in love and who is putting water in the wine. And this, the one thing, he puts in a kettle."

"The first one said bread and lemons," said Mara.

"I saw. On your telephone." Ivka looked at the box. "And one apology to Ivka. One. Fifty-five years, and he gives me one."

"What was it for?"

Ivka picked up her coffee, found it was cold, and drank it anyway.

"In 1971 there was a dance in Split," she said. "The Saturday before Easter. A proper one, with a band from Zagreb. He asked me in January. In January! Three months I had to wait. I bought a dress in Split, apricot, with a belt, and shoes that hurt. Saturday comes, I'm on the riva for the ferry, everybody's on the riva for the ferry. No Ante. The ferry goes. I go home and take off the shoes." She put the cup down. "He said the light needed him. There were two keepers on that rock in those days, mala. The light did not need him."

"What did you do?"

"I married Frane the next spring. Frane danced like a goat falling off a wall, God rest him, and I loved him thirty-eight years." She shrugged. "Ante came to the wedding. He married Katica. We all grew old. He bought his lemons. He never said one word about it, not one, not in fifty-five years. And now," she flicked the shoebox, "a kettle says he's sorry."

The heater ticked. Outside, the bora found a loose gutter and played it.

"Tonight's," said Mara. "Number three. It's printed by now."

Ivka looked at her over her glasses.

"Well?" she said. "Go and get it. I'm eighty. I don't walk on that thing in a bora."

The causeway at half past ten was a long dark shove, the lamp going round overhead and turning the spray white each time it passed. Dino walked on the sea side, between Mara and the rail. She noticed and decided not to mention it.

The paper had come up a hand's width out of the teleprinter and stopped. Mara tore it off along the bar and read it under the bare bulb, and then she read it again, and then she handed it to Dino without a word, and he read it with his lips moving, the way Ivka had.

It was ten to midnight when they got back to the shop. Ivka had not moved, except to make fresh coffee and to put out a plate of dry figs, which on Galebnjak was the equivalent of a brass band.

"Read it," she said. "I left my glasses somewhere."

The glasses were on the chain round her neck. Nobody said so.

Mara read it.

FOR IVKA. ONE APOLOGY, IN PARTS.
1. 1971. THE DANCE. THE LIGHT DID NOT NEED ME. I SAID IT DID.
2. I WAS AFRAID OF YOU. YOU WERE THE BEST DANCER ON THE ISLAND AND I DANCE LIKE A WINCH.
3. I SAW THE DRESS FROM THE GALLERY WITH THE BINOCULARS. APRICOT. IT WAS VERY GOOD.
4. FRANE WAS THE RIGHT MAN. I SAID SO AT THE WEDDING BUT THE BAND WAS TOO LOUD.
5. FOR FIFTY YEARS YOU GAVE ME THE GOOD LEMONS FROM THE BOTTOM OF THE BOX. I KNEW.
6. SORRY.
ANTE

Nobody spoke. Dino studied his clipboard. The heater ticked.

"A winch," said Ivka at last. "That's true. He danced like a winch. I saw him at Frane's cousin's wedding. A winch with a bad bearing."

"Ivka."

"Binoculars." She shook her head. "Fifty-five years. The old goat." She held out her hand, and Mara gave her the paper, and Ivka folded it in four and put it into the pocket of her cardigan without looking at it. "Read number five again."

Mara didn't need the paper. "For fifty years you gave me the good lemons from the bottom of the box. I knew."

"I give everybody the good lemons," said Ivka.

She took the fourth cassette out of the shoebox and turned it over in her fingers. LAST, it said, and nothing else.

"Tomorrow," she said. "It says one a night."

Dino had the clipboard pressed flat against his chest, the way he had held it on Mara's doorstep, except that now it looked less like a breastplate and more like something he was keeping warm. In ten minutes it would be Thursday. The crew came on Friday's ferry. Somewhere under the berthing forms and the fuel forms was the one with the soft corners, and Mara watched him not take it out.

"For the record," he said, and then, it seemed, could not think what the record wanted, and ate a fig instead.
