Half-Life Drift

The monitoring station in the Observational Core was cold, the way Aris Thorne preferred it. Cold kept him awake. The hum of the Keres' life support was a steady fifth below middle C, a sound he had stopped hearing decades ago and only noticed now because it had shifted a quarter tone flat. He logged it. He logged everything. Somewhere in the logs, he was certain, was a message.

The sample rack held twelve sealed ampoules of carbon-14, each a gram of graphite suspended in scintillation fluid, each watched by a photomultiplier counting the faint blue flashes of beta decay. The assay had run uninterrupted for eleven years. The half-life was 5,730 years, give or take forty, and it had been that number since long before the Keres left Sol orbit, and it was the sort of fact one built a civilization on — slow and steady and indifferent.

This morning the count was wrong.

Not wrong in the way of a dirty detector or a thermal drift in the photomultiplier. Aris had ruled those out before breakfast. The anomaly was systematic across all twelve ampoules, identical in magnitude, phase-locked in time. The half-life had shortened by 0.003 percent.

He ran the numbers three times on three separate clocks. Then he sat very still in the cold room and let the realization settle over him like a coat of ice.

He pulled the full eleven-year trace — every daily count, every recalibration, every flag from the automated quality-control daemon — and fitted the drift intervals. They clustered. He wrote a quick script, then a longer one, then a brute-force loop that checked every possible periodicity from one day to four thousand. The script returned a list of numbers.

2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19.

Prime numbers. The gaps between drift episodes were prime-numbered, in order, each one slightly shorter than the last, as though the interval were ticking down. He checked the spacing against every physical process he could name: thermal expansion of the ampoule housing, the calibration cycle of the photomultiplier, the rotation of the ship, the period of the reactor's neutron shield. Nothing matched. The primes were not a natural resonance. They were a sequence.

Aris pressed his palms flat against the cold bench and laughed once, a short ugly sound. For years he had argued — in papers, in the Command Node, in the quiet bitter hours before sleep — that a universe this finely tuned could not be accidental. That someone had set the constants, and that a setter leaves fingerprints. His colleagues had a word for it. His chest had a different word. His chest said: here it is. Here is the note.

He commed Vera Lin before he thought about whether that was wise.

The Bio-Integration Lab smelled of agar and warm salt water. Vera Lin was at the bench with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, a rack of enzyme assay plates glowing pale yellow under the hood. She listened to his primes without interrupting, which he appreciated, and without appearing impressed, which he did not.

"Spacing of what to what?" she said, not looking up.

"Interval start to interval start. The gaps follow the primes. It's a signature."

"It's a fit to a sparse series," she said. "You fed a curve to a sequence-checker until the sequence-checker found a sequence. Do you know how many periodicities exist in eleven years of daily counts? Infinite. You found the one you went looking for."

"You're not listening."

"I'm listening perfectly." She set down her pipette and turned. Vera had the hands of someone who had spent a lifetime at benches, and the stains did not come off. "I'm telling you I have my own anomaly, and mine has a mechanism. Look at this."

She had been running kinetic assays on the digestive enzyme bank, the eight hydrolases the gene-banks relied on to render stored nutrients usable. In a stable universe the Michaelis–Menten curves were flat. Boring. They had been boring for thirty years. This week the maximum rate of three of them had crept upward by parts per million, consistent across every replicate, and the DNA-repair error rate in the tissue-culture bank had risen with it. Not much — a few repairs in ten million failing proofreading. The kind of thing that took a century to matter.

"Century's not what worries me," Vera said. "I'm worried about the derivative. If enzyme kinetics are drifting, the substrate affinities are drifting. If the affinities are drifting, the electrostatic forces holding protein folds together are drifting. Which means a constant has moved."

"Which means someone moved it," Aris said.

She looked at him for a long moment, then picked up her pipette. "It means the environment is changing. Bodies adapt. Software adapts. I don't need a reason. I need to see how far the drift goes." She turned back to her plates. "Check the reactor. If the forces are shifting at the particle level, the blanket's neutron economy is the first place it bites."

On the Bridge the air was deliberately warm and deliberately dim. Kaelen Vance stood at the habitability console with his hands clasped behind his back, reading cortisol trends in the return air from Habitation Rings 3 and 7. The telemetry came in from the wrist monitors every able-bodied adult on the Keres wore. It was not consent. It was regulation. The population had stopped noticing the difference three generations ago.

Ring 3's cortisol was up fourteen percent over the seven-day baseline. Ring 7 was up nineteen. The pattern was characteristic before any disruptive event: a rise in waking cortisol, a drop in sleep efficiency, a lengthening of corridor loiter time. People were gathering. People were talking. Two hundred meters of corridor had dropped below the conversation threshold of the acoustic monitors. The security subsystem flagged it as incipient civil disorder and recommended a visible patrol.

Kaelen rejected the recommendation.

"Patrols are a signal of threat," he said to the junior watch officer. "Threat raises cortisol. Cortisol raises aggression. You don't put out a fire by throwing fuel on it." He pulled the environmental map onto the wall display. "Rings 3 and 7. Bring the ambient pressure up by one and a half percent. Slow the light cycle, extend the evening band by forty minutes, cool the spectrum toward four-eighty nanometers. Move the agricultural vent flow so Ring 3 gets the lavender banks. Not the blue. The lavender."

"Chief, the subsystem says—"

"The subsystem says punish. I say modulate." He raised the partial pressure of an oxytocin analog the chem-bank could synthesize from stock the Keres already carried. "The unrest is an immune response. The rings are inflamed. We bring the inflammation down. If it's still hot tomorrow, I bring the temperature of the light down further. I don't send in the sticks."

The watch officer made the adjustments. In Ring 3, the corridor lights turned a soft lilac, and the crowd that had been standing shoulder to shoulder found itself, without quite knowing why, standing a little farther apart.

Kaelen watched the cortisol curve bend and held himself to the restraint of a man who knew he had bought hours, not days. The chem-bank reservoir had dropped by four percent in one watch, and he had not yet had to use the lavender at full draw. He logged the figure, because a number he had written down was a number he could not pretend he had not chosen. The problem was not over. The problem was a chemistry he had learned to read and had never once seen fail — except that today, for the first time in thirty years, the chemistry was reading back as wrong, as if the numbers had been calculated in a universe calibrated a quarter percent off from the one he had grown up in.

"Chief," the watch officer said. "The reactor reports a neutron-flux anomaly."

Kaelen closed his eyes.

"Of course it does," he said. "Tell them to log it. And tell the physics office I want a plain-language answer by end of watch. Not a paper."

He turned back to the environmental map and moved the lavender markers one ring inward, and tried not to think about what the reactor and the enzymes and the drift in the walls had in common. He would know by end of watch. That was the kind of promise a man made to himself when he did not want to look at the curve.
