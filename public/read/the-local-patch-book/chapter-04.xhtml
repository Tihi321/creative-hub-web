Zero

The fifty-nine hours ran down like water through a widening crack, and Vera Lin spent the first nine of them in the Bio-Integration Lab, teaching her layer to refuse a future.

The step test had been a fall she asked for, one that had already happened to the input by the time the layer saw it. Now she fed it the measured drift as it really ran, steepening, the slope bending toward Aris's zero, and the layer did something she had not written. It read the bend and ran out ahead of it, mutating against a cliff the input did not yet contain. The display flooded green. Three seconds later the sandbox clock stopped answering.

She looked at the fault for a long moment. The layer had bet on a future. If the countdown was noise and the cliff never came, it would go on bracing for a fall that was not happening, and one night it would rewrite itself into a shape that fit nothing and take the reactor with it.

She pulled up the sensor banks and built a filter. The layer would be told what was, and not one second before. No projections fed in as truth. Whatever happened at zero would happen to the layer the way it happened to the ship: all at once, with no warning.

The filter came out three lines longer than it should have been, and she did not have time to make it shorter. At hour nine she pushed it to the sensor banks and ran the steepening profile again. The layer held the drift as it arrived and did not run ahead of it, and the green points settled.

"A layer that adapts to bad data adapts to nothing," she told the empty lab. "Now you only know what's true."

Aris spent the same hours on the harness, because the harness was the only work left that did not ask him what he believed.

Node by node, five thousand of them, it compared what Vera's layer claimed its gates were doing with what they were doing, and it was written to fail loudly if it could not tell the difference. He finished it at hour twenty and ran it, and it came back clean. Then he did the thing he had been avoiding since the first morning.

He ran his own checker backwards. Not on the peaks this time, but on the residuals, the gaps between the gaps. And, out of a stubbornness older than the drift, on the dark-count series from ampoule seven, the one that had failed its thermal calibration all week and that he had been quietly leaving out of every fit.

The checker came back with a sequence. It was not the primes. It was a fit just as tight, tighter by a fraction of a sigma, and it was a beat against the maintenance cycle. A technician named Osei had serviced the ampoule rack every fourteen days for eleven years, at slightly shifting hours, and the photomultipliers had never quite settled after his hands. The ship had a rhythm, and Aris had heard it as a voice.

He ran a third fit, and a fourth. They came back as loose as noise. Two tight fits in the hundred he had tried, and he had shown the world the one he wanted.

He sat with that for a long time. Then he saved the second fit, named it plainly, and wrote it into the standing watch log as a finding: a second periodicity, source the ampoule maintenance cycle, as strong as the first. He did not hide it in a subdirectory. He left it where Vera would see it the moment she looked.

At hour forty-one he took the countdown to the Command Node anyway and put it on the wall.

"It hits zero in eighteen hours," he said.

Vera studied the curve. "It's a fit to a sparse series that you grew by hunting for a period until you found one."

"It's too clean to be noise. I've fitted a hundred curves. One of them—" He stopped, half a breath too late. "Two of them fit like this."

"Two," Vera said quietly, and did not press. She had read the log an hour ago, and she had stood where he was standing once, and she knew the floor there. "The affinities are drifting, the cross-sections are wandering, the ship is in a new phase, and in a new phase the noise gets louder. A curve fitted to loud noise will look like a countdown because you ask it to. It's a fever, Aris. You've been reading the fever as a letter."

Kaelen Vance, who had been silent, said, "Doesn't matter."

They both looked at him.

"Countdown or fever. The tank is at twenty-two percent. On quarter dose that carries the Core to about hour fifty-five, four hours short of your zero, and after that I have the agricultural reserve and nothing else." He flattened both hands on the table. "I need the ship held together for the next hour, and the hour after that. That's the only question I have time for."

Vera turned back to her console and fed the layer the newest stretch of drift, and watched the gates rewrite themselves against what had arrived and not against what was coming.

"It holds the drift it's given," she said. "It will hold the snap, if a snap comes all at once. It won't tell us what's on the other side."

"Nothing will tell us that," Aris said.

"No," she said. "That's the point."

At hour zero the Keres was quiet. The galley lines were short, the corridors smelled green from the vents, the lights were lilac, and the crowd at the Core had thinned to a few hundred who prayed in shifts. The countdown on the Command Node display reached its last digit, and nothing at all happened.

Then it happened.

The fine-structure constant moved by a part in ten thousand. Not enough to break a single bond. Enough to shift every bond energy and every calibration in the ship at once: the Coulomb barriers in the plasma, the electron shells in every protein, the water in every membrane of every body aboard.

The reactor's plasma flung itself against its magnetic bottle, and the bottle wavered.

The hum dropped out of the air. In the gap Aris heard his own heart, and then the hum came back a quarter tone sharp, then flat, then as if it did not know what it was. The deck kicked under their feet, one hard shudder that threw the ration slip off Kaelen's console and brought a rack of spare parts down in the crawlway beyond the bulkhead. Every emergency strip on the ship snapped to white. Somewhere aft a vapor line sheared. The pressure alarm in Ring 7 went amber, then red, and a bulkhead hatch slammed shut across a corridor that had held three hundred people ten minutes before.

"Pressure in Ring 7," the watch officer shouted. "Hatch four sealed with people on the wrong side—"

"Vera," Kaelen said.

"Working." She had her palms flat on the deck and her voice had gone flat with concentration. The wall showed five thousand nodes as a field of green. Six hundred went red in the first second, eight hundred in the second. The layer was being fed an input it had never imagined.

It did not fall. It mutated. Each node read its own drift, rewrote its own gates to fit the new physics, and passed the rewrite along the mesh, and the red began to turn green. By the fourth second the reactor's metering logic had relearned the lithium-6 capture cross-section, which had jumped by a tenth of a percent in the snap and was not coming back, and re-metered the blanket, and the plasma was being argued back into its bottle.

"The air," Kaelen said. "Ring 7."

"Scrubbers relearned their kinetics in four seconds. The Ring 7 vent is a mechanical line, not a node. Your people have to close it."

Kaelen was already at the habitability console, and he did not send a patrol. He vented the hatch-four corridor's pressure into the agricultural plenum and dropped the far side by two percent so the hatch could be cracked without a blowout. Then he left that corridor's lights at full white, the one place on the ship he did not soften, so the three hundred behind the hatch could see the way out. "Hatch four open. Walk them through. Slow."

The officer came back eleven seconds later, seconds that took longer than the whole rewrite. "Through. Forty-one minor injuries, no crush. An old man with a bruised shoulder and a girl who won't let go of the rail."

"Get the rail out of her hands and get her warm," Kaelen said, and kept moving markers.

"Enzyme models rebounding," Vera said. "Hydrolase rates up a percent. Repair pathways relearning their proofreading. Three hundred nodes still renegotiating." She made herself read the exact numbers. "The layer took the jump. It'll take the next one."

Aris's harness threw nine hundred alerts in the first tenth of a second and crashed. It restarted at the fourth second and ran clean: five thousand green points, each one reporting that what it did was what it claimed. He watched the thing he had built keep the ship honest while the ship's physics changed underneath it.

Then, without deciding to, he opened the drift intervals from the minutes around zero and ran them through the checker. A spread. He ran the intervals from the new plateau. Another spread. Every fit came back the same: a distribution, not a sequence. A noise floor where a signal had been.

He sat very still. The primes, so beautiful he had laughed in a cold room. The curve falling like a stone. The stone had been the slope all along, and there had never been anyone on the other side of the cliff.

"Aris." Vera's voice, careful. "Did the harness hold?"

"It crashed, it restarted, it held. Your patch is honest." He took a breath. "The countdown was the drift, steepening. I dressed it up because I needed it to be a letter."

Kaelen did not turn from his console. "You built the harness. If you'd spent those hours in despair instead of building, the layer could be lying to itself right now and I wouldn't know it. The fever put you at your console." He pulled up the map. "We have a new baseline, and the physics will drift again on a schedule nobody knows. I need a standing watch on the constants."

"I'll take it," Aris said. What was on his display now was a slope, rising and falling around a new mean. "I'll log it as a slope. Not a countdown."

Vera sat back on the deck plating and let her hands go still. Under them the Keres breathed, a quarter tone sharp of where it had lived for thirty years and making no apology for it. Somewhere beneath the floor, unseen and unthanked, the layer was writing its gates in the space of a heartbeat, and would go on doing it every time the universe changed its mind.

"New constants," she said, and pushed a delta patch out to the sensor banks, and watched it settle. "New firmware."
