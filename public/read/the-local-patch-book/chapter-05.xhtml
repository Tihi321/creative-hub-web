The Local Patch

The lights came back first.

Not the harsh white of the fall, but the lilac of Kaelen's evening band, steady now, a soft band across the Command Node floor that made the consoles look like furniture in a warm room. The hum returned beneath the deck — a quarter tone sharp of where it had lived for thirty years, and making no apology for it.

The reactor breathed.

Vera sat with her hands folded on the table, watching the breeding ratio hold at one point one one where it had no business holding. The lithium-6 capture cross-section sat at a value no engineer on the Keres had ever written down, and the layer metered the enrichment to match it, and the ratio did not care. She watched the number settle and did not name the thing in her chest.

Kaelen pulled the ring-by-ring report onto the wall. Ring 3, cortisol at a low plateau, loiter time down to baseline. Ring 7, falling but not yet fallen. The resonance at the Core scattered into ordinary human noise — argument, gossip, the sound of three thousand people remembering they had hungry stomachs. No breaches. The chem-bank at nineteen percent and no longer draining.

"We have a new baseline," he said. "The physics has stopped moving. I don't know what schedule it will move on next. I intend to find out."

Aris did not answer. He was at his console with his hands on the keyboard, and the slope was on it now — the drift, risen and fallen around a new mean. He had logged it for six hours. He had not logged a single prime.

"No one spoke," he said. "I want that in the standing watch log, Kaelen, in plain words. So that when the next scholar finds primes in the noise, they find the correction underneath."

"Write it."

Aris wrote it. He wrote the date, and he signed it, and he felt something in his chest loosen that had been clenched for thirty years.

The first new drift came four days later, at 0317, and Aris was asleep.

He woke to the chime, then the second alarm: the harness, complaining that something had changed and it could not tell him what. He was at the console in ninety seconds, barefoot on the cold deck. The cross-sections had wobbled and come back — a part in a hundred thousand, gone in under two seconds — and the layer had caught it and rewritten its gates and settled, and the harness had flagged the rewrite because the rewrite had not matched its prediction. That was all. Nothing had failed.

He sat down and looked at it for a while. In the old days the wobble would have been a knock at the door; he would have run the long brute-force checker and found the fourteen-day rhythm of the ampoule rack and told himself it was a hand reaching through the physics. He did not run it. He logged the wobble as a wobble, with the layer's rewrite attached, and set the watch for the next shift, and he was almost back to his bunk before he understood that he had not been tempted at all.

The door opened. He had not heard it.

The woman in the doorway wore the grey of the Faithful and carried a folded ration slip, and she stood there as though she had come a long way and was not certain of the last step.

"Doctor Thorne," she said. "I'm Sera. I kept the primes. I kept them in my bunk and I prayed to them and I told my daughter they were the Programmer's countdown." She unfolded the slip. On it, in careful hand, was a sequence of intervals. "They've started again. Last night, at the end of the watch. The drift came back and it spaced itself the way it did before. I came before the Faithful hear it, because I don't — I don't know what I would do if they heard it from someone else."

Aris took the slip. On it, in careful hand, were the intervals from the wobble he had just logged, and beneath them the girl had worked out the spacing and it did look like the old countdown, because the old countdown had been the shape of a slope, and any slope she fed it would come back as a countdown. He thought of the harness flag, and of the layer rewriting its gates without asking his permission, and of how close he had come to making the same mistake in the other direction and believing it again in the dark.

"I know how this feels," he said. "I built the whole thing. Here is the truth, Sera, and I want you to hear it plain before the Faithful come looking for a version they like better. The primes were the maintenance cycle. The countdown was the drift as it steepened. There is no one on the other side of the cliff. Last night the drift came back and spaced itself the same way because it is the same drift, and the shape is not a letter. It never was."

She looked at the slip in his hand. "And the ones who prayed?"

"You gave them something to hold in a bad hour," he said. "You held it too. That is not a crime and it is not a lie, so long as you put it down when you know better. And you put it down before it hurt anyone. That's the watch. That is exactly the watch." He stepped back from the console. "The slope is on the main display. It reads out plainly. Would you like to see how the night shift is done?"

Sera folded her ration slip and did not answer at once. Then she sat down in the second chair.

It became the habit of the ship, that winter of the new baseline, that the standing watch was read aloud at the end of each shift — the slope, the wobble, the plateaus — plainly, with no countdowns in it, and that anyone who wished could come and hear it.

Kaelen had started it, because a population that cannot see the numbers invents them.

"Read it as arithmetic," he told the officer. "Not a sermon and not a prophecy. And when the drift comes back hot, they will already know the language for it."

Vera did not attend the readings. She was in the lab, teaching the layer to fit a plateau it had not chosen — a harder lesson than the fall, because the fall had been a cliff and a cliff is easy; a plateau is a slow lie that the gates must be persuaded, every hour, not to accept. She patched and re-patched. She named it the local patch, because that was what the ship had always called a firmware fix — a delta, small, signed, pushed out to the node that needed it. After three centuries of them, the Keres was an accumulated stack. She wrote that into the layer's header, because a truth a layer could not rewrite was the kind of truth that got a ship killed.

On the ninth day the gene-banks threw a fault she had not predicted: a repair cascade that ran ahead of its input and tried to fit a proofreading rate to a plateau that was not yet there. The layer caught it — the filter she had built at hour nine of the countdown, three lines longer than it should have been — and the gates refused the bet and held at what was, and the cascade ran down and stopped. She watched the green points settle and sat back and let out a breath.

"You don't get to know the future," she said, to the empty lab. "You get to know the next second. That's enough."

Kaelen stood on the Bridge at the end of a watch and watched the map glow steady. The rings were calm. The chemistry was resting. The drift was on its way back and he would modulate it when it came.

He had moved the old patrol protocols to the archive, with the fixed-constant firmware, with a note that he had ordered it and why. He had written no protocol for the Faithful, and he had punished no one, and the two things were the same thing: a fever that is not punished breaks on its own, and a fever that is punished learns to hide. Sera sent him her night readings, and he read them, and she was good, and that was the whole of the ledger.

He felt no satisfaction. Satisfaction implied the problem was over. The problem was never over. But the ship was holding, and the holding was enough.

Far forward, in the cold of the Observational Core, Aris Thorne logged the drift and put down his pen.

The hum beneath the deck was a quarter tone sharp of the one he had grown up with, and it was steady, and it was not counting down to anything. It was just the Keres, alive, breathing, carrying its fifty thousand people through a universe that changed its mind.

Then Sera came on shift, and he handed her the watch, and she read the slope aloud to the small room and the two people in it, plainly, and her voice did not rise or fall to it.
