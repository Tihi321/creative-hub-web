Polymorphic Firmware

The reactor office gave Vera forty-seven minutes before the next maintenance window, and she used all of them. She sat cross-legged on the floor of the Bio-Integration Lab with three displays angled around her and her palms flat on the deck plating. Up through the plates came the cooling loops, a vibration somewhere between a pressure and a pulse. It had been steady for thirty years. This morning it had a limp.

The limp was the tritium breeding ratio. The lithium blanket around the core caught the fast neutrons the plasma threw off and bred fresh tritium from them, and the whole plant lived by one rule: breed more fuel than you burn. The ratio had read one point one four for as long as anyone had kept records. Yesterday it read one point one one. This morning it read one point zero nine, and the decline was steepening.

She pulled the cross-section data. The chance that a passing neutron would be caught by a lithium-6 nucleus, the number the blanket had been designed around, was wandering: parts per million, then tens of parts per million. The control logic had been written by people who knew that number would never move. It kept metering the enrichment to a fixed value and kept breeding less, correcting and correcting, a dog chasing its tail around the core.

She loaded her layer into the sandbox.

First she fed it the measured drift and asked it to hold the ratio. The layer read the drift, rewrote its own metering gates, and held at one point one one. Then she gave it a collapse, five percent in a hundred seconds, and the gates rewrote themselves thirty times while the ratio dipped to one point zero seven and climbed back. Then, because she wanted to know whether it could survive something the universe had not yet done, she gave it a step: the cross-section jumping all at once to a value she invented. Two hundred rewrites in three seconds. The ratio held. The simulated reactor did not notice.

Vera let out a breath she had been holding since the first test. She signed the layer as a delta patch, verified it against the firmware manifest the way every patch on the Keres had been verified for three centuries, and pushed it to the production nodes on the blanket. Through her palms she felt the plant take the change: a hitch, then a smoother pulse. On the display the ratio stopped falling at one point one one and stayed there.

She commed the Command Node. "The blanket will hold. Tell Kaelen the fire in the walls has a valve now. It opens and closes on its own."

She did not say what she was already thinking: that she had tested the layer against every fall she could invent, and against none she could not.

Aris Thorne had not slept in thirty hours, and the lack had taken on a texture, a granular clarity in which every number on his display seemed to hum against its neighbors. He checked the raw scintillation counts three times against the original logs, because somewhere around hour twenty-six it had occurred to him that if the drift was a message, someone might be editing his logs to sharpen it. He found no editing. That did not comfort him.

The intervals were tightening. In the first week the gaps between drift episodes had run the primes, 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, days apart. Then the sequence had begun again with shorter gaps, and then again, shorter still. He plotted gap length against cycle, and the points fell like a stone dropped from rest.

He tried to make it something else. A beat between two clocks. Thermal aliasing in the photomultiplier housings. The dark count locking its phase to the drift. He wrote each idea down and broke it, one after another, until only the curve was left, fitting nothing but itself, and at the bottom of the curve, where the gaps reached zero, a number.

Fifty-nine hours.

He sat in the cold room with his hands around a cup he had forgotten to fill and waited to feel afraid. What came instead was older. All his life he had argued that a universe this finely set had a setter, and all his life the universe had declined to reply. Now it had a schedule. He was not a man who had been ignored. He was a man who had been answered, and the answer came with a deadline.

At 0410 he posted the projection to the Command Node, flagged for the Chief, with its confidence intervals and a request for a full meeting before the window closed. He wrote it as a physics note. He read it back twice and could not find the place where it stopped being one.

Kaelen Vance had held the crowd at the AI Core since the third watch, eighteen hours now, and the chem-bank had fallen to thirty-four percent.

The oxytocin analog went faster than the synthesis lines could replace it. The precursor came from the gene-banks, the gene-banks were running their own drifting kinetics, and the yield had fallen with the same slope Vera was chasing in the blanket. Everything on the ship had that slope now. He was beginning to know it the way he knew a face.

Aris's note arrived at 0411. Kaelen read it standing, then took a ration slip from his pocket and did the sums on the back. Reservoir at thirty-four percent. Consumption steady. Synthesis falling. Fifty-nine hours to Aris's zero, if Aris was right. At full dose he could hold the Core for eleven of those hours. At a quarter dose, nineteen. After that, whatever came at the end of the count would come to a ship with empty tanks.

"Start a drawdown schedule," he told the watch officer. "Rings 3 and 7 at full dose until hour eleven. Then I want a written plan for which concourses go cold first, in order, signed, with my name on it."

The officer hesitated. "Chief, that means choosing—"

"It means choosing which corridors get a calming agent and which get nothing. Yes. Write it." He looked at the Core feed. Three thousand people in the blue light, still humming. They had not moved since the third watch. The cortisol telemetry called them calm. The physiological feed said their heart rates had begun to synchronize, three thousand hearts finding one cadence, the way a marching column finds the frequency of a bridge.

"A patrol," the officer said, for the fourth time.

"A patrol is a perturbation. It might scatter them. It might couple them. I won't roll that die on three thousand people." He moved markers on the map. "Bring the Core concourse pressure up half a percent. I want them heavy and sleepy, thinking about their bodies instead of their hearts. And start drawing precursor from the agricultural reserve."

"That breaks the food-reserve protocol."

"Then log that it broke, and that I ordered it." He folded the slip once and put it in his pocket. The protocol had been written for a universe that held still. "If the count is real, the worst hour is the last one. I mean to have something in the tank for it."

He watched the crowd's pulse on the feed and saw, under the calm, the same slope again: not a valley they were resting in, but ground still tilting toward an edge he could not see.
