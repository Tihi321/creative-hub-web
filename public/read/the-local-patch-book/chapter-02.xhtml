Patch Faithful

The leak was not Aris's doing, not exactly. He had filed the prime-sequence analysis in the physics office log the way the regulations required, appended his confidence intervals, and gone to his bunk for four hours of the shallow, useful sleep that passed for rest aboard the Keres. By the time he woke, the log entry had been forwarded eleven thousand times.

The Keres ran on a mesh so old and so thoroughly mirrored that no administrator had been able to prune it in a century. Every file the physics office filed was copied to every terminal that had once subscribed to the physics office feed. Nobody had unsubscribed in forty years. The prime sequence — 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, interspersed with raw decay counts, the drift intervals shrinking like a fuse burning toward its charge — was on every display in every concourse by second watch.

Aris learned this from the corridor chatter before he learned it from the commands piling into his queue. He stood in the galley line for his ration of reconstituted protein and heard a woman two places ahead say, to no one in particular, "They're counting down."

The Patch Faithful had existed for years — a loose confederation of the ship's more devout, who had long held that the Keres was a testing ground and the constants a set of rules handed down by a Creator they called the Programmer. They had been a nuisance to the Bridge and a comfort to their own. What they had never had was evidence. Now, apparently, they had a prime-numbered signature with confidence intervals. They had a countdown.

By the third watch, three thousand of them had gathered at the doors of the AI Core.

The AI Core occupied the forward third of the habitat's central spindle, a cylinder of cold server racks and cooling loops that ran the ship's governance: traffic, rations, air, the gene-bank's registrar of births, the arbitration of disputes. It was not a mind. It was a stack of deterministic schedulers, and its governance was a rulebook three centuries deep. But it was the closest thing the Keres had to a throne, and the Patch Faithful had decided the Programmer was speaking through it, and that the ship should be surrendered to the Programmer's hand.

Kaelen Vance watched them gather on the Bridge display and felt the familiar read of a system under load. The acoustic monitors measured the crowd's murmur as a broad low band — prayer, he noted, not shouting. The cortisol telemetry from the corridors around the Core showed the rise but not the spike. This crowd was not angry. It was hopeful, which was more dangerous, because hope does not deplete. Hope compounds.

"Visible patrol at the Core doors," the watch officer said. "Standard containment protocol."

Kaelen studied the light in the crowd's faces. "No patrol," he said.

The officer hesitated. "Chief, there are three thousand people. If they breach the Core—"

"They won't breach." He pulled the environmental map and began moving markers. "They're not here to break anything. They're here because someone handed them a countdown and they need to do something with their hands. You don't answer that with sticks. You answer it with chemistry and light."

He flooded the Core concourse with the oxytocin analog — the in-group one, the one that made a crowd feel like a congregation, warm toward its own and calm among the familiar. He brought the lighting down and shifted the spectrum to a hard 480-nanometer blue, the band that had, in the ship's own decades of elderly-care research, slowed the agitated and deepened their sleep. He thinned the crowd's edge by opening the agricultural vents so the smell of the hydroponics bays washed up the concourse, grassy and green and ordinary.

The crowd did not disperse. But it softened. The murmur lowered to a hum — a sustained, sonorous hum of hopeful prayer, three thousand people swaying gently in the blue light, none of them shouting, none of them at the doors when the third watch ended.

Kaelen watched the cortisol curve bend and held himself to the restraint of a man who knew he had bought hours, not days. The chem-bank reservoir had dropped by eleven percent in one watch, and he had not yet had to use the lavender. He logged the figure, because a number he had written down was a number he could not pretend he had not chosen.

"Chief," the officer said. "The reactor office reports the neutron multiplication factor is falling."

"I know," Kaelen said. "I read the same telemetry you do. Tell Vera Lin to come to the Command Node. Tell her it is not a drill and it is not a paper. Tell her the fire is in the walls this time."

Vera Lin had been up through three watches, and her hands were trembling from the stimulant she took to keep them steady, which was a contradiction she had long ago stopped interrogating. She had the reactor telemetry on four displays and her own kinetic models on a fifth, and the two were telling her the same story from different ends.

The reactor's fission blanket relied on a beryllium multiplier to slow and multiply neutrons, and on a breeding ratio to replenish its tritium. For thirty years those numbers had sat where the engineers had left them. Now the neutron multiplication factor was drifting down — not failing, drifting, a slow erosion, as if the beryllium had lost a little of its appetite for the reaction. She ran it against her enzyme data. The same slope. The same signature. The forces that held the Keres together at the particle scale were changing, and the reactor, which was only a very large and very hot piece of applied particle physics, was changing with them.

She did not tell Aris. She had read his prime sequence in the same log he had filed, and she had run it through her own checker, and it had come back — quietly, to her, alone — as a fit. She had watched the drift intervals tighten into a curve that looked like an inverse of a countdown. She had felt, for one disorienting instant, the tug of his certainty. Then she had looked at her enzyme plates and her reactor numbers and felt the tug let go, because a countdown to what? She did not care. The universe was changing its mind. That was all. And the only question that mattered was how to bend with it.

The Keres already knew how to bend. Every subsystem on the ship ran on firmware that could be patched from the Command Node — tritium ratios adjusted, enzyme models reparameterized, the air retuned, each change pushed out across thousands of nodes in a single synchronized update. It was the ship's oldest architecture: the local patch. Vera had spent her career watching it work at the scale of the gene-banks, where a mismatch in a single base pair was caught and rewritten before the cell divided.

What she had never done was make the patch write itself.

She opened the firmware tree and began to code. Not a control system that held the reactor to a fixed set of constants, but a control system that read the constants as inputs — that measured the neutron cross-sections, the enzyme kinetics, the electrostatic forces in protein folds, and rewrote its own logic gates to match. A substrate that mutated with its environment. She worked in the polymorphic layer, where the logic gates were themselves data, and she wrote it so that when the constants moved, the gates would move with them, rewriting themselves in the milliseconds a reactor might otherwise take to fail.

She was three hundred lines deep when Kaelen's summons came, and she did not look up from the screen. The fire was in the walls. Good. Walls she could patch.

"Tell the Command Node I'm busy," she said to the empty lab. "Tell them I'm rewriting the physics the Keres is standing on, and I'll be there when it holds."

The reactor's multiplication factor dropped another ten-thousandth of a percent, and Vera Lin wrote the next line.

She did not finish before her hands gave out. The tremor had been there all watch, and the stimulant had been holding it, and at the bottom of the fourth hour the stimulant stopped holding it and the line she was writing came out wrong — a gate that flipped the wrong way under a cross-section she had already fed it. She caught it on the test run. She sat looking at the red flag for a long moment, and what she felt was not competence but its opposite: the rabbit-hole vertigo of a woman who has been awake too long to trust her own fingers, building the one thing on the ship that could not afford a wrong gate.

She backed the line out and wrote it again, slower, with the stimulant flushed and replaced by a cup of cold tea she did not drink. The gate held. She did not know, when she pushed the next build, whether she had caught the only mistake or merely the first. That was the cost, and she noted it, and it was going to be paid at the reactor, in the dark, at whatever hour the constants chose.
