The Coordinates

The weather station, afternoon

Ambience: a pump hammering over the rock, wind at the eaves

Through the window, Sella's crew work the vault mouth. A fuel-driven pump chatters flat and hard; by four the hose runs thin.

On screen: Mara (at the glass, arms folded, eyes hard), Idris (behind her, hands black to the wrist)

MARA Four-inch trash pump. By dusk it'll be spitting. They'll have the floor bare by dark.

IDRIS And the set on its plinth with the water gone. It can't be hidden. You knew that.

MARA I knew they'd find the gear. I didn't know what she'd do about it. What she does next tells me who's paying her.

Below, Reeve comes up the stair with his trousers black to the shin and stands over a slate with Sella.

IDRIS Twice he's pointed back down there.

MARA And she's pointing at nothing. That's a woman deciding what to call a thing she can't yet explain.

Sella turns her head and looks up at the radio room. Mara holds still. The look runs a second too long, then slides past her.

MARA Tomorrow she'll have a reason ready. Obsolete equipment removed as a hazard. By the time the lane's open it's a fact.

The weather station, night

Ambience: wind in the roof, a speaker's breath of static

The pump has stopped. On the apron the gear is sheeted; the vault door stands open, a black mouth with cold breath coming out of it. A cabin light shows on the Petrel.

On screen: Mara (log on her knee at the listing chair, pencil ready), Idris (tide table unfolded between two lamps)

MARA She's leaving the set in the water. That's a decision, not an oversight.

IDRIS Then we read the rest before she finds her reason.

MARA Where's the tide table?

IDRIS Behind the office distribution board. Nobody's put a hand back there in a decade.

MARA Get it. The sea's at its mark in an hour.

He goes, and comes back with the soft folded sheet. They spread it flat between the lamps.

MARA We compare at the end, not before. A message is only as good as a second reader.

IDRIS High water at 21:52. The set only fires above three point one metres on the gauge. So it only talks the top half of the tide.

MARA Two hours, maybe less. Then we've had the whole message we're going to get tonight.

Sound: five-second pulse, three rising tones, one flat

IDRIS (ear cocked) There she is. Four minutes after the mark.

MARA Call me the height. I'll write seconds and we'll let the tide table do the rest.

IDRIS Three point two one.

Mara writes. The speaker breathes. The wind turns on the roof.

IDRIS Three point three zero. Next group's coming.

MARA Keep them coming.

The weather station, 23:40

Ambience: wind dropping, the speaker's small static

Several pages of log lie under the lamp, columns of figures in two hands. The pencils are worn.

On screen: Mara (leaning over her column, brow tight), Idris (finger on the tide table, sitting forward)

Sound: five-second pulse, three rising tones, one flat

IDRIS Different group. The gaps are longer. That's a separator.

MARA The number's finished. The position is the first half, and the second half starts now.

IDRIS It's been starting there every spring tide since my father died. Nobody ever heard it.

MARA Write it down. Don't stop to think about it.

They work. The water falls away from the mark. Near one in the morning the set goes quiet and stays quiet.

IDRIS That's the top of the tide gone. We've had all of it there is.

MARA Then we do the arithmetic.

She applies the scheme: the height at the pulse for the digit, the gap before the group for its place. She reads her column twice, then a third time.

MARA I've got a chain. Position first. Then the digits turn over to letters. Each group isn't a number but a letter of the alphabet, spelled out one at a time. A name, spelled out in letters, five letters, then a space.

Close-up: Mara's log page, a neat column of figures, and beneath it in her square hand: DAN KELL

MARA Dan Kell.

The pencil stops. The lamp flickers. The office shrinks to a small room with the whole sea outside it.

IDRIS (gently) Your brother.

MARA He was on a ship that went down in a sudden storm. Twelve years ago. I was nineteen. They found three of the crew and never found him.

MARA The board said the weather came on them. My mother wanted a stone put up. I told her we should wait. I told her careful men don't drown, and if they hadn't found him there was a reason, and the reason would come.

She puts her palm flat on the page, not hiding the name but feeling it under her hand.

MARA Twelve years I've read every inquiry I could get the paper of, looking for a ship's name I'd know. I told myself it was work. It was never work.

IDRIS You never said.

MARA There was no one to say it to.

She takes her hand off the page deliberately, the way you take it off a hot surface.

MARA And now a dead man's transmitter spells his name at one in the morning, and I've cried about it exactly as much as I'm going to tonight. Read me the next group.

Idris waits, and she lets the name sit, and he does not fill the quiet.

IDRIS (looking up) The next group's not a letter. It's a number. A name off a hull.

Close-up: a second column of Idris's figures, beneath them a ship's name and a registration number

IDRIS I know this name. My father wrote it down.

He pushes the tide table across the desk and lays his finger on a pencilled line in careful blue ink: the same name in the margin, twelve years old.

IDRIS Same ship. He put it down in his first message. He found out who sank your brother's ship, and he wrote the name down, and then he went up to the light.

MARA Say it plainly.

IDRIS (to the desk) My father didn't fall. He went to the office and gave them the name and the position and asked them to act. They gave him a contract and sent him home. A few nights later he was dead at the foot of his own stair.

IDRIS And the man from the office came round asking for papers. Twelve years I've told myself it was the wind and the wet step. It wasn't the step.

He looks up.

IDRIS He was killed because he found what you've just written down. And I sat on it because I was frightened and nineteen and didn't know if a man who talked about locks all night was mad. I'm sorry.

The turbine shed door bangs. The beacon cable hums in the trunking. The office is as cold as the vault.

MARA (very quiet, very even) He wasn't mad. He was an engineer. He built a thing that worked and died for it, and twelve years later it told the truth to a pair of us at one in the morning.

MARA That's not mad. That's the only sane thing anyone on this island has done for a decade. Sit up straight. We've got work.

The weather station, night

Ambience: wind in the glass, a pencil moving on paper

The chart of the Farrows is spread on the desk. Mara's pencil is already drawing.

On screen: Mara (bent over the chart, precise), Idris (standing at her shoulder, pale)

MARA The position puts the wreck two points off the Farrow Sound, in about ninety metres of water. That's the channel the insurers closed.

MARA And the channel's where the Petrel is anchored, and the Petrel is a wreck-diving unit.

IDRIS She's not here for the vault. She's here for the wreck.

MARA She's here to lift the recorder off it and put it where it will never be found. The vault was only the thing she had to deal with first, to stop the beacon talking.

MARA If we clear the vault, she takes her time on the wreck. If we don't, she has to dive on a clock, and a thing done on a clock gets done badly.

IDRIS So we go down first. And we don't bring the box to her.

MARA A ship's recorder is a steel capsule built to survive the bottom. It's the only thing down there that says sabotage instead of storm. Have you ever dived to ninety?

IDRIS No.

MARA Neither have I. So we do it the way a job gets done when nobody's qualified: slowly, on a line, on the slack, and not one metre past what we've planned.

She draws the pencil across the paper, then stops and looks at what she has drawn.

MARA I'll be plain, because I'm the one breathing the air. Ninety metres on open-circuit air isn't a working dive. It's a climb, a wait in the cold, and a set of stops on a line, and the bottom time is one pass and no more.

MARA If it goes wrong down there it goes wrong faster than I can fix it, there's no chamber on this island, and no help nearer than St. Bride's. I know all of that. I'm doing it anyway, and not because I think it's safe.

IDRIS I'd go instead, if I could. I've no more diving than you and less nerve. But I'd go.

MARA I know. You're the one who kept the lock. I'll do the deep.

Sound: pencil tapping the chart

MARA How's the station's boat?

IDRIS The station hasn't had a boat since before the flood. My father's dory is up on the slip with a plank out and hasn't been in the water in three years.

MARA The skiff on the west slip.

IDRIS The old survey launch. She's chained to the ring, and the yard's put a padlock on her, and the key went to St. John's with the rest.

MARA What kind of lock?

IDRIS Brass or bronze. Shrouded shackle.

MARA Show me the chain and the ring.

The west slip

Ambience: swell against stone, wind pulling at oilskin

A flight of wet steps down to black water. The skiff lies against the slip, a good brass padlock through chain and ring. Mara crouches with her lamp and turns the lock in the light.

On screen: Mara (kneeling on wet stone, lamp held low), Idris (above her, hands in pockets, doubtful)

MARA Bronze body, brass barrel, six-pin. That's a good lock.

IDRIS The key's in St. John's.

MARA I know. And it's on a chain, and the chain goes round the ring, and the ring's a bolt-through set in concrete, with a hex head on top and the nut under the lip of the step.

MARA I can't reach the nut and I can't turn the bolt against it. But the concrete round the stem has cracked. Old pour, sea water, frost, twelve years of a chain working at it.

MARA The concrete's the weakest thing here, and it's the part nobody thought about. We don't touch the lock. We work what it's holding.

She takes the wrench from her coat and sets it on the hex head.

IDRIS That's a good lock.

MARA (turning, breath clenched) I heard you the first time.

IDRIS I mean I'm glad you're not fighting it. My father would have fought it.

The bolt turns by degrees. Water flings at her back. Her knuckle opens on the plate.

Sound: a grind of steel leaving old concrete

The bolt comes up. The padlock hangs free, still locked, still a good lock.

MARA Machines. You can trust a machine to be exactly as strong as it was made to be, and no stronger.

She has chain and ring in one hand and the skiff's lines in the other.

The wreck site

Ambience: low engine, water slapping a small hull

The skiff noses out of the west slip with its running lights dark. The Petrel's cabin light burns a hundred metres off her quarter. The sounder shows a bottom at ninety metres.

On screen: Mara (at the tiller, throttle down, listening to the wind), Idris (in the bow, lamp shaded, looking back)

IDRIS The battery's nearly dead from a winter on the slip. She caught on three cylinders and won't take the fourth.

MARA Three will do. Keep her slow and the noise behind us.

IDRIS A hundred metres off her quarter we'll be nobody. The Sound's full of men who don't want to be seen at night.

The sounder gives ninety metres, a broad lump with a scarp beside it, where the chart says clear water.

IDRIS There she is. And nobody's marked her, because she's in the lane, and a ship in the lane means the lane stays shut.

He stands forward with the lamp shaded.

IDRIS Mara. There's a thing about the Petrel I've been not saying for an hour because I didn't want to say it out loud.

MARA Then say it.

IDRIS She's anchored on the wreck. Near enough. A hundred metres off her quarter and we'd be on top of it.

IDRIS She's not here to wait on our clock. She'll put a diver in the water at first light.

MARA So whatever we bring up, we bring up before the sun.

She looks at the sounder's number and the Petrel's light, and understands that she has broken her contract and Sella's orders both.

MARA Get the lamp aft and keep it off the Petrel. We've a line to run and a bottle to rig, and I'd like both done before the sky knows what we're about.

Picture: Mara Kell and Idris Vane in the station's survey skiff over the wreck site at night, Mara at the tiller with the throttle down and Idris in the bow shading the lamp, the Petrel's cabin light small in the dark behind them

IDRIS Two hundred metres of line on the reel. One lamp. One bottle apiece, and mine's older than I am.

MARA Then yours stays in the boat. You'll tend my line and you'll not go over the side.

IDRIS And you will.

MARA One pass at the bottom. If I start up the line before I've found the box, you haul me in and don't argue.

Sound: sounder pinging

MARA Ninety. Slack's not for another hour. Rig your side and coil the line where I can reach it without looking.

IDRIS And if she puts her diver in early?

MARA Then she'll find wet footprints on a wreck she's told everyone was never there. That'll be a story she'd have to explain.

The skiff turns her nose into the swell and holds.
