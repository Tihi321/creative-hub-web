The Flooded Vault

Little Farrow, the slip — 06:00

Ambience: swell sucking at stone, wind in the scoria

The water lies grey with no sun in it. The sea draws back off the shelf and shows the drowned teeth of the old cable run.

On screen: Mara (rowing in, sleeves up, watching the shore), Idris (waiting with two lamps and a coil of line, face grey with no sleep)

Mara ships the oars. The dinghy grinds home on the stone.

MARA You're early.

IDRIS You're earlier.

He makes the painter fast before she asks. His hands don't shake. That costs him something.

MARA You've come to a conclusion.

IDRIS I've come to several. I don't like any of them. Bring your lamp.

The vault

Ambience: water dripping, a low hum from the cable heads

A concrete mouth in the rock under the station, half smothered in storm weed. The dogs on the steel door are worn bright from use.

Idris works the wheel with both hands. The door groans. The sound runs down into the rock and comes back wrong.

On screen: Mara (lamp up, jaw tight), Idris (lamp hanging, looking anywhere but down)

The beam finds a stair going into black water. The smell is cold and mineral: wet concrete, rust, and under it the sweetness of perished insulation.

MARA How deep?

IDRIS Twelve metres by eight. A metre over the floor at low water, a ledge above it, cable heads on the far wall. And something on the ledge.

MARA You've been down.

IDRIS Once. To see what my father was doing. I didn't stay.

They go down. The cold takes her boots, then her knees, and closes like a hand.

Her lamp finds rusted cable heads in ranks, the dry ledge, and on it a grey steel cabinet with its guts showing.

IDRIS Don't touch the busbars. There's charge in the batteries. The trunking back to the beacon is live.

MARA I know it's live.

She wades to the ledge and sets the lamp down beside the cabinet.

Close-up: the transmitter on its plinth, hand-loomed wiring tied neat, a strip of tape on the face lettered in careful blue ink: For the tide.

MARA This isn't a fault. It's a beacon of its own. A marine band set, bolted to a plinth, run off the station's batteries.

She follows the wiring with her finger, down through the sump, to a float on a rod.

MARA A float switch. The tide lifts it, it makes, the set fires. That's why the tones aren't even. The water isn't switching it. The water is the message.

IDRIS He said the sea keeps better time than any man and doesn't take a bribe.

MARA The five seconds. Three rising tones and a flat note. That's not a call sign.

IDRIS No. It's a key. He showed me once, the year before he died. Four tones to a group, the group's a number.

IDRIS The height of the water when it fires is the digit. The gap before the group is where the group sits in the message. The tide is the number. The gap is the place.

MARA The gaps are the clock. The heights are the words.

IDRIS He said it was a lock. Then he died, and I couldn't read it.

MARA Read what? You said you never got past the position.

Idris reaches inside his coat and brings out a folded square of paper, soft with years of handling.

IDRIS I didn't burn mine.

The vault, the dry ledge — 06:40

Ambience: water dripping, a low hum from the cable heads

Two lamps and a tide table between them, the column of pencil figures in the margin.

Close-up: tide table for Little Farrow, a column of heights in one hand and a second column in a smaller, older hand beside it.

MARA High water spring, high water neap, midway. The set only fires above a mark. So every pulse is at a known height, and the height is the digit.

IDRIS The first group of the night is always the start of the message. Then it runs all night, one group at a time.

MARA Then a full spring-tide night carries the whole thing. The rest of the month I was hearing the top of a sentence.

She reads the margin, checks the height, checks the place in the run, and does it again.

MARA Sixty-one. Sixty-two. Forty-three. Degrees and minutes. North.

She works down the page with her pencil.

MARA Then west. That's a position. Two points off the Farrow Sound, about ninety metres of water.

IDRIS There's nothing there. That's clear channel.

MARA A clear channel with a wreck in it.

IDRIS (to the wall) Because they sank it.

The lamp ticks. Mara sets her pencil down and lets the words stand.

IDRIS Somebody put a ship down in the lane, the lane closed, the insurers paid, everybody was very sorry. My father found out. He went to the office in St. John's with what he knew. They gave him a contract to keep the station, and he came home and built a lock.

MARA Why not write a letter?

IDRIS You don't telegraph a thing like that. You hide it where the water will keep it. And you tell one person where it is.

MARA Who did he tell?

IDRIS Me. Then he went up the hill to the light, and he didn't come down. They found him at the foot of the lighthouse stair. The inquest said he fell.

MARA Twelve years he climbed that stair in any weather.

IDRIS The morning before, he told me the vault was safe and the lock was set. Don't open it for anybody who comes asking. That night a man from the office came to the door, very polite. Asked whether my father had left any papers.

MARA What did you tell him?

IDRIS No. And he looked at me the way a man looks at a door he means to come back to.

Idris folds the tide table along its old creases.

IDRIS Three years I've kept the lock set. Most nights of the week I don't know if that was brave or cowardly.

Mara puts her hand flat on the cold concrete between them, where he can see it, and leaves it there.

MARA The rest of the message. After the coordinates.

IDRIS More numbers, then the flat note, then it starts again. I never got past the position. It'd want a full night's watch in the radio room, every pulse and every gap, and no sleep.

MARA When's the next spring?

IDRIS Tonight's the third night before it. It comes on the fourth.

He waits, and does not ask.

MARA Tonight we take the top of the tide. Four nights from now we take the whole message.

IDRIS Thank you. You'll want a better chair than the office one. It's got a list to port.

MARA So have I.

Mara has it in her hands before he hears it: the engine. A hard, busy note off the northeast, no fishing boat.

IDRIS That's the Rourke tender. The Petrel. She's been up and down the Sound all summer and never come in.

MARA Go up to the station. Tide table where it won't be found, vault door to. You're the man who keeps the radios. Nothing else.

IDRIS And what are you?

MARA The engineer who's here to silence a beacon. Which is exactly what I'm going to be.

The weather station

Ambience: wind over concrete, a generator ticking down

The Petrel stands off the slip, grey and long and new, a rubber boat on her foredeck. Her crew carry two trips of gear up the rock.

On screen: Mara (trousers wet to the thigh, lamp still belted on), Idris (at the door, face for strangers), Sella (buttoned coat, dry, smiling), Reeve (shaved head, laying out dive tools on a cloth with long practice)

Sella comes up the slip with her coat closed against weather that is not touching her.

SELLA Ms. Kell. You've been wading.

MARA There's a flooded vault under the station. The beacon's supply runs through it.

SELLA You told me your diagnosis was a supply fault. I didn't know it required a bath.

She turns to Idris, and her eyes go over him the way a survey runs a level over ground.

SELLA Mr. Vane. The keeper's son. I've heard a great deal about your family.

IDRIS I doubt that.

SELLA People talk. About the beacon, the lane, and the old keeper, who was a great loss. Twelve years, wasn't it. A fall.

Mara studies the gear on the apron: two aluminium cylinders per diver, a reel of line, hoses coiled and chained to a case.

SELLA Insurers' requirement. Any decommissioned installation the lane reopens past must be certified secure. Including flooded spaces. We'll be opening that vault ourselves, and I can't have unqualified personnel in the water.

MARA A site inspection with a cutting torch.

SELLA Structures have to be made safe. Doors dogged, cables cut.

Sella speaks without turning her head.

SELLA Mr. Reeve.

REEVE Ms. Rourke.

SELLA The vault wants pumping. Pump ashore by dark, water down by morning. Then the cables. The beacon circuit cut at both ends and the trunking opened and certified. Anything in there that isn't cable, you bring to me. Not to Ms. Kell. To me.

MARA Certified. By whom?

SELLA By me. I'm the contractor of record. That's what the clause means.

She steps closer. Her voice does not change, and that is the thing about her.

SELLA Your contract is for a beacon. Not a vault. Not a conversation with the keeper's son about old times. The beacon goes silent in forty-eight hours, and you'll do nothing else until it does.

SELLA You will not go down into that vault again. You will not open that fault record again. You will send no report anywhere except to me. When a contract is irregular, Ms. Kell, people get let go.

MARA Forty-eight hours.

SELLA Forty-eight.

She walks back to the apron and says something that makes Reeve laugh. The laugh carries on the wind.

Idris has not moved from the door. Only his eyes are wrong, going from Sella to the cutting torch and back.

Little Farrow, the slip — 08:50

Ambience: swell against stone, wind in the scoria

Mara gets the oars into the dinghy. Out in the Sound the Steadfast swings at her chain, the Petrel grey beyond her.

On screen: Mara (in the dinghy, hands on the oars), Idris (on the slip above her, not looking at her)

IDRIS She's going to open the vault and clear it out. The set, the tape, all of it. By morning it's scrap and my father's twelve years are a bad fall and nothing more.

MARA Not by morning. By morning the water's down. Then they open the trunking and certify. That's two days. She's given us two days without knowing it.

IDRIS Two days.

MARA And then it's scrap. Unless we're in the radio room before she gets her torch down that stair.

She shoves off and rows out with her back to the island.

MARA The clock's the tide's. It always was. Four nights to the spring, and the sea's already turning toward the high water that makes the next group.

The sea pushes at the oars, patient and heavy.

MARA The transmitter will tell me the rest of it before she gets her torch down that stair. I mean to be in the radio room to hear it.
