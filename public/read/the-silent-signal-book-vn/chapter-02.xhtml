The Keeper's Son

Little Farrow, the slip — morning

Ambience: swell against stone, gulls, wind in the scoria

Grey light on black tiered rock. The dinghy grinds up the slip; a scoria path climbs toward the low white roofs of the station. Farther off, the white lighthouse stands dark.

On screen: Mara (oilskin dripping, oars shipped, breathing hard)

Mara drags the dinghy above the tide line and bends to the ring bolt.

The iron is rust-red and worn bright at the eye, shiny from a hundred painters before hers.

MARA (quiet) Bright iron. Nobody's made fast here in a long time but somebody has.

The weather station

Ambience: wind at the eaves, a radio humming

The hut is concrete and timber, streaked green. Windows dark, but one door is propped with a brick and a thin yellow light leaks out.

On screen: Mara (at the door, one hand on the frame)

MARA A dead station with the kettle on.

She knocks twice and steps in out of the wind.

The weather station, radio room — morning

Ambience: valve hum, kettle ticking, wind on glass

Paraffin, hot dust and solder. Shelves of old radios worn smooth at the dials. A man works at the bench under a hanging lamp, a soldering iron in his hand, a cat asleep on a folded coat.

On screen: Idris (back turned, hunched over the bench, iron in hand)

IDRIS You're the engineer. From the tug.

MARA (by the door) Mara Kell. You've got my frequency.

He sets the iron down and turns. His thumb and forefinger are bright with solder.

IDRIS Idris Vane. I'd shake your hand but I've got solder on it.

He grins, then lets the grin go too soon.

MARA You keep the station?

IDRIS I keep the radios. The station's been dead since before the flood. Kettle's on.

MARA I should get to the beacon.

IDRIS The beacon will still be wrong in ten minutes. Sit. Stand. Whatever you like.

Mara does not sit. She takes the tea he pours anyway.

IDRIS You'll be here about the beacon.

MARA I'm here to certify it. It's been throwing a fault, and the insurers won't open the lane while it does.

IDRIS Ah. The lane.

He turns his cup a quarter turn on the bench.

MARA You know the lane.

IDRIS Everybody here knows the lane. Shut three years. Small boats don't come at all now.

He says nothing for a moment, and the nothing runs a beat too long.

IDRIS So they've sent you to fix the beacon.

MARA To silence it. It's a hazard, apparently.

IDRIS Is it.

He says it to his tea. Mara drinks hers and lets the gap do the work.

MARA Will you show me the gear? You're here. You know it.

He stands so fast the cat lifts its head.

IDRIS I'll take you. Bring your lamp.

The weather station, beacon tower — morning

Ambience: wind rising, turbine turning

The tower's steel leg runs into a concrete footing, the turbine turning slowly on its mast above. Mara walks around the tower twice before she touches it.

On screen: Mara (lamp belted on, eyes narrowed), Idris (hands in pockets, watching her too closely)

Mara opens the access panel and looks inside. The wiring is clean and neat.

MARA (half to herself) Clean. Neat. Somebody's been here and left no marks.

IDRIS The station looks after itself. Wind and sun.

MARA Not like this. What does the lamp want?

IDRIS Two hundred watts, when it ran.

MARA This circuit's pulling nearer four.

She traces the trunking down the tower leg and stops at a thin cable, older and neater than the rest.

MARA A second cable. Spliced in proper. Where does it go?

IDRIS (too quickly) The vault. The old cable vault, underneath. It's flooded.

MARA Flooded and fed. Somebody's been pumping power into a dead room.

IDRIS The wind and the sun feed what's on the ends of them. I only keep the radios.

He shifts his boots on the rock.

IDRIS You'll want the fault record. It's in the office. I'll get it.

He is already turning, and Mara lets him go, her eyes still on the splice.

MARA Bad liar. Same as a bad bearing. Not silent. Noisy.

The weather station, office — morning

Ambience: drawer rattling, wind at the glass

Two desks, grey filing cabinets, a yellowed chart of the Farrows over the radiator. Idris is in a cabinet to the elbow, longer than a man looking for one folder should be.

On screen: Idris (sleeve pushed up, arm deep in the drawer), Mara (standing back, arms folded)

IDRIS Here.

He lays a buff folder on the desk and stands away from it as if it might bite.

Close-up: fault record, a single page in careful blue ink

Beacon showing intermittent fault on test at 22:00. Signal audible on band at 4.8 MHz. Reported to contractor by telephone. No reply.

Mara reads it twice.

MARA Reported to which contractor?

IDRIS Whoever holds the maintenance contract. The office in St. John's.

She turns the page. A later entry, same hand, same careful ink: Pulse confirmed. Running to its old schedule. Will hold. A third, in pencil, unsteady: Still holding. Somebody has to.

MARA You wrote these.

IDRIS I keep the station's records.

MARA Your job is the radios.

IDRIS It was my father's job to keep the station. Then it wasn't a station. Then it was only mine.

He takes the folder out of her hand and shuts it.

MARA How long has the pulse been running?

IDRIS Years. Longer than the fault record. Longer than the lane's been shut.

MARA How many years is years?

He shakes his head and turns to the window.

IDRIS I'm not the person to ask. I'm only the radios.

MARA You're the only person here. You knew the band and you knew the schedule. That's not a hobby. That's knowing.

IDRIS (low, careful) My father kept this station twelve years after the lane shut. Keeper before that. When the weather service pulled out he stayed on the beacon, glad of it. He'd nowhere else to be.

Mara says nothing.

IDRIS Then one winter he went up to the light to check the lamp, and he fell on the stair. The inquest said he slipped. He'd gone up and down that stair twice a day in weather that would take the roof off, and he slipped.

MARA Idris.

IDRIS He's on that hill. Twelve years now. There's no weather station any more and no keeper, but I keep his radios warm, because that's what he asked me.

His eyes are dry. His hands are not quite steady.

IDRIS So when you ask how long, I'll tell you I know what I can prove and nothing more. Proving a thing and surviving the proving are two different jobs. I've had years to learn it.

On screen: Mara (fault record in hand, steady), Idris (at the window, hands gripped behind his back)

MARA The vault. That's where the cables come in. If the beacon's drawing more than it should, the vault's where I'll find the reason.

IDRIS It is.

He turns from the window.

IDRIS But not tonight. It's flooded, the tide's wrong, and I won't take you down there in the dark. Come back at the right water.

MARA Tomorrow.

IDRIS Tomorrow. And Ms. Kell.

He holds her eyes.

IDRIS When you talk to your contractor. Don't put too much in the report. Some things you write down, somebody reads them, and then things happen to the people who wrote them. I'd rather you didn't put a dead man's name where anyone can read it.

Mara goes out into the wind. Halfway down the path she remembers her torch and turns back.

Through the window she sees him at the bench with the folder open under the lamp, not reading it. Only sitting, his hand flat on the cover.

Steadfast, wheelhouse — afternoon

Ambience: heater ticking, wind on glass

The kettle steams. Mara comes in from the deck drying her hands, log open on the chart table.

Sound: radio crackle

SELLA (over radio, smooth) Steadfast, this is Rourke. You went ashore.

MARA I did. Fault's on the supply side. I'll have a diagnosis by tomorrow.

SELLA (over radio) That's not what I'm asking. Did you speak to anyone on the island?

Mara looks at her log. Her square hand, the last entry still wet.

MARA There's a man who keeps the station's old radios. Idris Vane. He showed me the beacon.

A quiet on the line. One second. Two.

SELLA (over radio) Vane. I see.

On screen: Mara (handset at her ear, very still)

SELLA (over radio) I'll remind you of the secure-the-site clause. Anything you find, anything you're told, I hear first, in writing, before anyone else in the world. There are people in this business who ask too many questions. I've had to let them go.

MARA Understood.

Sound: line clicks dead, static returns

SELLA (over radio) Report in the morning. Rourke out.

Steadfast, wheelhouse — night

Ambience: sea against the hull, wind in the rigging

The deck lifts slowly under her. Through the glass, the island is dark and patient to the east. The lighthouse on the hill does not burn white.

On screen: Mara (mug in hand, coat on, looking east)

MARA I told her about the beacon and nothing else. Not the cable. Not the ink.

She tastes the tea. Too strong. She drinks it anyway.

MARA And she heard the name Vane and checked her arrangements were where she left them.

Sound: five-second pulse — three rising tones, one flat

The needle jumps. Mara sets her mug down and opens the log.

Close-up: ship's log, one new line

20:41 — 4.8 MHz. Five seconds. Three rising, one flat. Tide near last of flood.

She caps her pen and closes the log.

MARA She already knew his name. That wasn't news to her. That was an inventory.

She stands at the glass and watches the dark hill.

MARA The vault at the right water, then. And whatever she's afraid of down there, I'd rather hear it from a man who can't lie straight.

The island shows no light at all.
