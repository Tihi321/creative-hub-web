The Turn

The wall above Camp Patience went up in three steps, like a staircase built by a mason who had run out of stone and finished with rubble. Forty metres of broken rock, then a shelf of snow, then the last thirty metres of ice that ran up to the rim and over.

They left at four in the morning, when the snow was still hard and the rock was still frozen together, because a man who wants to go up a step like that wants to do it before the sun loosens it.

Wren carried two days of food in her own pack and the compass in the sock against her ribs on the left side. Her left hand had come back to the wrist. It could open and close. It could not feel the rope, and Vell had stopped asking it to, and had put her on the back of the rope where a mistake costs one person and not two, and had not said so.

They climbed the first step in the dark with their lamps off and their eyes open, which is the cheapest way to climb rock. Wren found the holds by the shape of them against the sky and by the sound the surface made under her glove, and she checked each one by weighting it before she committed, which Vell had drilled into her on the moraine and which she now did without telling herself to. At the top of the step they stood on the shelf and looked down at the basin and the fire, which was a soft orange point in a hole in the world, and the mules were four grey lines beside it, and Crane's big tent had a lamp on inside it, so somebody was awake.

Vell counted her breaths at the shelf. She gave him twenty-eight. He wrote nothing down.

The last thirty metres were ice, and it was blue and hard and it had a skin on it you could not see, and Vell led it in three pitches and put in three screws and took them out behind him, so that when Wren came up the rope there was nothing on the face below her at all. That was how he climbed now. He left the mountain as he had found it, because a good guide takes out every scrap he carried in and leaves the rocks and the flowers and the shy creatures exactly where they belong.

At the top of the ice, at the rim, the ground tipped over and they walked up onto a snowfield that went away north in long shallow waves with no feature on it a person could name.

The sun came up while they walked. It came up on their right and made their shadows four times their length and laid them out ahead of them across the snow, two thin dark lines, one behind the other, and Wren looked at the two lines for a while and then stopped looking, because a shadow is not a thing, it is the absence of a thing, and she had had enough of that.

At six hundred paces she took out the compass.

The needle did not point outward.

She had it in the flat of her right hand, the lid open, level, and the needle should have swung across the case and settled on the rim they were walking towards, because the rim was the only thing out here that was not snow. It did not. It swung across the case and settled pointing back the way they had come.

Wren turned on the spot, slow, and the needle stayed with her, so that it was always pointing behind her, which meant it was not pointing at a place. She turned through a full circle and it followed her round and came back level with her hip and held.

She walked ten paces forward. The needle swung with her and settled pointing back down their own track.

‘Vell.’

He came back and stood beside her and looked at the compass in her hand and did not touch it, and did not ask, and did not move his head.

‘It is pointing at the track,’ she said. ‘It is pointing at the place we came from. It has never done that. It has always had a bearing on the land. This is not a bearing. It is just behind us, whatever behind us is, and behind us is a snowfield and a wall and a man’s fire and four thin mules, and none of that is a thing you could put in a case and carry down and stand in front of a room with.’

Vell looked at the needle for a while.

‘Ask it something,’ he said.

She asked it a single question. She said the words out loud and let the case sit level on her palm and waited.

The needle did not move. It lay where it was, pointing back along their own track, and it did not refuse the way it refused at the flat ice on the tongue, where the needle went dead and flat against the glass like a thing that had decided not to answer.

This time it answered. It was just answering with the same direction as before, and the direction was behind her, and the question she had asked was who had been standing at the gap in the wall in the snow all night, which is not a place and cannot be tracked, so the needle could not have been answering it, so she had asked the wrong question.

She asked the paired question. She had been saving it. She asked where Perrin died, and what the compass wanted.

The needle did not move either. It held its bearing down the track. And down the track, behind them, forty metres below the rim and out of sight, were Vell, and Wren, and the fire, and the mules, and the wall, and all of it together. The needle could not tell fifty answers apart. It had been telling one answer to every question for eight days and nobody had checked the arithmetic.

Wren sat down in the snow.

She sat down because her legs had gone, and she stayed down because once she was down there was no reason to get up, and she got the field book out and the pencil and she began to write it out, because a thing that is written is a thing that can be looked at twice, and she needed to look at this twice.

She wrote the dates on the left of the page and the bearing of the needle on the right, and she went back through the whole of the crossing from the workroom above the shop, from the first question she had asked over the folding rule, which was a question about a lost thing, and the needle had swung to the west wall of the workroom, low down, near the floor, where the rule had lain for four winters under a paving stone.

Then the little terrace and the dial in her hand swinging north-east at the black shoulder of the Icemark. She had taken that for a bearing on a place. She had taken it for the range. She had stood at her own window and looked at the needle and thought it was telling her where to go.

She wrote the moraine and the flat ice and the paired question about the survey cairn, and the needle swinging hard left at the rock rib below the hanging glacier, and Vell confirming it with his own glass, and the cairn being there, and being unreachable, and the serac coming off the fan an hour after they turned towards it.

She wrote Kell’s Mirror and the flat needle over the water, and the case under the ice with the lid carved by Perrin’s own knife, P A, and three words, and the lake cutting their spare line a minute and a half later.

She wrote the ramp and the ramp’s lip and the basin, and last night, at the fire, when she had taken the compass out of the sock and it had slid onto the snow and lain there in the open with the lid shut. And she remembered that in the pocket of her coat, through the cloth, against her ribs, all night, it had been pointing at her own chest, and she remembered this with the particular flatness of a person to whom a thing has just become obvious.

The compass never tracked treasure. It never tracked Perrin’s route. It tracked what its bearer had lost.

She had carried it up a mountain thinking it was a key. It was a wound. Every answer it had ever given her had been a confession, not a direction, and she had read each one as a finger pointing at the range, and the range had been the wrong page the whole time.

Vell sat down on the snow opposite her, three paces away, and put his hands on his knees. He did not look at the compass. He looked at the page she was writing on.

She said, without looking up: ‘It’s been pointing at you since we left the lip of the basin. It is pointing at your back. What did you lose?’

Vell was quiet for as long as it takes to breathe out and breathe back in four times.

‘Nine people,’ he said.

Then he stood up and looked at the sky.

Wren did not stand up. She sat in the snow with the field book on her knee and the compass in her open hand and the needle lying across the case towards Vell’s boots, and she did not write any of it down, because there are two kinds of a thing and only one of them goes in a book.

Vell looked north for a while. Then he looked down the track, at the place the needle was pointing, and he looked at it with the flat look he used for weather, which was the look of a man measuring a thing he had already decided not to talk about.

‘It is up there,’ he said. ‘My last party. It is up above this snowfield and past the rim and through the stair, and I am not going to say the rest of it tonight, and I am walking on now, and you can do the arithmetic on the way or you can do it sitting there, and either way we are still on this snowfield at four thousand metres with two days of food and a man’s fire below us, and if you stand up we will have made six hundred paces of the day and I will count that.’ He shouldered his pack and adjusted the strap over the left shoulder, which he could not lift past level, and the shoulder clicked like a latch and he did not react to it.

Then he walked north, up the snowfield, at his own pace, one breath to a step, and did not look back.

Wren stayed sitting.

She thought about Perrin in the margin of the chart, writing never meant to leave the family, which she had read for six months as an instruction and had now to read as a sentence, the way you read a sentence in a book: a man saying the thing out loud because he was going to die and had nowhere else to put it. She thought about the cairn on the rock rib, unreachable, which the compass had pointed at when she asked what Perrin was doing when he died, and she understood that the needle had not been showing her a route to her master’s last work. It had been showing her her master. She had followed the compass across a range and a lake and a wall because she believed a needle that found lost things would lead her to a lost thing at the top of the range, and all the time it had been leading her to the only lost thing she could not get back, which was the man himself, which was a place she could not climb to.

It had also been pulling at Vell’s pocket the whole way. Nine people. She had watched him stand all night in the snow for no reason she could name, and she had thought it was stubbornness, and it was arithmetic, the same arithmetic she did in her field book. A man carrying a thing the compass could feel, walking up a mountain on purpose.

That meant the artifact was not a chest of paper. A needle that finds loss does not bother with a chest of paper. Whatever was at the top of the range, the compass had never wanted it, and would never have found it, and the whole of the last eight days had been Wren reading a wound as a map and climbing after the wrong thing while the true thing sat in her own coat.

She looked at the snow under her boots. The needle had pointed down at Kell’s Mirror once, under the water, and she had taken that for a case and a lid and three words, and it had also been pointing at the lake and everything the lake had kept, which is another way of saying the same thing.

She wrapped the compass and put it in the sock and put the sock inside her coat and did up the coat.

Then she stood up in her own tracks, which were the two print-holes in the snow that were the only record of a person who had sat down, and she counted her first twenty breaths after Vell, and she walked north.
