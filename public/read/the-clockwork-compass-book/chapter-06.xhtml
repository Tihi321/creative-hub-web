Camp Patience

They crossed the last of Kell's Mirror under the wall, because the ground that went up on the far side went up behind the hanging ice and there was no other way to it.

The far end was not a lake. It was a field of blocks the size of carts, standing on end and lying on their sides, with black gaps between them and a floor of new ice in every gap, frozen the same night it opened. The blocks leaned at their own angles and none of them agreed with any other, and the ice in the gaps was grey and thin and rang hollow when the rope dragged over it.

They put the rope on and kept the coils short and walked it at a fast walk and did not talk. Wren counted her twenties and watched her own boots and put each boot where the last print of her own going had been, which was the rule now on bad ground. Her left hand could not feel the rope, so she held it in her right and kept her eyes on the coils and let her left arm hang, and the armpit of her coat had gone stiff with the wet of it and frozen.

Vell asked for her number before they stepped onto the broken ground. She said five. He kept it in his head like a man keeping change.

The wall stood above the end of the lake, two hundred metres of ice leaning out over nothing, blue along the crack at its base where the light went in and stopped. Under it the slope was dirty snow and old ice balls that had come down before, some of them the size of a man's chest, their faces fresh and sharp and not rained on.

Halfway across the broken ground the wall ticked.

The sound was a fingernail tapped on a pane. Then a run of ice let go somewhere up in the blue and came down the face in small pieces, a hiss, then a rattle, then a dozen white balls bounding out across the slope and stopping fifty metres short of the blocks they were standing on, and lying there, and settling.

Wren did not look up. She kept her eyes on the block in front of her boot, because looking up is a thing you do when you have time, and time was the only thing anyone spent up here.

They came off the ice onto rubble and stood for a minute with their hands on their knees, breathing, at twenty-six breaths a minute, at sixty per cent of a breath, which she had stopped writing down because the number no longer changed. Vell counted his own out loud without meaning to, four and six and eight, which he never did.

Her left hand had come back as far as a dull ache at the wrist and no further. The fingers moved when she told them to. They could not feel the rope. She tied the clove hitch by watching her own hands do it, and checked it by watching it again, and Vell leaned over and put two fingers of his good hand under the knot and pulled, and nodded, and that was the rope check now, and neither of them said so.

Then the ground went up.

At the lake's edge the rubble rose in a ramp along the base of the wall, at the angle of the mill stairs at Lowmarch and a great deal longer, and they put their heads down and climbed it. First on rock, grey and shattered and holding frost in every crack of it. Then on old ice that had come off the wall in some earlier age and frozen onto the ramp in a dirty crust, weathered into ridges, and the ridges ran uphill and she used them for steps. Then on snow that went in and held.

It was the first snow since the tongue that had not been scoured to glass, which meant it was the first snow in a week the wind had not walked on. Snow, at last, that a boot could print. Wren put her glove down into it and closed her fist and opened it and the print stayed, with all five fingers in it, clean.

Two hundred and eighty metres of the ramp. She counted her twenties to keep the rest of her mind in a place she had chosen. Each twenty was four breaths and each breath had a shape to it and the shape was work. Her lips were split at the corner and the split was a small pain she could use for counting, and she used it.

She smelled the fire two hundred metres below the top of the ramp.

Wood smoke and boiled meat and onions, which was wrong, because there are no onions on a mountain. The wrongness of it was what made her stop and stand still with the rope in her hand, so that Vell came up level with her and stopped too and stood breathing and said nothing.

'Fire,' she said.

'Cook fire,' he said. 'Wood, so a crate, and coke under it, so two crates, and a man whose only job today is the fire. That is not a fire that was lit for warmth. That is a fire that was lit to be seen from the valley, and to say that somebody got here first and is still here.' He looked at the ramp above them with the flat look he used for weather. 'Go on up. Show them a party of two with no sledge and no porters and let him do the talking. Do not answer the first thing he says, because the first thing is the one he has practised.'

Then they came over the lip and into the basin.

Camp Patience was a hole in the mountain with a wall around it.

The wall was rock, forty metres high and broken back at the top, and it made a circle three hundred metres across, and the floor of it was flat snow with a stream of meltwater running out through a slot on the far side that a person could step across. The stream's banks were white with frost salt. The snow on the floor was trodden into a hard yellow-grey platform around the fire and pitted with the little rings where pots had stood.

Whoever found it had found the one place in a day's walking out of the wind, and whoever chose it had chosen it the way a man chooses a chair by a fire.

Wren stood on the lip and did what she always did, which was to measure it.

She read the aneroid at four hundred and forty-eight millimetres, which was sixty per cent of her shop window, to the nearest point she could read, and that was the lowest number she had ever written down. She counted the wall's height against the height of a man and got nine men, and wrote forty. She paced the basin's long line and got three hundred and ten paces of her own going and wrote three hundred, and in the margin she wrote the word windbox, and under that the date, and under that the number of breaths she had taken getting over the lip, because that was a measurement too.

Then she got out Perrin's chart and laid it on a crate and turned it to the right quarter, and the basin fell inside the ruled box he had drawn there, the one with nothing in it but the rule and a promise. He had never been here. She had. She drew the circle in the box at a quarter of an inch to a mile and wrote the height in the corner in pencil, small, and she was aware the whole time of the pencil in her hand and of a man watching her use it.

There were crates. There were more crates than Wren had ever seen in one place on the ground: stacked four high against the wall in a dry angle, lashed with line, some stencilled and some plain, one of them open with straw standing out of it, and a tarpaulin over three more that were roped as if they were going to be dragged somewhere. There were two tents, one of them a big one with a stove that put out a curl of smoke of its own. There was a table made of two cases and a board.

And there were mules.

Four of them, picketed in the lee of the wall on a line of wet ground where the stream ran, heads down and blankets on and ribs showing the way snow shows on a rail. One of them stood with its feet apart and did not lift its head when the rope came over the lip. There was a boy beside them with a bucket, and the bucket held barley that had been boiled, and there was steam coming off it, and Wren stood and looked at a pot of boiled barley at four thousand two hundred metres and did the arithmetic of what it had cost to get it there, and stopped, because the arithmetic went on for a while.

Then a man came out from behind the big tent and called her name, and he called it warmly.

'Wren Ashgrove,' he said. 'Perrin's girl. Come in, come in out of it, you look like a person who has been on the ice for a week and eaten like one too.'

Halder Crane was fifty or so and built short and wide, and he had a beard that somebody had trimmed with scissors in a mirror at some point in the last month, and he walked towards her across the snow without hurrying, and when he got to her he took his glove off and put his hand out and shook hers, and his hand was warm, which Wren had not expected and could not account for and did not forget.

'Sit,' he said. 'Somebody get her something with sugar in it. Not the tea, the other one, the one with the milk in it.' He looked at her hand, at the left one, which was inside her coat. 'That one. Frost or water?'

'Water.'

'Then keep it against you and stay near the fire and don't put it in hot water, whatever anyone tells you, and if it blisters I have a proper kit and a man who has used it and a supply of the dressings, and all of that is yours for nothing, so don't argue with me about it.' He sat down on the case by the fire and put his own hands out to it, and he asked, 'Where did you cross?'

'Kell's Mirror. The near shore to the far shore and then under the wall.'

'In one day?'

'In two. We cut a hole.'

'Did you.' He said it the way a man says it at a table in a town. 'How deep?'

'Half a metre through to water. The lake came back on us for it and cut our spare line, so we are one rope and two days of food, and we climbed the ramp on our own fat and nothing else, and I am telling you all of that on purpose, so that you can decide what to do with it and I can watch you decide.'

Crane laughed, and it was a good laugh and not a performance, and he turned to the fire and said, 'She's his, all right. That is Perrin Ashgrove's sentence, that is the same sentence he used on me at the mill in Brack when I offered him a job. He said it flat and then he looked at my hands to see what I would do with it. He was the best line-drawer in the north and I would have paid him four times what a chart shop pays and he would not come, and he died on this range with a pencil in his hand, and I have thought about that most days for six months.' He looked up at her. 'Do you want the tea or not?'

She took the tea. It had milk in it and she had not tasted milk in nine days and the first mouthful of it went through her chest like a hand.

The compass came out at the fire, and she had not meant it to.

She had the field book open on her knee and the pencil between her teeth and the chart unrolled at the edge of her, and she was copying her basin note into the book, which meant the chart was in her lap, which meant the compass was under the chart in its sock, against her thigh, where it always was. One of Crane's porters moved a crate to sit down and knocked the corner of the chart and the compass slid out of the sock onto the snow in its brass case, and for one second nobody at that fire was doing anything else.

Crane looked at it for a moment and then looked at her, and he looked pleased in a way that had no malice in it at all.

'There it is,' he said. 'I sent a man to Lowmarch in the spring. He came back with a story about a little shop on the top bench and a missing instrument and a young woman who was very helpful and very tidy and not quite able to look at him. He was quite put out. You cost me a horse and four days and a good deal of pride.' He drank. 'I will say the offer out loud so that we can both be clear that it has been made, and then I will leave it alone, and you can have your tea. Two thousand crowns, in the bank in Lowmarch, on the day I come down. And on the front sheet of the survey, under my name, printed: surveyed by H. Crane and W. Ashgrove, with the year. Not under it as an apprentice. Beside it. Your father's name in a proper catalogue, and yours held up next to it, and the whole of the north reading both every time they open the case. Now, that is the offer. There is no third offer. I don't ask a person three times. I ask twice and I shake their hand and I go and I find out what they did with the answer. That's the way I have run every expedition I have ever run and it has never once failed me.'

'You offered twice to my master too,' Wren said.

'I did. And he said no twice, and I funded his last season out of my own pocket anyway, because a man like that is worth paying for whether or not he ever works for you, and I would like you to know that, because I do not think he ever told you.' Crane put his mug down. 'The mountain does not give itself to the deserving, Wren. It gives itself to whoever is still standing on it when the weather lifts. A thing belongs to whoever wants it most and is willing to say so. That is not a vice and it is not a boast. It is the whole story of the range, and it is the only reason anyone has ever come down from it with anything at all.'

Wren closed the lid of the compass and put it back in the sock and the sock back inside her coat, and she did not say yes and she did not say no, because saying no twice to a man like this was a thing that could be done once and only once and she wanted to spend it properly.

There was a woman on the far side of the fire with a board on her knees.

She had a lamp on the board and a stone on each corner of the paper to hold it down, and she was drawing with the flat side of the pencil held at a low angle so that the line came out grey and even, which is how you draw ground you are not sure about. There were eleven sheets under her left hand, numbered in the corner, and the top one was a ridge.

Wren looked at the ridge and then looked again, because she had come over the bottom of that ridge two days ago.

'Your headwall,' she said. 'It runs right-hand down to the bowl. I came over it. It is a hundred and forty metres from the trough to the rim, and I counted it twice on the climb and once again coming down in my head, so call it a hundred and forty with four either side.'

The woman looked up. 'A hundred and forty-two,' she said. 'I have it at a hundred and forty-two from the lake, with the bottom of the trough below the rim at four one five, and an error of six on the whole thing, and I will take your number and put mine in the margin and initial yours, because two measurements by two people are worth more than mine twice, and I have not had anybody else's number in eleven days.' She turned the corner of the sheet and wrote 140 in the box and W A underneath it and the date beside that. 'Juno Rell. I am the surveyor on this expedition and I have been drawing the same ridge since the sixth and it comes out different every time and I have not had a single day when I could say why. Eleven sheets. Take one, they're not mine, they're his, and looking at them won't do anything to me that has not already been done.'

'The range is moving,' Wren said.

'The range is moving,' Juno said, 'and one of us is going to write that sentence in a report and sign it, and the other one is going to write it in a report and not sign it, and we both know which is which, so don't. Yet.' She looked across the fire at Crane, who was talking to a porter about fuel and not listening, and then she looked back at Wren, and she said, quite levelly, 'Ask him what he thinks the artifact is. He'll tell you. He can't not. It's the only reason he brought me.'

So Wren asked him.

'What is the thing at the top,' she said. 'Not where it is. What it is.'

Crane turned round on the case and put his elbows on his knees, and he was pleased to be asked, and it showed.

'It is a survey the Icemark keeps rewriting,' he said. 'We think of a map as a promise the ground made us. This range does not make promises, it makes minutes, and it revises them. Your master worked it out and wrote it on a lid with a nail, and he was right, and he was the only person alive who had understood that the thing at the top was never a chart. It is a practice. Every few years the range puts out a fresh opinion of its own shape, in its own hand, and the shrines hold them, and the reason nobody has ever brought one down is that nobody has ever been willing to say out loud that the last one was wrong, because a surveyor's whole life is saying the last one was right and tidying up the edges cheaply.' He held his hands out to the fire. 'We are going to bring the stack down, under crates, in order, dated, whole, without a corner bent. And we are going to stand in front of a room in the capital with it and say: this is what the range did, and this is when, and this is the copy we took, and here is the man who drew it, and the men who carried it. That is the find. That is the whole find.' He smiled at her across the fire. 'That's what we're here for. It is a better answer than money. It is a better answer than the compass, and you may keep the compass, and I mean that, and I will put it in writing if you like, because I have already decided that I am going to be generous to you in front of everybody here and I would like you to watch yourself having been generous to and let that sit with you for a day or two.'

Wren looked at Juno Rell across the fire, and Juno Rell had her pencil down on the board and her eyes up, and she did not say a word, and she did not smile, and she did not back him. She held Wren's look for the length of a breath and then went back to the eleventh sheet and put the flat of the pencil on the paper and drew.

Wren had seen two sorts of silence now: Vell's, which was a door with something behind it, and this, which was a door with nothing, and a person standing in front of it waiting for someone else to open it first.

They gave her a place in the big tent and food off the fire, and she ate it.

It was meat and onions and a potato and it had been cooked in a pot by a man whose whole job was that pot, and it was the first hot food she had eaten in nine days and it was very good and she ate all of it. Twenty minutes later she went out past the mules and was sick behind a crate, quietly, on her knees, holding her own hair out of the way with the hand that worked.

Vell was at the gap in the wall. He had been at the gap since they came in, standing in the slot where the stream ran out, with the wind going through it at what she measured as the whole of the weather, looking out and down the valley and up at the sky in turns, and he had not come to the fire at all.

She took him a mug of the cook's tea and a piece of bread with fat on it.

'There's food,' she said.

'I have food,' he said, and he did, and he took a piece of biscuit out of his own pocket and ate it where she could see him do it, which was a way of answering two questions at once.

'It's his fire,' she said.

'It is his fire, his bread, his fat, his pot, his cook, and his two thousand crowns, and his sheet of paper with your name beside his. Stand there a while and count what he has given us and then count what he has asked us for, and you will find that the first number is bigger and that the second one is the only one that matters.' He looked at the wall of the tent with the stove in it and the smoke going up into a still pocket of air inside the wall, where nothing blew it, and he did not go on. 'Sleep with your boots on and your mittens inside your coat. If anything comes off the wall at the top tonight you will have about six seconds and you will not be doing up laces in it. I will be here.'

She stood with him a while in the gap with the tea going cold in her hand. She offered the mug across, and he let it stand between them, and she drank it herself. The wind came through the slot at the height of her shoulder and took the heat out of the side of her face while the fire side of it stayed warm, and she stood with one side of her head hot and the other side of it freezing, which was the most exact thing that had happened to her all day.

Then her feet went from cold to hurting, all at once, the way they had when her hand came out of the lake, and then from hurting to nothing at all, and when she could not any longer tell by any feeling in them where her feet were, she went in to bed.

She lay in the tent in the dark with the stove ticking and the smell of onions and wood smoke in her coat, and outside the gap in the wall a man stood the whole night in snow that came and went. He let the fire be somebody else's. He ate his own biscuit and nothing off Crane's board. When she came out once, near midnight, to check the anchors on the tent lines, he was still there in the slot with his back to the wind, and he did not ask her for anything, and she did not ask him anything either, and the two of them stood a hundred metres apart in the falling snow, listening to the wall.
