The Glass Stair

They walked the snowfield for four hours.

It ran north in long shallow waves, and the waves were so alike that after the first hour Wren could not have said whether they had crossed three of them or nine, and by the second hour she had stopped trying. No rock broke the surface. No crevasse showed. Nothing had been scoured or carved or written on by the wind. She counted paces until the count reached four hundred and then let it go, because a count with nothing to check it against is only a number a person has made up.

Once she stopped and turned a full circle, and the snow held the print of her boots and her pack and nothing else in any direction, and she turned back to their own two lines of tracks and stepped into them and went on.

The compass lay against her ribs, inside the sock, inside her coat. She took it out twice. Both times the needle lay across the case on the same bearing, steady, without swing or tug, and both times she put it away without asking it anything. She had stopped believing hours ago that a needle on a snowfield could point at a place. A thing that finds what a person has lost lies quiet where nothing has been lost yet and jumps when the ground where it was lost comes near, and that was all this was. It was not a guide. It was a bill, and it arrived after the meal.

At the fourth hour the snowfield ended.

It ended the way a table ends, without notice, at a lip of wind-packed snow that had been undercut by weather, and past the lip there was air and the far side of the valley and the wall.

Wren went down on her belly and put her head over the edge.

The cliff fell away in one clean sweep of two hundred metres or more, and it did not fall the way she had seen rock fall. There were steps in it. They stood out of the face in long flights, and at every landing the flight turned and went on again, so that the whole face carried a stair from the scree below to the rim where they lay, corners squared, treads levelled, in a face where no two living maps agreed there was anything at all.

She got Perrin's chart out and laid it flat on the snow with a stone at each corner. There was no stair on it. There was the ruled box in the empty quarter, and inside the box a pencil line that stopped halfway and never went on.

She looked along the rim for where the stair met it, and there it was, twenty metres to her left, coming up out of the face onto the shelf they were lying on, the top tread wide enough for a man to stand on.

'It is twenty metres behind us,' she said. 'We walked past it.'

'We did not,' Vell said. 'It was not there at the rim when we came up. I looked.'

'It is there now.'

'Yes.'

He studied it with the flat look he used for weather.

'I have been past the second moraine,' he said. 'I have not been up this. The maps put the stair a day east of here and a hundred metres lower, and I have known those maps to be right about where a thing is and wrong about where it has got to.' He stood and coiled the rope off his shoulder and held it out to her. 'You lead the first pitch.'

Wren had never led anything.

She had followed the whole crossing, on the back of the rope, with a hand that could not feel the rope, and following meant that a mistake stopped with her. Leading meant the mistake went down the rope and took Vell with it.

She put on her harness and tied in. She checked the knot by looking at it, feeling it, and looking at it again. She took the rack off Vell's sling and put it over her shoulder, and the weight of it pulled her down on the left, so she moved two of the big cams to the right side and stood up straighter.

They were on rock now. The last tread of the stair was dry grey stone with a texture like a file, and under her boots it was nothing like the ice she had walked on for six days.

'Your boots,' Vell said.

She looked at them. Rigid ice boots, front points, built to stand on two steel teeth in ice and to do nothing else. On dry rock the sole was a flat plate and the edge was a steel rail, and neither had any grip in it. She sat down and took them off.

The rock shoes were in the bottom of her pack, wrapped in a wool sock to keep the rubber from going stiff. She got them out and put them on, and they were thin and soft and her feet felt naked in them, and when she stood the holds came up through the sole the way a coin comes up through a glove, small and exact and readable. Boots for ice, shoes for rock, and back and back. Vell had said it on the moraine and she had written it in the load list without understanding a word of it, and now her feet understood it before her head.

She started up.

The first pitch was forty metres at about the steepness of a ladder, and the holds were cut into the rock, small square edges the width of two fingers laid one above the other up the face. She climbed with her hands on the edges and her body out from the rock, and the rope ran down to Vell and then stopped running, and she knew he had her.

Halfway up the cut edges ran out. The rock went smooth and blank for four metres with a single crack up the middle of it, hairline, running vertically, thinner than her little finger.

She stopped.

She had a rack of protection on her shoulder that she had never placed in her life. She had watched Vell place it on every pitch of the crossing and it had looked like a man making small talk with a tool. She took off a nut, a wedge of metal on a wire, and threaded the wire through the crack at chest height and pulled it up snug, and it rattled.

She sat back on the rope and took it out and looked at the crack again. It narrowed downward. A nut under load pulls downward, and a nut that rattles in a crack that narrows downward will wedge, because the narrow part is underneath it. She put it back, pulled it up, jerked the wire with the heel of her hand, and it did not move. She clipped the rope to it.

Then she climbed the blank four metres with the nut below her and a smaller one above it, and her toes found enough in the flat stone to stand on, and she did not fall. At the top she built an anchor out of three nuts in a horizontal crack and a sling over a horn of rock, and brought Vell up.

He came up and tested her anchor with the heel of his hand, downward, hard. It held. He said nothing about it, which she had learned by now was the highest thing he had to say.

'Cam, next time the crack widens,' he said. 'A nut in a widening crack will walk out. A cam has springs in it and the springs make it hold by itself.'

'I know what a cam is.'

'Then you would have put one in at the corner there instead of the nut on the wire, and you would have been right to.' He was already coiling. 'A wedge wants a crack that narrows. The spring wants a crack that does not.' He tapped the four-lobed device on his own rack and unhooked it and put it on her sling, and did not explain it further, and she did not need him to. She had watched him place one on the wall below the shrine and she had it in her hand a moment later and it made sense in her hand, which is how tools are.

They climbed the stair all day, and the stair did not behave like a stair.

The flights ran between ten and forty metres and turned at each landing, and every fourth or fifth flight the treads went under a glaze of ice that had frozen over the cut edges in the night. Then Wren stopped at the landing and took the soft shoes off and the rigid boots on, and pulled the crampons over them, and went up on front points and axes, and at the top of that flight she reversed the whole order, because on a cut stair the ice is thin and the rock is what holds.

Her hands learned the order before her head did. By the afternoon she was doing it with her gloves in her teeth and her eyes already on the pitch above, and the only part of her that still thought about it was the part that would have to write it in the field book in the evening, if there was an evening.

At noon she took the altitude reading and passed the aneroid to Vell.

'Four hundred and fifty-one. Fifty-nine per cent.'

He nodded. He did not ask for her headache number and she did not give it, because it had gone from a five to a six and she did not want to hear the six said out loud.

She counted her own breaths at rest on the landing. Thirty-two. At home it had been sixteen.

They heard Crane before they saw him, because sound carries up a face the way it does not carry down one.

The first thing she heard was a man's voice giving an order twice, patiently, and then the small steel knock of a hammer and the long travelling hiss of rope paid out over rock. She looked down and there was the wall below, and on the wall a line of four figures coming up the stair from the scree, and behind them, at the foot of the cliff, a fifth man standing at a stove, and above it all the crates and the mules on the valley floor, as small as dice.

Two flights below her, on the same face, Halder Crane was coming up the stair. He had left Camp Patience without his sledge and without his crates and put his porters on a cliff they had never climbed, and he was doing it fast.

She watched a man come off a landing onto the pitch below her. He moved like a man who had been hired rather than a man who had chosen. The rope went up from him to a second man, and then a third, and they were close enough that she could see the coil on the last man's shoulder.

'He has four,' Vell said.

'Four and the one at the stove.'

'Four and the one at the stove,' Vell agreed, and spat over the edge, which marked nothing.

They climbed on. Above, the stair went up into a band of shadow where the whole face stood in shade; below, the porters came up one flight at a time, and once Wren looked down and saw the man on the second pitch standing on the flight she had led three hours before, his feet in the same cut edges.

The two parties were not on the same face. It sloped, and anything that came off the upper part of it came off directly onto the men below, and the upper part was thawing in the sun, and twice she heard a stone come loose above her and go past her ear like a bird.

'Rope up short,' Vell said. 'Directly above me. Keep me between you and the men below.'

She was on the fourth flight of the afternoon, in the soft shoes, thirty metres up, when she came onto a landing and stood on it and could not remember how she had got there.

That was the whole of it. The landing was there and she was on it, and the pitch below went down into shadow, and a man on that pitch was clipped into a nut she had placed four hours before, and her gloves were in her teeth because it had been warm. She stood and worked out what must have happened, and the working out was correct, and it did not help, because she could not bring back to her the moment of standing up from the flight below and putting her hands on the rock. There was no hole in the day. There was one piece of it that had not been written down.

She shook her head slowly and the mountain leaned a little and came back.

She looked at her watch. The watch said the same time it had said on the last landing, which was not possible, so she looked again and the hands were where they should be and she had not read them the first time.

She sat down, which is what a person with a thin head ought to do, and took out the field book and wrote the hour and her breath count and the plain fact that she had lost a piece of the afternoon and would have to say so out loud to Vell, in the plainest hand she had, rounding nothing down.

Then she checked the tie of her harness and the clove hitch at the anchor and the nut and the cam on the pitch below, all of which she had already checked, and she did not remember checking those either, so she checked them again and watched her own hands do it, and this time the checking stayed with her.

Hypoxia, Vell had said at the edge of the mirror, in the flat voice of a man reading a notice. It goes quietly, and it goes before the legs.

She stood up and climbed the next flight slowly, calling each hold out loud in a low voice, one word at a time, so that the words would keep her where she was.

At the top of the sixth flight the stair became a ledge that ran back into the face, and from the ledge the stair went on, thread after thread, cut one above the other into the glassy top of the cliff, visible for a hundred metres.

Wren came onto the ledge and stood on it and took the compass out of the sock, because she had earned one look at it before dark.

The needle lay across the case, steady, pointing up the stair, still and certain, and it was pointing at the rock in front of her, at the cut tread twenty metres above her head, and not past it, and not at anything beyond it at all. It pointed at the stair itself as though the stair were a room and not a direction, as though the whole of what the compass wanted were the treads and the work and nothing at the top of them.

She turned the case slowly in her hand, and the needle turned with it and came back to the same tread above her, and Wren understood that the compass had never once asked her to reach the summit. It had been asked, eight days ago in a workroom, what she had lost, and it had answered the whole way up with the truth of who she had lost, and it was answering still, and the answer was the climb.

She closed the lid and put it in the sock and buttoned her coat.

Below them, on the flight under the ledge, a man paid out rope for the man beneath him, and the rope hissed along the rock, and Crane's voice came up from somewhere under the ledge, cheerful and carrying and tired, saying that they had better get on before the sun came off the face.

Vell was already looking up the stair, counting the threads with a finger in the air. He did not look at her and he did not ask.

Wren put her gloves in her teeth and checked the laces on the soft shoes and stood up.
