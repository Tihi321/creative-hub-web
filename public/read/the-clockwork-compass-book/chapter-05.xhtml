Kell's Mirror

They crossed the rest of the tongue by numbers, and the numbers were Vell's.

Fifty minutes of moving and ten of standing, and the ten were taken standing up, because sitting down at that height was a decision you had to pay for when you stood again. Three hundred metres of vertical in a day and not one more. Water out of the bottle inside his coat every hour, warm and flat and tasting of brass, and then her bottle, and then his again. And the last number he did not say out loud. On the second morning he held up two fingers, and she nodded, and that was the food, and neither of them named it.

The tongue climbed and narrowed and turned white over its whole surface, and the whiteness was the wind-scoured ice where the flour had been blown off and the ice under it was hard enough for the front points to bite. Above that the slope stood up at last into the headwall of the bowl, and the line Perrin had drawn ran up the right-hand side of it in a thin unbroken pencil and stopped at the top, where the pencil became a dotted line, and the dotted line was not a route. It was the edge of what he had seen.

Wren took the aneroid out at the foot of the last rise and read it and wrote it down.

Four hundred and sixty-two millimetres, and a little. In the shop window before she left it had been seven hundred and forty-one, with the pointer down.

She worked the arithmetic while she walked, because arithmetic was a thing she could do with her hands in her pockets. Seven hundred and forty-one was what the sky weighed at the level of the mill. The air here weighed sixty-two per cent of what it had at the mill, and the sixty-two per cent was the whole of the day. Sixty-two per cent of a breath was what she got each time she took one, and she had counted her breaths at rest in the shop at sixteen a minute, and at rest on the ice at the foot of the rise she counted twenty-six, and the twenty-six breaths a minute gave her about the same as sixteen at home, and it cost her more to get it.

She put the aneroid away. She looked at the ice in front of her and did not look up.

The head came on before the top, and it came as a band across the front of her skull, above her eyes, tight, the way a hat is tight.

She had had headaches before. She had had the headache that comes from ruling a long line badly, and the one that comes from a window left open on the stair, and neither of them was this. This one had a floor to it. When she bent to tighten a crampon strap it moved inside her head like something poured, and when she stood up there was a half-second of grey at the edges of the picture, and then the picture was back and she was standing and nothing had happened.

Vell stopped them at the ten and stood with his back to the wind and looked at her while she drank.

‘Head,’ he said.

‘Nothing to write down.’

‘That was not the question.’ He waited. The wind went round them and took the end of the rope and moved it, and he let it, because the rope on the ice was not a rope he was using.

‘Four,’ she said.

‘Out of what.’

‘Out of ten. Behind the eyes. It started below the last trough and it has not moved since.’ She said it flatly, the way she would report a frost crack in a pane. ‘What is the number I am allowed.’

‘There is not one. There is a name for what happens to a body that stays above this line too long, and it is not a bruise and it is not a cold. The body stops making a proper account of itself. It keeps the water in the wrong places, and the blood thickens, and the judgement goes before the legs do, and it goes quietly, so that a person cannot tell by asking themselves. That is why we are not asking you. That is why we are counting.’ He looked at the sky over the headwall and then at the ice. ‘It does not get better by getting used to it. It gets better by going down. We are going down in four days and not before. So we go slowly, and we go in the cool of the day, and you tell me the number every time I ask, and you do not round it down to be kind to me.’

‘That is the same as rounding it up to be honest with you.’

‘Yes,’ he said. ‘Do that instead.’

She had eaten half a biscuit at the ten and the other half had gone into her pocket, and the nausea came on the last rise in a slow grey wave and settled behind her teeth and stayed, patient, like a dog sitting down.

The bowl's rim was a hundred and forty metres of ice at forty degrees, and they took it in one push because standing on a slope like that was more expensive than climbing it. She counted her steps in twenties and lost the count twice and found it again by looking at the runnels, which were consistent, and ran up the face in front of her left boot and did not move, because they were frozen.

Then the ground was flat and they were over, and the whole of what came next was in front of them, and Wren stood still with the rope in her hand and looked at it, because looking at it was free.

Kell's Mirror lay in a bowl of its own under a wall of ice, and the wall was two hundred metres of hanging glacier leaning out over the far end of the lake with a blue crack running along its base. The lake was ice, grey-white and matte, with long white streaks where wind-blown snow had filled the cracks in it and been pressed down. It ran about two kilometres from the near shore to the point where the hanging ice stood over it, and it curved a little to the left at the far end, the way water does when it is finding its way out of a bowl, and the curve was the only thing on it that was not flat.

The ice was clear in patches. Where the wind had polished it she could look down through half a metre of it and see the grey going deeper and then stopping, and below the stopping there was nothing she could see at all.

She got the field book out and wrote the lake down the way she had been taught to write open water: extent, curve, the shoreline both sides, the hanging ice above the east end and its base, and in the margin, small, what the pencil had said and what the pencil had not.

Vell read the sheet over her shoulder, which he had never done before.

‘How long to cross,’ he said.

‘Two kilometres flat, on ice with a scoured surface. Four hundred metres an hour if we move well. Five hours, and we lose an hour to the curve and the last section under the wall, because under the wall we walk in whatever falls off it.’ She capped the pencil. ‘Under the wall is where the ice is moving. That is why the far end is broken and this end is not. If we are going to hear anything, we will hear it under the wall, and it will be at the far end, where there is nowhere to be except on the lake.’

‘Then we cross it in the morning, when it is cold and quiet, and we spend the night on this shore, and I will hear your number at dark and again at first light.’ He swung his pack down off his shoulder, carefully, using the right arm to take the weight off the strap, and set it on the rock. ‘And there is something here you should see before you sleep, because you are a surveyor and you would rather see a thing than be told it.’

The ice on the near shore, where the lake met the rubble, had been walked on.

The tracks ran out onto the lake in a single line, and the line was not a climber's line, because a climber on a frozen lake spreads out and picks his own way. It was a file. Boot after boot, the same boot twice, left and right and left, each print close behind the one in front of it, and beside the file there was a second mark, a smooth shallow groove, four inches wide, running the whole way and not stopping, and a third mark and a fourth beside that one, parallel, a hand's width apart.

‘Loads,’ Vell said. He crouched with one knee down, which was how he did everything now. ‘That is a man carrying a crate on his back with a tumpline, and he is walking where the man in front of him walked, because he has been told to. These' — the groove, and the other groove beside it — ‘are a sledge. Two runners. Dragged, not carried. There is a party here of ten or twelve, and they went out onto the lake together and they came back off it the same way, or they did not.'

Wren put her hand flat in a boot print. It had set hard, and the new snow in it was a thin film, and the film had been through one warm afternoon and one cold night.

‘Wednesday,’ she said. ‘Two days, perhaps three. He is ahead and he is not far ahead, and he is still going up, because the tracks return and then the file leaves again on the far side of the shovel marks where they filled their bottles.’ She stood up. ‘Twelve people walking in single file under loads on an ice lake under a hanging glacier. He has three porters to every climber and he puts one man behind the other so that they walk in the same twelve footprints and the weight goes into ice that has already taken it. That is not carelessness. That is very careful indeed.'

‘It is careful and it is slow,’ Vell said, ‘and it is why he was a week ahead of us in the valley and why he is two days ahead of us here. A man who carries a sledge can wait out a storm. A man who carries a sledge has to know where he is going, because he cannot turn round quickly.’ He stood up. ‘There is the mark of the hole they cut, off to the right.'

It was where the lake met the shore, in the shelter of the rock, and it was a ring of chipped ice frozen into a raised circle, the chips still sharp, and in the middle of the ring a disc of newer ice half a metre across, refrozen and slightly proud and slightly darker than the rest of the lake.

‘They took water here,’ Wren said. ‘Through half a metre of ice in the warm, before the first night, with a saw and a pot and a boy to knock the chips clear, and then it froze over again in eight hours, grey and clean. That is a fact about the lake and I am writing it down. It takes a hole. And it heals the hole overnight and does not care that it was a hole.'

They ate, and she kept half of hers, and Vell did not comment on the half, and she slept in pieces with her mouth against the inside of the bothy bag and the headache steady above her eyes, and at first light he asked for the number and she said five, and he said, ‘Better or worse than four,' and she said, ‘It is the same four said by a person who has not slept,' and he said, ‘Then it is better than four,' and they put their packs on.

They crossed Kell's Mirror in the cold, on the flat, with the rope on and the coils short between them, and for the first hour it was like walking on a floor.

There was no crevasse flagging to read and nothing to correct on the chart, because there was nothing to draw but the shape of the lake, and she had drawn it. She walked and counted her steps and listened to the crampon points, which went in and rang, and the ring was the same for two hundred metres and then it went dull, and the dullness was older ice, and the older ice was safe. The far wall came and went in the cloud above them. Once the cloud came down and took the wall's base out and there was blue over their heads and no sight of the top, and then the cloud went up again the way water goes off a shelf.

At four hundred metres from the near shore, she noticed that the ice under her boots had changed colour by a shade and had begun to sound different under the rope when she dragged it.

At six hundred metres she got the compass out, and she did it because the headache had moved, and she was honest enough, afterwards, to know that she wanted a number.

She did not ask it anything. It came out of the sock in its brass case and she held it flat on her glove and did not open the lid, and then she opened the lid, and the needle was lying down flat on the card against the glass.

Her first thought was that it was broken, and the thought came with a small cold shock that had nothing to do with the temperature, because she had seen two broken compasses in the shop, one dropped and one rusted through the pivot, and a compass that lies down on the glass is a compass that has been dropped.

She got down on the ice on one knee and set the brass case flat on the surface, and the needle lay down again, flat, hard against the glass, pointing at nothing on the card and at everything under the floor.

‘Vell,’ she said.

He came back along the rope and stood and looked down at it, and the wind took his breath away in white, and he said nothing at all for a count of ten.

‘It is not broken,’ Wren said. ‘It is doing the only thing it can do with a bearing that is not in this plane. It cannot point at the middle of the lake, because the middle of the lake is beside it. It is pointing down. That is where the answer is.’ She was talking, she knew, to keep the arithmetic out of her chest, where it had arrived a moment ago and was sitting, cold and certain. ‘It will not answer one question. So I ask two, and both of them have to be in the same place, and I have to choose the pair so that the two answers are in the same place and that place is down there, and not up at the stair, or at the top, or anywhere else in the range. And I will pay for it. I want you to know that I know that, and that I am going to ask anyway, because whatever this is, he put it here for a reason and I am the person he put it here for, and I am not going to walk over the top of it and tell myself that a survey is a survey.'

‘Ask it,’ Vell said.

She said both questions out loud, one after the other, plainly, because a question said under your breath is a question you can pretend you did not ask.

‘Where is the survey case my master left on this lake, and where is the last thing he wrote by his own hand?'

The needle went down and stayed down and did not move at all until she closed the lid on it.

They cut the hole ninety metres out from the point where the needle had gone flat, which let them do the work on the bare grey ice away from the shore rubble, and they cut it in the old way, which is to say with the sharp end of an axe and then with the axe's pick, and it took a long time.

The first twenty centimetres were white and crumbled and came off in plates. Then the ice went hard and grey, and the pick rang on it, and every stroke put a white star into the grey and nothing else. Wren worked until her right arm burned at the elbow, and then Vell took the axe in his one hand and worked with the neat economical strokes of a man who has cut holes in ice for forty years, and the chips came off in curls and flew, and her job was to knock the curls out of the ring with the flat of her glove so that they did not freeze back into it.

At half a metre the ice went clear. She could see chips of her own, frozen in, from the seventh or eighth stroke, suspended in the ice like seeds in glass, and below the chips the ice was clear all the way and then there was the dark, and the dark was water and the water was deep.

The pick went through on the next stroke and the grey went blue and water came up the hole, so cold that it smoked where the air met it, and it rose in the hole to the level of the lake's own level and lay there and did not freeze for one moment, and then the first skin came across it and began to thicken.

She had stopped believing that she would finish before the hole did. Then the pick came back up through the water with something on it that was not ice, and it was not a chip, and she stopped.

It was a corner. A corner of a box, in the ice below the level of the water, at the hole's edge, held in a lens of clear ice that ran sideways under the surface in a thin layer a hand wide. The wood was black and the corner was brass with green on it, and past it, out of reach in the same layer, there was more.

The case had gone into the water and sunk to the bottom of a shallow margin and been covered by the refreeze of that winter, and the winter after that, and each winter had put a layer over it, and the ice had closed round it the way ice closes round everything and holds it exactly as it was.

Her glove had a hole at the thumb and she worked with the axe anyway, chipping a channel around the corner, and the water in the hole kept rising to its own level and the new skin kept forming, and every time she put her hand near it the skin broke and the cold came up off the water like something with a mouth. Vell put in two ice screws back from the hole's edge and ran the spare line through them as a haul line, and she tied the line to the case with a clove hitch in the one place where the brass corner would take it, and then she got both hands under the case and lifted.

The case came out of the ice with a small ugly noise, and her left forearm went into the water to the elbow, all at once, glove and cuff and sleeve.

She got the case up onto the ice and then she was on her knees with her arm out of the hole and her hand inside her coat against her ribs, wrist to heart, because that was the only warm place on the mountain and it had to be used fast. The pain arrived about forty seconds later, the deep dull bone pain that comes when blood comes back into something, and she sat on the ice with her jaw shut and let it. Her hand was beside the compass, inside her coat, both hands pressed to the same two ribs, and she could feel the case of it through the sock and could not feel her fingers at all.

‘Hold it there,’ Vell said. ‘Ten minutes. If you take it out now you will lose the tips and the tips are more useful to me than the case is. What is in it.'

‘Rotted through. The leather is black. The papers have gone to a mat.’ She looked at it on the ice in front of her with her good hand on her knee. ‘There is writing on the inside of the lid. Carved, not inked. With a point of some kind, and hard enough to raise the grain under, because the grain is standing up around it like a scar. P A, scratched at the top. And below it three words and no more room, because he carved them small to fit, and he was a man who always measured the space before he wrote in it.'

She read them out loud.

‘It keeps changing.'

The lake cracked a minute and a half later, and the crack started at the hole.

It went with a noise like a plucked wire doubled and tripled, a long rising hum that came out of the ice and up through her knees, and then the hum broke into a boom, and the boom ran away from them across the middle of the lake towards the far end, under the hanging wall, and went on and on the way a train goes on when you are standing at the top of a cutting and it has gone past.

‘Up,’ Vell said. ‘Off the ice. Not fast. Fast is how a person falls over at this height. Walk to the shore, keep your points flat, and do not look at the crack, look at the shore.'

They walked. The ice moved under them the whole way — not much, a slow swell, as though the whole plate of the lake had considered something and come down on one side of it, and along the hole's edge the clear ice that had been under the water was now standing out of it in a raised rim. Wren's coil of spare line, thirty metres of it, had been lying unclipped on the ice beside the screws while she held her hand to her ribs, and as the plate shifted the seam opened under the coil and swallowed it down to the last two turns.

She got her hand out of her coat and grabbed the end. It went taut and she had a moment of thinking that she had it, and then the two plates of the lake came together again with a sound like a door in a house where nobody lives, and the line was cut so cleanly that she was standing with two metres of it hanging from her fist and the sharp end swinging, and the rest was in the ice and would be for a hundred winters.

She looked at the cut end. It was flat and slightly frayed, exactly like the food sack.

They got off the lake onto the shore rubble at the far side, and Wren put the case down on a stone and sat beside it with her back against the rock and her bad hand inside her coat, and the ice went on talking behind them, a long boom, then quiet, then another long boom further round towards the wall, and each boom came out under the hanging glacier and found the wall and stopped there.

Vell set the stove going in the lee of the rock and put snow in the pot with the hand that worked, and the water came to a boil and no further, and they drank it with sugar in it and watched the lake.

‘Spare line gone,’ Wren said. ‘That is thirty metres and our second rope for the abseils off the stair, and the case is worth nothing but a lid, and we have two days of food and a day and a half of climbing to the stair, and we have made the biggest hole anyone has made on this lake and we have made the ice angry two kilometres in both directions, and we have to walk out over it in the morning to get the ground that goes up.’ She said it level, into the pot. ‘What did it take, Vell. This time. Whose was the nearest body this time.'

‘Both of us,’ he said. ‘That is what a rope is. Eat your food. Tomorrow we cross back over what we just did, and it will be there, and it will be a little wider, and I have been over worse.’ He drank. Above them, out of the cloud, the hanging wall answered the lake with one soft, terrible tick.

She kept her own hand inside her coat, all night, against her ribs, on the brass.
