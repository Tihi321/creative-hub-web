The Collapse

The storm came in from the north at ten in the evening and settled onto the bowl of Hollowmere like a hand over a cup. Wren lay in her bag in the house above the wall and counted thirty-two breaths at rest before she stopped counting, because counting had become a way of frightening herself, and she had a day's work in the morning and no use for fear.

Outside, the wind went up and down the street and shook the loose sheets of ice that hung off the roofs. Near midnight something let go on the wall above the village and came down with a long grinding sound and stopped.

Vell sat in the doorway with his back to the frame. He had his hat off, which he did now without being asked, and his left arm rested across his knee with the hand turned palm-up, the way a man holds an arm that will not lift.

‘It will be a white morning,’ he said. ‘Two feet on the bowl and not settled. Nothing will hold on the face above the stair.’

‘Then Crane will not fire.’

Vell looked at the dark outside the door for a while.

‘He has been at that corner with the lamp since the day we came,’ he said. ‘He has the levels in his book. He has a man at the stove keeping the powder dry. A man who carries a hundred and sixty kilos up a stair does not stop for weather. He has been telling himself the same thing all week that I have been telling myself for nine years: one more morning and the mountain will agree with me.’

Wren turned on her side so she could see him.

‘What do you want to happen?’

‘I want the morning to come,’ Vell said. ‘I have wanted that for nine years and it has never once been a useful want.’

At four she was awake before the light. The snow lay as he had said, two feet on the bowl, drawn into long feathers along the house walls. The sky over the rim had the flat yellow underside that comes before a real storm and not just a snowfall.

She put her boots on and laced them and went out. The street was knee-deep and the trench where the ice had gone back had filled to its lip and showed no seam. The night watch’s tracks were already half gone.

Crane’s lamp burned at the shrine end of the bowl. She could see the yellow point of it through the snow, and the crates, which no longer sat under the sledge but stood in a line against the shrine wall with their lids off, and the stove, which was alight, because a thread of heat-shimmer stood over it and bent the falling snow as it went.

He had set four charges. She counted the wires coming out of the drilled holes at the shrine corner and running along the ice to the stove, and there were four, each thick as her little finger.

Juno came out of the shrine with her bundle strapped high on her back and her field case under her arm. She stopped beside Wren and looked at the same four wires.

‘Two and three-quarter kilos each,’ she said. ‘Four of them. He has not asked me to check the set, and I would not, because it is wrong, and I will not have my initials on it.’

‘How wrong?’

‘The ice at that corner is a metre and a half thick and it is holding the whole west face of the shrine. One charge would take the door. Four will take the corner, the wall behind it, and about six thousand tonnes of the bowl floor that has been waiting nine years for a reason.’

Wren looked at the village and did the arithmetic the way she did every arithmetic, on the page and plain.

‘How long?’

‘From the fuse, thirty seconds to the first shot. After that it is not a thing that can be timed or stood about and watched. Do not stand in the street. Do not stand under the wall. Get to the stair and go, and take your man with you, because he is worth more than anything in that chest and I have told Crane so twice.’

Crane came along the wall with his hat down and his coat buttoned. He stopped in front of them and looked at Wren and then at Juno, and he was, for the first time since the basin, entirely cheerful.

‘You will both want to be up at the stair when it goes,’ he said. ‘The smoke will tell us the depth of the take. Miss Ashgrove, if you have copies in your coat, take them out. I want the original, in order, dated, and I will carry it down myself.’

‘I have told him,’ Juno said.

‘She has,’ said Crane. ‘Now take your copies out and go up, and I will see you on the shelf below the top landing. There is still time to be generous or to be answered, and I would rather do it at the stair than in the middle of a street I no longer own.’

Wren did not take her copies out. She turned and walked back to the house and woke the four porters and told them, in the plainest words she had, to put on every layer they owned and go up the stair now and not wait, and the first of them was on his feet before she finished the sentence.

It went at a quarter past five.

The sound was a single flat knock, like a mallet on a barrel, and then two full seconds in which the snow went on falling and a bird, which should not have been up there at all, lifted off the ridge pole of the shrine and went sideways along the bowl. Then the pause ended.

The floor of Hollowmere dropped. Wren, standing on the step of the house, felt it come up through her boots before she heard it, a long soft settling, as if the whole bowl had been resting on a shelf and the shelf had been taken away. The ice under the street, which was the roof of a village, went down in one wave from the shrine end to the stair end and broke open along the seam the ice had left the night before.

The wall came next. The west face of the shrine leaned, hung, and came down onto the shelf where Crane and his crates stood, and the snow it threw went up the bowl a hundred metres and came down on the roofs as a warm white rain.

Then, above all of it, from the rim, came the sound Wren had heard once before and would know the rest of her life. The deep flat crack of the hanging glacier letting go. Not a piece. Not a run of ice. The whole blue base of the wall above the bowl where the ice had leaned over nothing for a thousand years.

She was already running.

The street to the stair was forty metres and it took her a hundred. Snow to the knee, some of it broken into blocks where the shrine had come down, the ice opening under her feet in seams that were black and then not there. She did not look back. She counted her steps out loud, the way she had counted on the sixth flight with the hypoxia sitting on her chest, and she came to the top landing of the stair, which was a shelf of worked stone a metre wide with the drop below it going away into the snow, and there she turned and looked.

Hollowmere was gone. The bowl was a white moving field with three roof poles still showing and the open trench filled and shut. The wall above it had come down to the level of the rim and was still coming.

Juno was ten metres behind her on the landing. Behind Juno the four porters came up the street in a line.

Behind them, at the edge of the smoke and the falling snow, a man carried something down out of the shrine.

Crane had the chest. He had broken the frame out of the block and he was walking with it, both arms round the front of it, holding it against his chest like a man carrying a child out of a fire. It was a box of stone a metre long and he had his feet under him and he was coming.

He could not come. The shelf he was on was a tongue of ice that had already broken away at the back of his heels. Wren could see it and Juno could see it, and Juno began to shout at him with the flat certainty of a surveyor measuring a slope.

‘Crane. You are on an island. Drop it and step left. Drop it and step left now.’

Crane looked up at them. He looked down at the water coming up round his boots. He shifted the chest so that he could hold it with one arm, and stepped right.

The tongue went into the bowl. It went down slowly at first, the way a plate goes down in a sink when the water gets under it, and then it went down quickly, and the last Wren saw of Halder Crane was the top of a stone chest with a carved sun on it and two hands above it, going down together into the white.

‘Get to the stair,’ Wren said.

The stair in a storm is a different object than the stair in the sun. The treads were glazed. Every other flight was packed with snow that had to be scraped off with an axe before a boot would hold. The snow fell so thick that the face above them was not there, only the wall at their shoulder on the right and the drop on the left and the rope.

Vell was already at the first anchor, working. He had cut two V-threads into the ice at the top of the landing and threaded the line through them and clipped it. His hat was back on and his hood up and his right hand was doing what his left hand could no longer do.

‘One at a time,’ he said. ‘Bourdon first. Porters on the line. Miss Rell, you back up the first man. You have the numbers.’

Bourdon, who was the oldest of the four and had carried for Crane for eleven years, went over the edge and down the first pitch with the line through his belay and his boots chattering on the treads, and at the bottom, where the second landing turned, he stopped and unclipped and waved up.

Vell pulled the line back and cut it.

Wren, standing at the anchor with her gloved hands on the live rope, watched him do it and did not understand the first one, and understood the second one entirely.

‘How much line do we have?’

‘One length of sixty,’ Vell said. ‘Two pitches of thirty, or four of fifteen, and there are nine flights and a bad one under the ice. Whether it is sixty or thirty, the number of pitches I can belay is a number of bodies and not a number of metres.’

‘Then we do not have a rope to leave.’

‘I am not leaving it. I am cutting it. Whoever is last goes down on a sheepshank and a prayer, and I would rather say the prayer than be dragged off my stance by a man falling below me. Crane taught the mountain one thing this morning and it was that a rope with two loads on it is a rope with no loads on it.’

He had the next porter clipped in. He belayed him down, pulled the line, cut it at the anchor, and re-tied. Fifteen metres of line went down onto the face each time and stayed there, hanging above the drop. Wren watched it go, and did the arithmetic she had been doing since the moraine, and saw that it came out even, and exactly.

The third porter went. Then the fourth, and the fourth was the one who had been at the stove, and he was slow, and halfway down he stopped, and the line went tight and stayed tight, and Vell stood absolutely still at the anchor with his right hand closed on the rope and his left arm hooked through the anchor sling, and counted, out loud, in tens, until the man moved again.

‘What are you counting for?’ Wren said.

‘To know how long I have,’ he said. ‘Do not ask me again.’

They were on the fifth flight when the top of the face went. It was not the village wall this time. It was the rim above the stair, where the snowfield had been overhanging, and it came off in one piece and came down the line of the stair with a sound like a train in a tunnel, and the air it pushed in front of it took Wren off her stance and put her against the wall.

Vell got the last porter down and called up to the landing for Juno, and Juno clipped in and went down in fifteen seconds flat, without a word, correctly, and Vell pulled and cut and re-tied.

Then it was Wren.

The anchor was a block of worked stone at the turn of the fifth landing with a thread of ice through a crack in it. The line came off it and went down to the fourth landing, where Juno stood in the lee of the wall with the porters and the copies, and there were fifteen metres of line between them and no more, and Vell stood at the anchor with the last cut end in his hand.

‘You go now,’ he said.

‘You come down after me.’

‘I will come down after you,’ he said.

Wren looked at him. He was standing the way he had stood at the top of the wall above Camp Patience, and at the doorway of the house above the wall, and on the glass when they had first come into Hollowmere and he had looked down the uncovered street and said its name. Hood up. Right hand on the line. Left arm at his side. He was the last man who would ever be able to tell anyone what had happened on this face, and he had been telling her for nine days, and she had been listening for four.

‘Give me your pocket,’ she said. ‘The names.’

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he reached inside his coat with the right hand and brought out a folded packet of paper and a small tin and put them in her glove and closed her fingers over them with his own.

‘Nine,’ he said. ‘Six on the ice. One on the wall. One in the hollow. And me. My name goes in the paper and not on the stone, because I came down and they did not, and I will not have my name above theirs.’

‘Vell—’

‘Miss Rell has your copies,’ he said. ‘She has the figures and she has my account, because I gave it to her last night while you were asleep and she wrote it down in her own hand, and she will take it north, and a surveyor who signs her name to a thing will get it read. That is the whole of the plan and it has been the plan since the second night, and you did not need to know it in order to do your part, which was copying, and which you did very well. Now go, because I have one hand for this line and it is the hand I tie with, and there is a man at the landing below with a case under his coat that I would rather see in the north than on this mountain.’

Wren clipped in.

‘On the line,’ Vell said. ‘Feet flat, weight down. It is fifteen metres and the last two are on ice. Come off at the turn and do not look up.’

She went over the edge. The line was cold and thin in her right hand and the wall was at her shoulder and the snow fell so thick that she could not see Juno below her. She went down in the dark of her own breath with her feet flat against the treads, counting, as she had been taught, and she did not look up.

At the fourth landing Juno had her by the harness and had her off the line before her last foot found stone. Wren turned to look up.

The landing above was a white shelf in falling snow with no line on it, and above that was nothing at all, and there was no man standing at the anchor and no man anywhere, only the snow coming on out of the upper dark, turning over and over, and settling.

Juno pulled her arm.

‘The line is cut above,’ she said. ‘We have thirty metres and four pitches, and the porters are at the landing below. We go now or we do not go. You have the packet. Do not tell me what is in it, because I have no room in my case and no name of my own to put against it, and a thing I cannot sign I cannot carry. That is yours. Say it out loud one time when we are down and I will write it in the account, and then it is in two hands and not one, and two hands is what gets a name read.’

Wren put her hand inside her coat and closed it round the packet of names and the tin. Then she took the fifteen metres of cut line off the anchor at the fourth landing, Vell’s line, the last of it, still warm from his hand in a place where nothing stayed warm, and she coiled it over her shoulder and clipped it to her harness. She stood up and went down the stair, leading, calling each hold out loud as she went.
