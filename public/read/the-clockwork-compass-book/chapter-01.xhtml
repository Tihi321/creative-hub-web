The Inheritance

The barograph in the shop window had lost four millimetres since breakfast, and Wren Ashgrove had ruled the same meridian three times.

The fourth attempt was worse than the third. The paper had taken up the damp of the valley and swelled at the edges, and the ruling pen dragged where it should have run. She wiped the nib on the rag, refilled it from the bottle of lampblack, and held her breath the way Perrin had taught her — not from superstition, but because breath moved the elbow, and the elbow moved the line.

Lowmarch sat in terraces above the river, each street a bench cut into the old moraine, and the chart shop stood on the second bench with its window facing the road. Above the shop the benches climbed and narrowed: third terrace, fourth, the quarry, the scree, and then the Icemark, which was not a thing you looked at so much as a thing you looked away from when the cloud came down. Below the shop the benches ran to the water and the mill and the last bridge.

The town had a road, a post, a magistrate and a dealer in anything strange. That was the whole of its importance, and every mapmaker in the valley knew it.

Wren ruled the meridian at last, lifted the straightedge, and set it down again when the bell over the door rang twice.

The man in the doorway took off his hat before he took a step inside, which told her he was from out of town. His boots were town boots, black and thin-soled, and he wiped them on the mat with the care of a man who had been told to. Behind him a clerk waited in the street with a leather bag held in front of him like a parish register.

‘Miss Ashgrove.’ He inclined his head. ‘Fennick. I wrote to you a fortnight ago.’

‘You wrote to the shop,’ she said. ‘The shop is the same address.’

‘So it is.’ He had the flat, patient voice of a man who read wills aloud for a living. ‘I have the keys to the upper workroom, and the magistrate is satisfied that the effects may now be examined. Five weeks is the usual interval. I am sorry for the delay and sorrier for the occasion.’

Five weeks. She had counted them in charts. Perrin had gone up the valley in the third week of the closing season with two weeks of food and a bad hip, and the search party had come back with his rope and one crampon. The magistrate had written presumed on the paper, which was the closest a magistrate came to grief.

Wren signed where Fennick put his finger, and watched her own hand do it. The pen was his, not hers, and it was a poor pen.

She had been apprenticed to Perrin Ashgrove at twelve years old, and had kept his shop through the whole of the seven years since, which made her nineteen and made the shop hers on paper, in a way the sign over the door did not admit. The sign said P. ASHGROVE & APPRENTICE because a sign was a fact and she had no other name of her own to put on it. There were people on the lower terraces who assumed the shop had passed to a niece. There were people who knew better and said nothing, which was the same thing said more quietly.

‘Are you kin?’ Fennick asked, as he followed her up the stairs. He asked it without weight, the way he might ask the time.

‘No.’

‘Then the lease and the stock pass to you under the terms,’ he said. ‘The rest is a matter of what he left. There is a chart. There is a field book. There are letters held back for six months. And there is an instrument, which is noted separately, which I am told is of no great value and which he asked to be delivered to the shop rather than to the estate.’

The workroom had been shut for five weeks and smelled of it. Cold paper, cold dust, a little coal, a cup on the bench with something dried black in the bottom. Perrin’s coat still hung on the nail behind the door with the shoulders gone out of shape. The lamp had a scorched chimney. Nobody had come up here to take anything, because nobody had the key, and now the key was in Wren’s pocket and the pocket was against her leg.

She went to the plan chest first, because paper was the part she understood.

The chart filled the top drawer and half of the next one down. It was the Icemark, drawn in Perrin’s narrow draftsman’s hand at a scale of one to twenty-five thousand, and it was nowhere near finished. The valley mouth was inked and the lower glacier was inked and the long ridge above Kell’s Mirror was inked as far as a col she had never heard of. Past that the line thinned to pencil, then to dots, then to nothing at all. Somebody — Perrin — had ruled a neat rectangle in the empty quarter and written inside it, small and upright: No agreement. Redraw when able.

Wren put both hands flat on the paper. It was colder than the air.

In the margin of the sheet, in the same pencil, there were two lines that were not about terrain. The first stood alone in the white space where a scale bar would go if anyone ever finished the sheet. A survey is a promise. This one I cannot keep.

The second ran along the edge nearest the glacier, written smaller, as though he had had to squeeze it in between the border rule and the last of the hachures. The instrument was never meant to leave the family.

Wren read both lines twice. Then she took her hands off the paper, because she had left two damp prints on it, and because the second line made no more sense the second time than the first.

‘The field book,’ Fennick said, and set it on the bench.

It was a pocket book with a board cover and a band of elastic that had perished. The first forty pages were what field books always were: bearings, times, altitudes, the weather, the state of the snow. 0630. Clear. Wind NNW, light. Crevasse field as charted. Rope checked twice. Then the writing changed, because the range had changed.

Range not as charted. Melt line forty metres higher than the survey puts it. The stream in the second valley runs east of where I put it in ’09.

Cannot fix position. No horizon. Ice under the moraine, all the way down.

And on the last written page, one word, alone, underlined twice.

Icemark.

Wren turned the pages after it. They were blank. Perrin had filled the book from the front and left the back untouched, the way a careful man left himself room, and then had stopped anyway.

Fennick cleared his throat. ‘The instrument,’ he said.

It was in the back of the drawer, wrapped in a grey wool sock with a hole in the heel. That was Perrin all over: a thing could be old and valuable and it would still go in a sock. Inside the sock was a tin box, and inside the tin box was the compass.

It was brass, sixty millimetres across and thicker than a pocket watch, with a hinged lid and a scratched glass. The case was worn smooth at the edges where a hand had held it. The dial was silvered and the needle was blued steel, and it swung on a jewelled pivot with almost no friction at all — she could see it tremble when a wagon went past down on the road.

There were no letters on the dial. No N, no E, no cardinals, no ring of degrees. Only a scale of small punched dots, sixty of them, and inside that a second ring with nine.

Inside the lid, stamped in small capitals, was: P. ASHGROVE. And under that, in a shallower stamp, as if added later by a different hand with a different punch: and after.

Wren carried it to the bench and laid it on the meridian she had ruled that morning, which ran true north to the top of the sheet and true south to the bottom, and which she had drawn four times to make it exact.

The needle did not point north. It pointed at the wall.

She turned the case on the paper. The needle held its bearing. She picked the case up, walked to the window, and held it at arm’s length; the needle swung slowly, hunted, and settled again — at the west wall of the workroom, low down, near the floor.

‘It wants repairing,’ Fennick said. ‘I am told that is common with old instruments.’

Wren did not answer him. She set the compass back on the bench and stood over it with her hands on her hips, and thought about the word lost, which was a word she used in the shop every day and which in the shop meant a chart filed in the wrong drawer.

Then she asked it, out loud, feeling like a fool with a solicitor standing behind her.

‘Where is my folding rule?’

The needle lay still. It did not refuse the way a compass refuses a magnet held too close, snapping to a false pole. It simply stayed where it was, unimpressed, and Wren stood over it and understood that a single question got you nothing. Whatever this was, it answered in pairs.

She had lost the rule four winters ago on the lower terrace, carrying two rolled charts in a wind, and she had heard it go down between the paving stones and had never got it back. She had looked. She had spent an hour on her knees with a lamp. It was a good rule, brass-bound boxwood, thirty centimetres, six folds, and she had bought it with the first money she ever earned.

She leaned on the bench with both palms and asked the whole thing at once.

‘Where is my folding rule, and where is the thing Perrin was looking for when he died?’

The needle swung. It moved the way a needle moves when a magnet comes near it, hard and fast, and it stopped on the same low bearing at the west wall, and it stayed there.

Fennick said something behind her. Wren was already putting the compass into her coat pocket and going down the stairs.

The lower terrace paving was old and laid dry, without mortar, because the town had learned that mortar cracked in the first hard frost and lifted the stones the following spring. The gap she remembered was between the fourth and fifth stones from the drain, and she found it in the dark by counting rather than by looking.

She had brought the tyre lever from the shop, the flat one, the one for the press.

The stone lifted with a complaint of grit and came away in her hands. Under it was a bed of packed sand and small stones and a length of old wire. And under that, edge on, green-black with oxide, was the rule.

Wren knelt on the wet paving and worked it out with two fingers. Two of the six folds were seized. The brass edge had a dent in it the shape of a boot heel, and something had been at the boxwood — damp, or a rat, or four winters under a wet stone. It did not matter. She held it in her palm and it was exactly where it had fallen, four winters ago, and the compass had known. It had known because she had asked for two things at once, and the second was the true one, and the needle had answered the pair with one bearing, which meant it had answered by finding the thing she had lost and put the other question on the same hook as ballast.

She stood up and took the compass out and opened the lid.

The needle had left the gap. It was pointing north-east now, up over the roofs of the second terrace, over the quarry and the scree, at the black shoulder of the Icemark where there was no light and no detail and no road.

It held that bearing while she turned, and it held it while she closed the lid, and it went on holding it against the glass, which she could feel through the case like a knuckle pressing to get out.

Wren put the compass in her pocket, the rule in her other pocket, and went back up the stairs. On the way she thought about the second line in the margin, and about the fact that a man who writes never meant to leave the family on a chart is writing to somebody, and that the somebody was, by any reading she could find, her.

The man came at the shop door after nine, when the lamps on the lower terrace were out and the valley had gone quiet in the way it did before the first real snow.

He knocked twice and no more, and Wren opened the door with the lamp behind her so that the light fell on him and not on anything behind her. He was fifty or so, in a good wool coat that had been to a tailor and to no mountains, and his horse stood in the lane with its head down and its breath going up like a kettle.

‘Deller,’ he said. ‘I buy for an expedition outfitting in the north. I am told you have the effects of Perrin Ashgrove.’

‘I have the shop,’ she said.

‘You have the shop.’ He smiled, briefly, as if she had made a joke he intended to remember. ‘There would have been an instrument with the charts. A compass, brass cased. It would be of no use to a chart shop, and I am prepared to pay for it as though it were.’

He named a figure. It was more than the shop earned in a season, and she worked out in the moment it took him to say it that he had named it low, because a man who wants a thing does not open with his best number.

‘The effects were paper,’ Wren said. ‘The instruments went to Kessick on the lower terrace three weeks ago. Loose tools, the level, the two bars you can see in the window, all of it. Kessick sells on Tuesdays. You might catch him before he sends a cart down the valley.’

It came out level. It came out with the right small hesitation in the middle, the kind a person has when they are trying to be helpful and are not sure of the day of the week.

Deller looked at her for the length of one breath, and then at the window, where the two barometers were still sitting, plainly unsold.

‘Kessick,’ he said.

‘The lower terrace. Blue door. He opens at seven.’

‘Then I will try him.’ He put a card on the step — heavy paper, one line, no address, just C. Deller — and went down the lane to his horse. She heard him mount, and heard the horse go along the terrace stones, and heard both sounds stop about sixty metres on, at the corner where the road turned and a man could sit and watch the shop front without being seen.

Wren bolted the door. The bolt went home with a sound she had never heard it make.

She did not put the lamp out. She went back up to the workroom, in the cold, past the coat on the nail, and set the compass on the bench along the meridian she had ruled four times that morning. Then she took her hand away.

The needle swung north-east and stayed there, pressing against the glass.

She put two fingers on the brass rim to steady it and did not take them off, and the cold came up through the metal into her hand and along the bone, and she stood in the dark workroom with her fingers on it and did not move until the ache reached her wrist.
