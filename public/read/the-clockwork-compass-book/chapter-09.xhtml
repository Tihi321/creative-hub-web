Hollowmere

They came off the top of the stair at the end of the day and onto snow, and the snow was not snow.

It rang under Wren's boots. She stopped and put her weight down twice more, heel and toe, and each time the ground gave back a dull flat note with a seam in it. She crouched and scraped with her glove and found ice, and under the ice, a foot down and clear as a window, a thing that was the colour of slate and shaped like a roof.

She scraped along the ridge of it with her hand. Slates, laid one over the other, and the ice was so clear she could see the heads of the nails.

'Wren,' Vell said behind her, and his voice was wrong, and she stood up.

They had come up into a bowl. The lip of the stair opened onto a floor of glass-ice three hundred metres across, tilted a little away from them, and under the floor there was a village.

She could see the whole of it at once, the way she saw a town when she stood at the head of the map table. Roof ridges in rows, and between the rows, streets, and the streets were frozen channels twelve feet down with their walls still standing. A chimney rose under the ice with a bird's nest in the top of it. A cart stood in a yard with its shafts up and its wheels frozen in place. The snow had not got into the yard because the snow had long ago turned to ice and the ice had sealed the yard round.

Wren walked forward over the rooftops of a village.

At the near row one door had swung inward as the ice came and stayed that way. She knelt over it and looked down through a foot of green glass at a kitchen. A table. Two bowls on the table and a spoon in one of them, and the ice had gone over the top of the spoon in a smooth mound. A coat hung on the back of the door, and the door had frozen open with the coat on it, and the coat still had its buttons.

She took off her pack and set it down, because her hands had gone weak in a way that had nothing to do with the altitude.

It had come over them all at once, she thought. Not a flood. A flood would have moved the cart. This had arrived like wax on a table, quietly, from somewhere underneath and somewhere above, and it had risen over the village and closed on the ridge poles and stopped, and nobody had ever dug it out because there had been nobody left to dig.

She stood and looked at the far end of the bowl, where the ice shelved up into the wall, and the wall was white with no marks on it.

Vell had not moved off the top of the stair. He stood at the edge of the glass with his pack still on, his hands at his sides, and he was not looking at the houses. He was looking at a place at the left of the bowl where the street ran out from under the ice and into the open, forty metres of it uncovered, a trench of dry stone with its walls showing and dirt frozen hard under the stone. The ice had gone back from that place. As if it had changed its mind at the last house and left the next four out of its reach.

Wren walked over to him. Her boots rang.

'It is Hollowmere,' he said.

'You know it.'

'I have seen the roofs from the col above and I have never come down into it,' he said, 'because I came down the other side.' He adjusted the strap of his pack on the left shoulder, which he could not lift, and the strap did not move, and he left it.

'Vell.'

He walked past her onto the open ground, where the street came out from under the ice and stopped being a street and became a path, and he went up the path a hundred metres to the head of the bowl. There was a low wall, dry stone, chest high, with a gate in it. The gate stood open and there was a house behind the wall, the only house out of the ice, on the highest ground, with its door standing shut.

Vell opened the door and went in.

Wren waited outside the wall, and while she waited she took the compass out of its sock.

The needle did not move.

She stood there with the case in her palm and turned slowly on the spot, and the needle lay across the glass without a tremble, as flat and quiet as a needle in a closed drawer, and it had not been quiet since Lowmarch. It was not refusing the way it refused a single question. Refusing had a lean to it. This was a needle with nothing left to point at, and she turned the whole way round twice and it stayed dead.

Then it turned.

It came off its stop slowly, without swinging, the way a sundial moves, and went round in a full circle, unhurried, complete, and came back to where it had started and stopped. Wren stood in the yard of a house above a dead village and understood where she was standing.

Nine people, Vell had said on the snowfield, and then he had walked north.

She wrapped the compass and put it in the sock and put the sock inside her coat and went in.

It was dark in the house and the light came one-eyed through a window of ice. There was a hearth with a stone lintel. There was a long bench on three legs, and on the bench, in a row, nine small things that had been cut from the lid of a crate and shaped with a knife.

Vell sat on the floor with his back against the wall and his bad arm in his lap. He had taken off his hat. His head was bare and his hair was thin and grey, and Wren saw that he had a bald place at the back of it, which she had never seen, because he had never taken his hat off in front of her in nine days.

'Nine,' she said.

'Nine.' He did not look up. 'I built them out of the floor of the house and the lid of the case. The ground was forty centimetres of it and under that there was ice, so I built them on the ice and weighted them with stones out of the wall, and in nine years the ice has been kind and has not moved them.'

'In nine years.'

'It was worse for me to leave them unmarked than to mark them,' he said. 'That is the whole of my reasoning and I have had nine years to test it and it does not change.'

Wren sat down on the floor across from him with her back against the other wall, so that they were two people in a room with a bench between them and nine pieces of wood on the bench.

'Tell me.'

Vell looked at the window.

'We were a party of nine and I was second,' he said, 'and we came up the stair on the third day of the fifth month and down into this hollow because the range had moved and the pass we drew was here. We were four days in the hollow. We were on the ice when it came at us. It did not come like a wave. It came like a gate closing. It took six of us in one movement, and the three that were left were up on the ice shelf at the head of the bowl with me, and we got off it onto stone, and the stone was the wall of this house. And in the night the ice came again and it was quick, and the two of them were on the wrong side of the wall and I could not.'

He stopped.

Wren said nothing.

'I came down the stair alone,' he said. 'And in Lowmarch I told them the range had taken us in a storm below the mirror, and that I had gone for help and come back and found nothing, which is a lie in the shape of a true thing. A storm is a thing that does not stay to be checked. Nine families asked me and I gave them the storm.'

'Why not the true thing?'

'Because the true thing does not fit on a headstone,' Vell said. 'The true thing is that the ground moved under us while we stood on it and then moved again in the night. You cannot put that on a stone. It would not fit, and they would not believe it, and if they believed it they would want to come up here and see, and the coming up would kill some of them, and I would have two more names to carry.' He looked at the nine pieces of wood. 'So I gave them the storm and I came back up here seven times to build these, and each time I went down past the second stair and told nobody where I had been, and I have been the man in Lowmarch who guides and turns around.'

'You did not turn around,' Wren said.

'I turned around at the top of the stair,' he said. 'Nine years ago. Every year since, I have come up and done the wall and gone home, and I have never gone up past the bowl.' He put his hat back on. 'Until this month.'

They were still sitting on the floor when they heard boots on the stone.

The boots came up the path in a line and stopped outside the wall, and there was talk in the yard, and then a voice said, 'The door is open,' and Halder Crane came in through it with his hat in his hand, and behind him in the doorway stood Juno Rell with a lamp.

Crane stopped two steps into the room and looked at the nine pieces of wood on the bench, and he took that in, and he looked at Vell, and he looked at Wren, and he was a man to whom the shape of a room was never hidden for long.

'Mr Vell,' he said. 'This is your village, then.'

'It is not my village,' Vell said.

'It is your people's.' Crane set his hat down on the bench, beside the nine pieces, and did not turn it into a performance. 'I am sorry. It is not a phrase I use. I am sorry.'

Vell said nothing.

Wren got up. Her legs had gone stiff and she stood with one hand on the wall and looked past Crane at Juno in the doorway, and Juno looked back at her over the top of the lamp, and neither of them spoke, and Juno lifted the lamp a little, which is what a person does when they want a room to be seen properly.

Crane was out of the house before anyone else. By the time Wren had her pack on he was standing at the far side of the bowl in the light of a second lamp, at the shelving ice where the wall came down, tapping the face with the head of his axe, methodically, left to right, listening.

Twenty metres up the white face there was a shape under the ice. A doorway with a stone frame around it and a lintel across it, and above the lintel a fan of carving, a sun or a wheel cut into the stone, and the ice had gone over it and the ice was clear, and behind the ice there was dark.

'The shrine,' Juno said beside Wren's shoulder. 'It is a shrine. Four villages had them. The ledger of the village went in a chest in the shrine, and the chest was stone with a lid, and the lid was sealed.' She stopped. 'That is on the strength of one page of a Lowmarch library book and a carved sun. I would like to be corrected later if I am wrong.'

'How long has he been at that wall?'

'Four minutes.'

Crane tapped the wall to the right of the door and then to the left, and then went to his men and gave an order, and two porters came forward over the ice with a coil of line and an iron bar and began to scrape a shallow step in the face below the door.

'No,' Wren said.

She had crossed the bowl without deciding to. The ice rang under her boots the whole way, and when she got there Crane stood at the foot of the face with his hands behind his back, watching his men cut the step, and he turned and was glad to see her and did not pretend otherwise.

'Miss Ashgrove.'

'You cannot open that with a bar.'

'I could open it with a bar in an hour,' Crane said. 'The ice over the door is a hand thick at the top and thick as my wrist at the foot. The door behind it is stone and the stone will need a chisel, and the chisel will not go through in a night.' He was not boasting and he was not angling. He was telling her the arithmetic of the thing, which was the worst of him, because it meant he had thought it through and would do it anyway. 'So: I will cut the ice off the door tonight, and I will look through the door, and I will read the lid of the chest with my own eyes, and I will go down the stair in the morning and come back in the spring with a proper party and take the whole shrine out in one piece, on skids, with the record intact. That is the only way the survey means anything. Loose sheets in a cold bag mean nothing to anybody. I have said this at camp and I will say it here.'

'It is a grave,' Juno said.

He turned to her and there was a moment when he did not answer, and Wren saw that Juno had said the one thing in the world he could not put a number against, and that he knew it, and that he answered it anyway.

'It is a record,' Crane said. 'Mr Vell has put his people on the bench in his house and he keeps them there, and I will not touch his house or his bench or his cairns. The shrine is a written thing. Written things are for the living.'

He picked up his axe and went to the wall and started cutting the ice off the door.

Wren did not watch him. She went back across the bowl and in at the first open door she came to, the one that had frozen open with the coat on it, and got down on the ice above the kitchen and put her lamp on the glass and read what she could of the room, and then she went and found Vell.

He was in the open street, in the uncovered trench where the ice had gone back and left the walls standing, standing at the end wall with his lamp held low, and when she came up beside him she saw what was on the wall. Line after line of marks cut into the stone, small and close and even, a whole wall of them, and higher up the marks became drawings, and the drawings were of the village and of the mountain and of the two of them together.

'It is the same wall in every village,' Vell said. 'Every year they cut the ridge line where it stands. So that the dues and the buryings would know their own ground.'

Wren lifted the lamp higher.

The lowest lines were the oldest and they had been cut in a different hand, and they showed the ridge of the range above the bowl as a long smooth curve. The line above it, cut later, showed the same ridge with a notch in it. The line above that showed the notch opened wider. The line above that, drawn by a hand that was still careful and much younger, showed the whole ridge shifted so far west that the bowl in the drawing was not a bowl any more but a shelf.

Two hundred years of a wall. And in two hundred years the ridge above the village had walked down into the hollow one bay at a time until it came over the roofs.

'They knew,' she said.

'They knew,' Vell said. 'And they had no arithmetic for it. They thought the mountain was displeased.'

By the time the lamp fuel ran low, half the village was out of the ice and nobody had slept.

Crane's two porters had opened the door. The ice had come off the stone in sheets as thick as a book and gone down onto the shelf in shards, and the stone door stood in its frame with the sun carved above it and a slot in the lintel where a bar had gone, and the bar was on the inside and had broken when the ice came, and one of its pieces lay on the step. Crane had put his lamp through the gap he had cut at the hinge side and looked in, and had stood at the hole a long time with his lamp up and had not said anything at all, which for Crane Wren took to be the loudest thing he had ever done.

Then he called for her.

The shrine was a cell four metres by four with a stone chest along the far wall. The chest had a lid and the lid had been prised up, and under the lid the chest was not full of hides and dues as Juno had said it would be. It was full of paper.

Crane had the lamp in at the gap and Wren put her face to the ice and looked, and the chest under the lamplight was a churn of sheets, sheets rolled and sheets flat, some stacked in tied bundles and some loose and jammed against the sides, and every sheet in it was a drawing of the ridge.

Some were on hide and some on linen and some on paper, and the topmost loose sheet had been laid across the others, and along the bottom of it in a fine square hand somebody had written a row of figures and, at the end of the row, a small careful drawing of the mountain above the bowl with only one line down the middle of it.

Crane was on the shelf with his arm through the hole. He pulled the top sheet out and passed it down to Juno, and Juno laid it flat on the ice and put stones at the corners, and they all crouched round it.

Juno read it with her finger, moving left to right. 'Age of the ice on the roof of the third house, eleven spans. Notch of the south ridge, one and a half bays east of the marking stone. Dues this year, three hides and a half.'

'It fits the wall,' Wren said. 'The ridge in this drawing is where the second line from the bottom is on the wall.'

'It fits the wall,' Juno said. She went very still. 'Mr Crane. The chest is a stack. The top sheet is the current one.'

'Then get the chest out,' Crane said, and his voice was not level any more, and he was already looking for a way to widen the hole. 'Get the whole of it out, all of it, order and date, and we will have every ridge this village ever drew.'

'You cannot pull a stack of two hundred years of paper out through a hole in the ice in the dark.'

'Then we will take the chest.'

'It is a metre of stone.'

'Then we will take the door and the chest and the wall if we have to,' Crane said, 'and it will go down the stair on the sledge, and in the spring it will be in the capital in a room with a table in it, and every ridge the range has ever had will be on that table where a person can read it. That is the whole of what I want, Miss Ashgrove, and I want it for a reason and not for a prize, and I am telling you plainly so that you do not think I am a vandal tonight.'

Wren knelt on the ice with the sheet under her hands.

And then she understood the sheet.

She had read it the way you read a map. She had not read it the way she read a chart, which is a document with a date in a corner. The sheet had no date. It had an age of ice and a ridge position and a list of dues, and those three things together were a date, and there was a series of them, and the series was the point. This was not the survey of the village. It was the village's keeping of the range's changes, and there was a stack of them because the range could not be written down once. She had come up nine days of mountain to a stone chest in a dead village with a wall of cut lines standing outside in the open, and she had found the same thing Perrin had found and carved on a case lid and then died for.

'Crane,' she said. 'Put the lamp down.'

He put the lamp down.

She crawled closer to the sheet with the lamp beside it and read the figures at the bottom again, and then raised her head and looked up the bowl at the wall in the open street where the two hundred years of the ridge line were cut into the stone, the lowest line smooth, the topmost one shifted.

'The survey is not wrong,' she said.

Nobody answered.

'Perrin drew the range in his third season and drew it again in his fifth, and the two drawings do not agree, and the reason is not that he made a mistake in the third or in the fifth. The range is doing what the wall says it is doing. The ridge over this village walked down into the hollow over two hundred years and the ice came over the roofs, and the ice came from underneath the ground as much as off the mountain.' She put both hands flat on the sheet so that the edges curled up. 'It is not a document. It is a habit. It rounds its own corners. The survey is right. It is the range that is moving.'

Crane looked at her for a long moment.

'Then it is worth more than I said,' he said, 'and it goes down whole, in order, and it is dated by the ice in the roof of the third house, and every sheet in it in its own place in the stack. Which means we take it as it sits.'

He stood up and went back to the hole.

Wren worked on the ice shelf for the rest of the night and copied.

The first three sheets came out through the hole and she took them and laid them on the glass and copied them onto the clean leaves at the back of her field book, and by the fourth she had stopped copying what they said and started copying what they were. She drew each sheet as a small plan in a ruled box with its three figures in the corner and the age of the ice in the roof of the third house, and then the next, and the next, and she went through the chest at a sheet every twenty minutes, working backwards down the stack from the newest and skipping whole bundles that were the same in their first four figures as the bundles around them. She made a list of the bundled ones and what tied them and what age each front sheet carried, and when she had seven sheets copied and eleven bundles listed, Juno knelt down on the other side of the lamp with a pencil of her own and copied from Wren's copies, marking each one R for reference, and they did not speak and their hands moved at the same speed.

At the sixth hour Wren's copying started to run together. She caught a bundle and set it down and made herself read the figures aloud before she wrote them, and the words came out slower than she wanted and the wrong side of the sheet went bumpy, and she made a note in the margin of her own book that she was making mistakes, and that anything she wrote after the sixth hour should be checked against the original by another hand if that hand ever came.

Behind her in the bowl the two porters slept on the shelf against the wall. Vell stood in the doorway of the house above the street with his lamp, keeping the open street out of the dark, and the spray of the lamp went out through the door and hung in the cold air over the uncovered walls.

Crane worked at the hole.

At first light the ice let go.

It let go at the top of the face first, with a sound like a whole glasshouse taking a stone, and it came off in a panel four metres across and went down onto the shelf and broke there, and the panel took the top step of the porters' cut stair with it and spilled the water that had been under it into the street.

The street was cut in two. The trench that ran from the ice out past the wall opened into a new gap, and the gap went down, and under the gap there was a room they had not seen, with a winter's worth of black water standing in it, moving.

Vell walked down the path to the edge of it and looked over.

He took off his hat and held it in his two hands.

'That is the burying end,' he said. 'That is where you put your people in the ground when the ground is stone.'
