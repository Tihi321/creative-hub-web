Vell's Account

The water in the burying room rose for an hour and then stopped rising, and by the middle of the morning it stood level with the lip of the new opening and held there, black and still, with a skin of new ice already forming at its edges. Vell stood at the edge with his hat in his hands for a long time. Then he put the hat back on and walked away from it and did not look at it again.

Crane came down off the shelf at noon. He had cut the ice back from the shrine door to the frame on both sides, so that the door stood clear with its carved sun above it and its broken bar on the step, and he had stopped because his arms had stopped, not because the work had stopped being possible. He ate standing up and slept for two hours rolled in his coat against the wall of the house, and woke when the light went off the bowl, and went back to the shelf with a lamp.

The rest of them slept in shifts through the afternoon. Wren took the middle shift and woke Juno at four, and Juno went out to the shelf with her pencil and her own field book and a page of Wren's list of bundles, and began checking the front sheets of the tied bundles against Wren's figures, one bundle at a time, without being asked.

Wren slept again and woke into the dark with her mouth dry and her head thick, and knew before she counted that she was worse than she had been on the lake. She counted anyway. Twenty-nine breaths at rest, lying flat, against sixteen at the shop. She wrote it down. She drank half a bottle and ate the last of the biscuits she had kept back on the tongue and had never told Vell about, and the eating of them was the loneliest thing she did on the mountain.

At eight by her watch she got up and went along the street to the house above the wall.

Vell was on the bench outside the door with the lamp at his feet and a piece of the broken door bar across his knees. He had been whittling the end of it to a point with his knife, slow and even, one long curl of wood on the ground by his boot at a time. He did not stop when she came up.

‘You should be asleep,’ he said.

‘I slept.’

‘You counted your breath.’

‘Twenty-nine.’

He turned the bar over and went on with the point.

Wren sat down on the step below the bench with her back against the wall of the house, and put Perrin’s chart across her knees and did not open it. Above the bowl the sky was clear and hard and the stars stood in it at the size they only reach above four thousand metres, which she had not seen before and did not want to look at for long.

‘You said we crossed with Perrin,’ she said.

Vell kept his knife moving. ‘I did not say that.’

‘You said your team crossed years before, and this month you told me Crane funded Perrin’s last season and you did not argue with it, and on the snowfield you said my master walked a chain of cairns from the shoulder to the top, and you said it as a man who watched him do it.’ She looked at the point he was making. ‘How many summers?’

He set the knife down on the bench and turned his hand over and looked at the two fingers that were not there, at the second joint, and closed the hand.

‘Four,’ he said. ‘Not four summers in a row. Four crossings, spread over seven years, and on all four of them your master walked with us, and on the third of them we were nine, and on the fourth of them we were nine different people, and he was the same, and I was the same.’ He picked the knife back up. ‘I liked him. He did not talk in the mornings and he did not talk at the tops and he never once told me a number that was not measured.’

‘Tell me the fourth.’

‘You have the wrong voice for asking,’ Vell said. ‘You ask like a clerk reading out a rule.’

‘Tell me anyway.’

He put the knife down and the curl of wood down and set the bar on the bench, and he leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging, and he looked at the ground between his boots and told it.

‘We went up in the second month,’ he said. ‘Nine of us. Two ropes of four and a spare man on the third, which is not a proper rope and we knew it and did it anyway because the fifth man had come sick and we would not wait. Your master went with the upper rope. He carried his own chain and his own stakes and he drove a stake at every fourth cairn and wrote the numbers in the book he kept in the inside pocket, and he read them out to me at the halts because I was second and the second is the man who has to know the numbers.

We came up the tongue and crossed the mirror in weather, and we took the stair in two days, and we came into the hollow the way you came into it, over the top, in the evening, and it was quiet.

It was quiet for three days.

On the third night it moved. Not much. A hand’s breadth, if that, under the whole hollow, and the ice in the street ran a seam and the seam went under the houses and came up through the floors, and we were up and out of the tents in one movement and standing on the highest ground by the wall with everything we owned on the ice below us.

In the morning your master took his chain and went up the south side above the bowl to find out what had moved, because that is what he was for. He was gone six hours. He came back down with the numbers written and his hands cut from the rock and he sat on the wall and said to me, this ground is moving and it will move again. He said it the way he would say a reading. And I said to him, how do you know it will move again, and he said, because it has moved before, and there is a drawing cut in a wall in this village two hundred years old that says so.

We should have gone down that morning.

We did not go down that morning, because one of the four was sick with the altitude and one of them had cracked a crampon and the weather was sitting on the wall of the bowl like a hand on a table and the stair would be a sheet of water once the sun was on it. We said tomorrow. Tomorrow is a word that goes up with you and does not come back down.

On the fourth night it moved again.

It came from underneath and it came from above and it was one movement. The shelf at the head of the bowl came off and it went down the hollow in one piece, and the ice in the street went up over the first two rows of roofs and closed over the top of the third, and it did all of it in the time it takes to count to five. Six of the nine were on the ice. Two of them were on the wrong side of the wall in the dark and I had the lamp in my hand. I had got to the wall and the wall was there and they were not, and I did not have a rope on and there was nothing to throw that would have held them.

And when it stopped, the village was quiet again, and the roofs were smooth, and where the six had been there was a floor of grey ice and it was better finished than any floor I have ever laid.

Four of us were left on the stone. Your master and me and the lad and the man with the chest. And your master was standing at the wall looking at where the six had been and he had his book in his hand and he opened it and wrote in it, in the dark, with the pencil wet, and I said what are you writing, and he said, the crossing, because if I do not write it now it will not be true, and it will go down the mountain as a storm like everything else that happens up here.

He wrote for an hour. Then the four of us sat on the stone in the dark and did not talk. And at first light the man with the chest was dead.

He had been sitting against the wall with his chin on his coat the whole night and he did not speak once and we let him alone because we took it for a man keeping his own counsel. And in the morning he did not get up. It was the altitude and the cold and the night. It happened quietly, and it happened while we sat beside him.

Three of us came off the wall. And above the second moraine the lad’s hands went, and I took his pack and put it on the top of mine and I could not lift my left arm past level and I still do not know to this day whether the arm went in the collapse or in the days after. And your master and me carried him over the tongue for two days and he walked the last of it, and we came down into Lowmarch in the rain and we were three and the village had seen nine go up.’

He stopped.

Wren did not say anything.

‘Three,’ she said at last.

‘Three of the nine. Two walked into Lowmarch behind me with their boots round their necks and their families at the bridge. The six on the ice did not come down and the one on the wall did not come down and the authorities put nine names in the paper with a storm against them. My name was not in it. I was the man who had gone for help. And the man who lost his two toes at the mill is the lad I carried over the tongue, so if you are counting the survivors, count him and the other, and then count me, and that is all three of us and no more.’

‘And the compass,’ Wren said.

‘Your master’s.’ Vell rubbed the point of the bar with his thumb to test it. ‘He had it out of his coat on the second day, the second of the four crossings, on the col above the mirror, and he set it on a flat stone and sat beside it all one night and did not ask it anything and would not answer me when I asked him what it was. It swung all night, first one way and then the other, and by morning it had stopped, and he looked at where it had stopped and got up and walked to the lip of the col and stood looking down at the way he had come up, and then put it away.

On the fourth crossing he had it out every day. Every day. And on the day before the hollow moved he had it out on the wall of the bowl and it pointed into the middle of where we had the tents set, and he looked at it and looked at the tents and put it away and said nothing to any of us, and he was up all that night with his lamp lit.

The night it moved, he had it in his hand on the top of the wall. I saw it. He was holding it flat with the lamp over it and the needle was going round and round, and it went round and it did not find a stop while the ice was coming up the street. It only stopped after. And when it stopped it stopped on the wall we were standing behind, and on the ground under the wall, and on me, and on him, and on where the six had been, and it did not point once at the stair.’

He looked up at her for the first time.

‘It does not point at the way out,’ he said. ‘It never points at the way out. It points at what you have lost. And nine people wanted it to point at the stair, that night, and it would not, and your master took the drawing off his chain the next morning and drew the dotted line up the rock rib and did not draw the stair at all.’

Wren had the chart open on her knees before he had finished the sentence.

She turned past the tongue, past the bowl, past the line up the right-hand side of the headwall that became dotted and stopped where the sight stopped. She turned past the rock rib and the four stones on it, and she came to the empty quarter at the top of the sheet, the ruled box with the stair not in it, and beside the box, in the margin, in Perrin’s hand, the line she had read every day for nine days and had read the wrong way every time.

It was not an instruction. It was four words and a full stop.

Never meant to leave the family. P.A.

She turned the chart and held it so that Vell could read the note, and she put her thumb under the line and looked at him down the length of the lamp.

‘He wrote that in the workroom,’ she said. ‘In the month he died. He sent the chart down from Lowmarch with the solicitor’s office and he went back up, and it sat in a drawer for six months, and the compass was noted separately and delivered to the shop and not to the estate, and nobody would say why.’ She kept her thumb on the line. ‘Why.’

Vell read it. He read it twice, which was one more time than any of the numbers he had ever given her had needed.

‘Because he knew what it would do in a stranger’s hand,’ he said, ‘and he knew what it would do in yours. It finds what you have lost, and if a rich man holds it he will go and get whatever he has lost, and he will pay for it, and the paying will be done by people who never asked the question. Your master thought about it more than I did. I had nine years and found nothing to do with the thought. He had one season and he did something with it, and what he did was put it in your hand and write four words beside a box on a chart and go up the mountain without it.’

‘He carried it up,’ she said. ‘In the book. On the rib.’

‘He carried it up the mountain,’ Vell said, ‘and he left the drawing with the box empty, and he wrote his own name in the corner of it, and he made you the keeper of the whole of it. You are reading it as an instruction. He wrote it as an obituary. A man who has just worked out what a compass costs does not write the cost as a duty to his apprentice. He writes it as the last true thing he is going to say.’ He put the bar down. ‘A hoarded thing is not a truth, Wren Ashgrove. He did not hoard it. He sent it down. He sent it to you because you were the only one he could send it to who would read it and then do what he had not done, which was carry it back up in the open, where anybody could see it.’

‘And what is the thing he had not done?’

‘Showing it to somebody who would not believe it,’ Vell said. ‘That is the whole of the trade, at the top, in a season like this. You find a true thing and you take it home and you stand in a room and you say it out loud to a man with a desk and it is his to call a lie. Your master did not like that room. He liked the mountain, where the true thing and the man agree. That is why he kept going up.’

Crane had come up the path to the wall in the last of it and had stood at the gate with his hat in his hand and listened, and neither of them had heard his boots on the stone.

He came into the yard and set the hat down on the wall and stood with his hand on the gatepost and said nothing for a moment, and when he spoke it was to Vell and it was meant.

‘I heard the last of it,’ he said. ‘I will not say I am sorry again. You have had that.’

‘What do you want,’ Vell said.

‘A reading of the thing that was said.’ Crane came in and sat down on the end of the bench, unasked, and pulled his coat round him. ‘You have the chart and you have the note and you have the compass, and the three of them say the same thing, and I agree with every word of it, and I want to say the disagreeing part out loud, because we are going to be on the same stair tomorrow and I would rather be a man you have argued with than a man you have been polite to.’

‘Go on.’

Crane turned his head and looked at the chart on Wren’s knees.

‘It is true that the range is right and the map is right and both of them are moving,’ he said. ‘It is the finest thing I have ever heard on a mountain, and I will spend four years of my life proving it, and I am not ashamed of the wanting. But a truth in a dead village under a hundred feet of ice is not a truth. It is a rumour. There is one chest in the world with two hundred years of the range’s own opinion in it, and it is in a stone box behind a broken door two hundred metres from here, and if we go down and leave it, then in the spring the ice comes over it again and the door goes under, and there is no second shrine, and in fifty years there is a young woman with a compass standing on this same ice and finding nothing at all.

I am not taking it to sell. I am not taking it to beat you to it. I am taking it because a survey nobody can read is worth nothing, and a survey on a table in the capital, where a hundred men can read it and a hundred men can argue with it, is worth everything, and I will be the man who paid for the table and set the chairs and I do not care that there is a cost in that, because the cost of leaving it is the whole thing lost.’

‘It is a grave,’ Juno said, from the gate.

There was a silence.

‘The burying room is under it,’ she said. ‘The water broke into the burying room this morning. There are people in it. I counted the openings while you were asleep, Mr Crane, and there are eleven, and each one is a metre and a bit, and each one holds one. You will be taking eleven people out of the ground to bring a chest of paper into a room with a table in it, and the table will be upstairs in the capital and the people will be nowhere at all.’ She came in through the gate and stopped. ‘That is my objection. I have made it once tonight and I will not make it again.’

Crane looked at the ground between his boots for a moment, and then he raised his head and he did not shout, which was the worst of him all over again.

‘Then we take the chest and we re-inter them,’ he said. ‘On the shelf, above the waterline, with the stones named where we can and the rest numbered and recorded, and the record goes down with the survey. I have the labour here. I have the rope and the crates. It is a week’s work and a week of fuel and I will do it and I will pay for every hour of it, and that is not a bribe, Miss Rell, that is a plan.’

‘It is a good plan,’ Juno said. ‘I do not believe you will keep it.’

Wren went out of the yard before the rest of it, and got her field book, and came back.

She did the copying at the end of the bench with the lamp at her elbow and the chart folded away and Vell’s piece of whittled wood under the page for a rest. She copied it all: every sheet she had taken, seven whole and eleven bundles listed with their front figures, and she copied them in her own hand, twice, on the clean leaves of the last two gatherings of the book, and she ruled a box at the head of the first copy and wrote in it the figures that dated it, the age of the ice in the roof of the third house, and her own initials, and the word temporary.

It took her two hours and she was careful and she read every figure out loud before she wrote it down, because after the sixth hour of the night before she had made mistakes in the first set and she still did not know how many.

When both copies were done she closed the book and went out through the gate and along the street to the house above the door with the coat on it, and Juno was standing in it at the bench with her own lamp, checking the bundle figures for the third time, and Wren stood in the doorway with the field book under her arm.

‘If he does it with charges,’ Wren said, ‘a chest of paper in a stone box behind a metre of ice wall does not come down. It goes up.’

‘Yes,’ Juno said.

‘How does he do it?’

‘I have been the surveyor on two of his expeditions and the third was last year in the east range and I read his stores list, which is a thing he does not think I read.’ Juno put her pencil down and came and stood by the bench and folded her arms. ‘He has brought one hundred and sixty kilos of blasting powder in crates marked as fuel, and a man from the capital who sets it, and the man has been at the stove below the stair since the day we crossed the snowfield, keeping his powder warm and his hands dry. Mr Crane’s public plan is to come back in the spring with skids and a proper party. His actual plan is to put a charge into the ice at the corner of the shrine at first light on the third clear day, take the door and the shrine wall and the ledge under it down as one mass onto the shelf, re-cut the chest out of the block while it is still moving, and be off the stair before the face answers. It is a good plan. It will work. The block will come down as one thing and the paper inside it will take no harm.’

Wren looked at her.

‘You are telling me this.’

‘I am telling you this because I will not set the levels for it and I will not measure the drop and I will not stand at the foot of the face and call the fall,’ Juno said. ‘I am the surveyor. If I do it, it is a survey, and if it is a survey then it is true, and I will have made it true. That is the one thing tonight I am certain about. I have the numbers wrong, I accept that, I have had the same ridge wrong eleven times. But I know what I will not do.’

Wren stood a moment with the cold running in at the open door.

Then she took the field book out from under her arm and tore it in half down the spine, and gave Juno the second half, the copies, all of it, and Juno took it with both hands and understood it at once, because she had been marking R for reference on every page of the first set for two nights.

‘Where it will not be found,’ Wren said.

‘Inside left,’ Juno said. ‘Under the coat. Against the ribs.’

She put the book inside her coat and buttoned it to the collar and put her arms down and stood so that nothing showed at all, and Wren looked at her for a moment and then looked past her at the bench, where the nine pieces of wood stood in a row in the shape of the only record Vell had been able to make, and she understood where all of this was going and what was left that she could carry and what was not.

Outside, on the shelf, Crane had gone back to the ice with his lamp.

The store of powder stood in two crates by the wall of the house, and between the crates and the shelf lay two hundred metres of glass-ice with a burying room under it, and a lamp burned over the crates all night, and the man who kept it sat with his back to them and his hands in his armpits because he did not trust the cold.

Wren went out of the door and stood in the street, and looked at the two crates and the lamp and the man, and then at the burying room, which was a black gap in the street with a skin of new ice on it, moving. She did not go back inside to sleep. She had been on this mountain nine days, and she had learned the arithmetic of it the hard way: you do not lie down in a house and wait for the morning and call the waiting a decision.

She walked round the crates to the man at the stove.

He was young and his face was grey with the height and he was glad of the company and said so. He told her the powder was the worst of the cargo, that a man had to keep it warm or the damp got into it, that it was a hundred and sixty kilos and no use to anyone at the bottom of a valley, that the man from the capital was asleep in the big tent and would be up at four, that the four charges were drilled and wired already at the corner of the shrine and set for first light on the third clear day and that tomorrow was the second clear day, so it would be the day after tomorrow. He told her all of it because she asked, and because she was the only person who had spoken to him in a week, and she listened with her hands in her pockets and gave him nothing that was not a question.

Then she took the small folding knife off her belt and went to the tongue of the sledge that stood loaded and lashed against the shrine wall, and the sledge was the thing that would carry the chest down, and the powder was the thing that was going to open the ice in front of it, and both of those things were a plan that other people had made while the water came into the burying room under the street.

She cut the lashing on the powder crates. One cut on each, two cuts, clean and quick, and the line fell away.

Then she went back to the stove and stood beside the man with her hands held out to it as though she were cold, which she was, and she said that the lashing on his crates had come loose in the night and that he had better tie it again before morning or the powder would shift on the drag, and he said what, and got down on his knees to look, and the lamp was on the ground and his back was to the wall of the house.

Wren took the lamp.

Not the stove, and not the powder, and not one grain of it, because the man was on his knees beside the crates and the man had done nothing to her and the powder was the only thing that could take the chest out whole for a room with a table in it. She took the lamp, which was the one thing on the shelf that was hers to take, and she carried it into the burying room and stood at the edge of the black water with it held low, and looked down at the eleven openings in the flooded ground under the stone of the street, and counted them in the light, one to eleven, and put the number in the field book with the figures and the date and the words: openings, eleven, bearing east.

That was the thing she did about the powder: she did not wet it and she did not move it and she did not warn the man from the capital. She measured the burying room by lamplight and wrote the number down, so that whatever happened on the third clear day, the eleven would be in the record and not only in Juno’s count.

At the top of the street Vell was standing in the open doorway of the house above the wall with his lantern in his hand, watching her, and he had watched the whole of it, the crates and the lashing and the lamp and the burying room, and he had not come down to stop her and he had not come down to help. That was an answer of a kind. She walked up to the door and stood in front of him.

‘You know what is in the crates,’ she said.

‘I know what is in the crates,’ he said.

‘And the third clear day.’

‘And the third clear day.’

‘And you have said nothing to him.’

‘I have said nothing to him,’ Vell said. ‘He is fifty years old and he knows what the powder is for and he has heard what Miss Rell said about the burying room, and he has given us a plan with a table and chairs in it and a week of work and a page in the record. He is not a fool and he is not a villain. He is a man who wants a thing to be seen, and I have been a man who wanted a thing to be hidden, and between the two of us there is a mountain we have both agreed not to say anything to.’ He held the lantern a little higher so that the light of it lay across her face. ‘I have been up here nine years and I have kept my village a secret and put my nine on a bench in a house and gone down the stair every time and said nothing to anybody. You are not going to do that. You told a man from the capital what you found and you read his own note back home to him and you put the number of the openings in your book in ink and not in pencil. So you tell me, Wren Ashgrove, since we are both awake and neither of us can sleep: what are we going to do when it goes off, and what are we going to carry down?’

Wren stood in the doorway with the lamp at her feet and the field book against her ribs next to the compass in its sock, and she thought about the shrine and the two hundred years of the range’s own hand, and about the four words carved on the inside of a case lid under a metre of lake ice, and about a stone chest she could not pull out of a hole in the dark, and about a survey on a table in a room in the capital that would be read by a hundred men who would call it a lie or the finest thing they had ever seen, and about eleven people in the ground in a room she could not show anybody, and about the copy of a copy of a ridge, half of a book, inside the coat of a woman with a signature to put on it.

‘The chest is not the artifact,’ she said. ‘The field is. And the field is a higher field than this, above the snow, on the far side of the rim, and if anything on this mountain survives a man with a hundred and sixty kilos of powder, it will be that. We do not stop him. We do not fight him over a stone chest with a room of dead in it. You will get me up the snowfield and over the rim to the top, this morning, before the powder, and you will do it on the arm you have, and I will do the rest, and whatever Crane carries down the stair I will have the higher thing in my own book and in my own hand, in order, dated, with a signature under it, and his will be one drawer of mine and he will know it and the room with the table in it will know it too.’ She picked up the lamp. ‘And Juno Rell has half of my book and a cold road north and she will not wait for us. That is the whole of the plan and I have just made it standing in a doorway, so you may argue with it if you want and you will be the first to be told that you had nine years and did nothing with them.’

Vell looked at her for the length of four or five breaths.

‘You have the altitude in your head,’ he said. ‘You have had it since the lake. You will get up that field on your own feet or you will not get up at all, and I will be behind you the whole way and I cannot carry you and I will not pretend that I can.’

‘I know that,’ she said.

‘Then get your boots,’ Vell said, ‘because the snow above the rim makes its own weather in the mornings and it is already four and the second clear day starts in three hours, and a person who waits for the third clear day on this mountain is a person who has decided to stand under a falling wall and call it patience.’

She went in to get her boots. Behind her in the street the young man was down on his knees in the lamplight, re-tying the lashing on the powder crates with the careful double knots of a person who did not trust his own hands in the cold.
