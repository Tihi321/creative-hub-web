The Descent

The storm came in from the west at dusk, and Wren went down the stair through it one rope length at a time.

Snow packed the treads level and hid the worked corners, so she read the face with her boots and her hands. Every fourth or fifth flight carried a glaze of ice under the new snow, and on those she turned inward, hip to the rock, and moved one point at a time. Vell's cut rope lay where he had left it, lashed at each landing, thirty metres and no more. She untied each length as she came to it, coiled it over her shoulder, and carried it down to the next anchor, and the wind took the loose ends and snapped them like wet sheets. She did not look up. That lesson had cost her a scar on a helmet and cost Vell his shoulder, and she had paid attention at last.

She did not stop to say anything to the empty landing above the third pitch. She tied off, called down to Juno that the pitch was clear, and started the next one.

Halfway down, the face above them made a sound like a door pulled off a hinge, and Wren flattened against the rock with both palms flat and her helmet turned into the face, and the avalanche came down the flight to her left, where none of them were standing. Snow went past her with a rip of air and a smell of cut ice. She counted seventeen seconds before the flow stopped, and then she counted her limbs, the way Vell would have made her do. Both arms. Both legs. Rope in her left hand, wet through, still running down to Juno, who was still tied in. She called down. Juno called up. The porters, below the fourth landing, shouted up that they were all four accounted for, in a voice that shook.

They reached the fourth landing at black dark. Wren rigged the next pitch off anchors that had survived the flow, let Juno go first because her hands were steadier, and followed on a line she had not built. Trusting another person's anchor was a different skill from placing her own, and she noticed, on the third landing, that she had stopped checking Juno's work two pitches back. She had not decided to stop. She simply found that she was not checking, and she tied in and went on.

They came off the bottom of the stair at two in the morning, and nobody spoke for a quarter of an hour. Wren's left hand had gone numb again, which it had not done since the lake, and she warmed it inside her jacket against her ribs and used her right hand for the knot.

The weather broke in the night and the morning came still. They crossed to Kell's Mirror with the sun on the far rim and the lake a different lake. The crack they had opened from their own hole had bent across the whole surface, and the far end, which had been standing blocks, was open water with grey slabs floating in it, knocking together with a sound like a bell with no clapper in it, and Wren stopped counting the knocks when the fourth one came.

At the near shore, in the walled hollow where Crane's expedition had left two crates and a tarpaulin, six porters were camped.

Six. Two had gone with Crane onto the face above the stair, and one had been at the stove below the landing keeping the powder warm, and the arithmetic came out of the boy with the bandaged forearm in a voice that would not go above a mumble. The man from the stove had gone up when the charges fired and had not come down. That made six here and three on the mountain, of whom one was Crane and one was the man from the stove, and one they did not name at all, and the boy looked at the ground when he got to that part of it.

They fed her. There was barley and dried mutton and tea with sugar in it, and Wren ate slowly and kept it down, and wrote in her book that she had kept it down. Then she slept four hours under a tarpaulin with her boots on and the fire at her feet.

When she woke, Juno was sitting with the case open on her knees, a lamp, a straightedge, and a pencil worn down to the wood.

'Read this,' Juno said, and passed her a single page.

It was a copy of the shrine figures in Wren's own hand, and under it, in Juno's neat small letters, a date, her initials, and one sentence. Measured from the field by W. Ashgrove on the 12th and copied by J. Rell on the 13th. The error west of the third ridge is mine, not hers, and is entered, not tidied.

'That will not be enough for most people,' Wren said.

'It is enough for me,' Juno said. 'My initials will have to do.'

'You did not take the copies to Crane's sponsors.'

'His sponsors wanted a closed survey. A chest. A treasure. One name at the head of the column, and theirs on the front page.' Juno looked at the page in Wren's hand. 'There is a land office in the north that keeps range surveys for the Crown, and it has a registering clerk who dislikes me and files what he is given. I will take what I have and go. It will be found. Cartographers will write and say the second hand made it up. The second hand will answer the letters. That is how it is settled. Not this year. In ten years, with the paper still on the shelf.'

Wren put the page down and took out the oilskin packet.

'Nine names in Vell's hand,' she said. 'Nine people died in Hollowmere and the town has been told a storm. Their families are in Lowmarch and in the villages past the second stair. This is a list, not a survey. It is the one I can carry.'

'What do you want for it?'

'Your porters,' Wren said. 'All six, down and out at the valley mouth and onto the Lowmarch road. One of them has a bad arm and all of them are frightened of the ice. You have wages and a letter of authority. Pay them south. I will walk with them and cross them over the tongue. I cannot pay them with anything but paper, and paper is not what they want.'

Juno was quiet a moment. 'That is the same trade I made,' she said.

'Mine is not the better one,' Wren said. 'But mine is mine.'

They shook on it, and the fire burned down, and the lake knocked four times while they drank one cup of tea and did not say anything else.

They left at four in the morning, six porters and Wren and Juno, and Wren led down the west side of the lake and onto the tongue in the dark. The tongue had changed too. The summer's meltwater had cut new runnels in it, and the crevasse under the snow bridge at the eight-hundred-metre mark had widened, and they crossed it on Wren's line, one at a time, and the bridge held, and she counted them across and went over it last and did not look down.

They came onto the lower moraine on the second day, and there were boot prints in the dust going up, fresh, town soles, a man's weight.

Wren stood and looked at them for a long minute. She had brought the compass down the whole mountain and had not asked it a single question since the cairn field, and standing in the dust she found that she did not want to ask it one now. If Deller was going up, he would find the stair and the buried bowl and the black water where the shrine had been, and nothing else, because the thing he had been sent for was a practice and not a chest, and it had gone east down the long fall on the wind and north in a case under Juno Rell's coat. Let him walk. She stepped off the prints and went on.

They camped that night at the head of the moraine, and in the morning Wren took the packet out of her jacket and looked at it by the grey light, and then took the six porters aside, all six together, and told them plainly what she was going to do with it and asked them whether each name was the right name and the right village.

Three of the six had carried for Vell in earlier years. One of them, the oldest, who had been at the stove at Hollowmere on the night it moved and had come down the stair with ice in his hair, corrected a surname from the way she had it to the way the family had it, and then corrected one more, and then sat with his hands on his knees and looked away at the valley and said, in his own language and then in hers, that the town had believed the storm and that he had let it, and that he had been glad to let it.

'We will say it in the town,' Wren said. 'All six of us, together, at the post house and at the mill, and the names go on the register. You do not have to explain why you were quiet. I have been quiet about things myself.'

The old man thought about it for a while, and then he nodded, once.

The valley mouth was a stand of stunted pines and a place where the water boiled up out of the moraine and turned west, and a cart road, and Deller was waiting on the road with a folding stool, a leather satchel, and a horse that had been brushed within the last hour.

'Miss Ashgrove,' he said, and stood up. 'You have come down.'

'I have come down,' Wren said. 'The six porters behind me have come down. My colleague is going north on this road and you are not to follow her. What do you want?'

'The compass,' he said, 'as before.'

'Why?'

'Because it finds what is lost. My employers have been looking for a thing for eleven years, and the story says the instrument would find it in a month.'

'It would find it,' Wren said. 'It would find it and charge for the finding, and the charge would not be paid by you. It would be paid by whoever carries it. Ask the nearest bodies. That is the whole of what it does, and I am telling you that as a fact I have paid for.' She turned her jacket aside, enough to show the cord and the brass case. She did not touch it. 'You may have the other answer for nothing. There is no artifact at the top of the Icemark. There is a survey, made on a Thursday and out of date by Sunday, and made again, and again, and that is all it ever was. That is what I lied to you about in my shop, and I will not lie to you twice. The compass is not for sale.'

Deller looked at her for a long time, at the cord and then at the white range and then at the six porters coming down the track with their loads done up.

'I will write to them what you said,' he said.

'Write it in full,' Wren said. 'If your employers want the survey, they can write to the land office in the north and ask the clerk for a date.'

He folded the stool and mounted and went up the road north. Wren and the porters and Juno went west, and at the fork Juno stopped and took her hand, and the grip left the impression of a case under her coat, and she said she would write, and Wren said she would answer.

They walked into Lowmarch on the third day, in the middle of the afternoon, and Kessick was on the step of the chart shop on the lower terrace, with the tin in his window.

'It came down on the porters,' he said. 'I did not open it. That is a lie. I opened it. Nine names, and I have been round the town once.' He took his cap off and held it. 'It has gone round twice. They are talking at the post house and at the mill.'

'Then let them talk,' Wren said. 'I will be at the post house at six, and the six porters will be with me, and you will be there, and the register will be on the table.'

The post house at six was full. Ostler stood behind her counter with the register and the pen, and Kessick sat by the door, and Tibbs came up from the mill with his coat still on, and the six porters stood in a line along the wall, and Wren read the nine names out of the packet slowly, one at a time, with the village after each name, and said what had happened to each of them, and where, and on what night, and who had carried them down and who had not been able to.

When she finished, the room was quiet for a while. Then the old porter who had corrected the surnames said his piece, briefly, and sat down. Tibbs stood up and said that he had been the lad carried over the tongue and that his two toes were in a jar on Kessick's shelf and that he would like them counted among the nine, which nobody understood except Kessick, who understood it perfectly and wrote an extra line in the margin of the register that said the tin had come down from the top of the Icemark on the third day of the descent, attested by six.

Then the questions came, from four women and two men and a girl of about twelve, and Wren took out the field book and read them Vell's account, quietly, in the order the things had happened, and did not soften any of it, and when the girl of twelve asked whether the ice had been quick, Wren said yes, and the girl nodded and did not cry, because she had been waiting nine years for an answer that held still.

At the end of it, Wren went up the terraces to the shop and unwrapped the compass on the counter where Perrin had drawn charts for thirty years.

She had kept her promise to it. She had not asked it anything since the cairn field. She had carried it down the whole mountain inside her coat next to her ribs, and she had given Juno the copies, and she had given the town the names, and those were the two things that could be carried down, and now she stood in the cold workroom with the barograph on seven hundred and forty-one and the lamp lit, and asked it one question.

Where is the shop from here?

The needle lifted off the south-east, where it had lain since the second day of the crossing, and swung west and then west again, more slowly, and settled pointing at the window over the lower terrace and the street where the shop stood, and it lay there steady against the glass and did not move.

Wren looked at it for a while.

Then she got a fresh sheet out of the plan chest, which was empty of everything but paper now that the shrine and the field were gone, and squared it, and ruled a neat border, and in the top right where a survey carries its title she wrote in ink the day, the town, and the words: Sheet one. The Icemark, from the terraces to the rim. W. Ashgrove, apprentice, of this shop.

She drew the river and the three terraces and the moraine and the mill, and the road that ran up the valley and stopped being a road, and in the far corner of the sheet, where the valley turned and the range came down into it, she drew the tongue in pencil, and the first cairn above the rim, small, and put a dot for Kell's Mirror where it had been on the day she crossed it.

In the margin, at the foot of the sheet, she wrote:

This is the day's opinion. It will need redrawing. The stair is not on it. Neither is the mirror nor the bowl of Hollowmere. They are above, and they move, and the way to draw them is to go up again.

She set the compass on the corner of the sheet, where its weight could hold the paper down, and went out onto the step to tell Kessick the kettle was on.
