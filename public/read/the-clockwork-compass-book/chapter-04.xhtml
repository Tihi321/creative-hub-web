The First Pair

They roped up at the head of the black shoulder, and the check they did there was the one that mattered.

Vell took the rope off his shoulder and ran it through both hands, turning it a quarter at every pass, five passes end to end, the way he had done it at the gate in the dark. Then he tied in. Then she tied in, and he checked her knots with two fingers and a thumb — both tails, the figure eight through the harness, the buckles doubled back on the leg loops — and pushed the loose end of the rope down inside her pack so that nothing hung below her knees to catch on anything.

He put two fingers under her chin strap.

‘I know,’ she said.

‘Then you will not mind me doing it,’ he said, and did it.

Above the shoulder the rock stopped and the ice started. Wren paced the transition, because she wanted the number, and the number was eleven metres.

From below, the tongue had looked like a white road running up between black flanks. Up close it was grey. Rock flour was in it, ground fine off everything above and carried down inside the ice, and the grey took the light and kept it, so that the surface had the flat look of a floor that has been swept and not washed. Where the wind had scoured the ice clean it was white and hard, and only there did the front points of her crampons bite. On the grey they skated.

She learned the tongue the way she learned a coastline: by its creases. A crease running across the ice was a crevasse with a lid on it, and the lid was snow that had fallen in and been blown level and had frozen into a bridge, and the bridge would hold a light man and not a heavy one, and there was no way from above to tell which of the two she was. The dirt lay in long lines where the ice had been near the surface in a warm summer. The sags told her where the tongue went over a roll and stretched.

She read all of it and wrote half of it. The half she wrote went into the field book in pencil, at the edge of the sheet, in a hand she kept small on purpose.

They crossed on the line Perrin had drawn, and the line held. It agreed with the ground to within a pencil width for eight hundred metres. Then it stopped agreeing, and Wren stopped to fix it, and the fixing took her four minutes, and Vell stood with his back to the wind and did not tell her to hurry, which was its own kind of statement.

The tongue flattened at three thousand three hundred metres by the aneroid, which read five hundred and eight millimetres and a tenth. The shop window had read seven hundred and forty-one with the pointer down. The air here was thinner, and she could feel the arithmetic of it in her calves — not the thinness itself, which she could not feel, but the blunt fact that the same slope now cost more.

Vell sat down on the flat ice with his back against his pack. ‘Twenty minutes. Eat something you can eat with a glove on.’

The ringing in her left ear had gone while they were crossing, or she had stopped noticing it, which is not the same thing and she knew it. She sat beside him and ate a biscuit and drank a third of her bottle, which was warm from being carried against her ribs beside the compass. Then she got the compass out.

She did it in front of him because he had seen it once already and had asked nothing, and because hiding it from a man she was roped to was a lie with a rope in it.

It came out of the sock in its brass case, and she held it flat on her gloved palm and opened the lid.

‘I am going to ask it a question,’ she said.

‘You are going to ask it two,’ Vell said.

So he knew. He knew the shape of it and not only the fact of it. She looked at the needle lying down on the card, at rest, pointing at nothing, and she did not ask him how he knew, because she had learned how far that went.

She asked the first one anyway, on its own, out loud, to be sure, and to be able to write it down as a test rather than a belief.

‘Where is the old survey cairn on this tongue?’

The needle trembled on the card and lay still. She gave it ten seconds. Two men in a town had asked her what it did, and one of them had offered the price of a season’s paper for it, and neither had thought to ask it a single question and watch it refuse.

‘It will not answer one,’ she said.

‘No.’

‘Then it will answer two, and it will answer them with one bearing, because a needle has one bearing to give. Which means both answers are in the same place.’ She said it into the field book as she said it out loud, writing the sentence down in her small hand, because a conclusion you have written is a conclusion you have to stand behind. ‘Where does the old survey cairn stand on this tongue, and what was my master doing when he died?’

The needle woke up.

It did not drift, the way a compass drifts when a man walks past it with a knife in his pocket. It swung hard left, past south, past the moraine behind them, and stopped.

Wren held her hand flat and let it stop and did not move her hand, because moving her hand would move the bearing, and she wanted to know whether the bearing was real.

The needle pointed up and left of them, at a white slope eight hundred metres away, a steep fan of snow and old ice that came down out of the hanging glacier between two black bands of rock. Above it, the hanging ice stood out over its own base, blue at the crack, with the whole weight of it leaning forward into the light.

Vell looked at it for a long count. Then he got the glass out of his coat, an old single-barrelled one with a dent in the tube, and looked for a while longer.

‘There is a cairn,’ he said. ‘Three stones, maybe four, on the rock rib at the left of the fan. It has been there a long time. The lichen on it is the size of a plate, and the lichen is black, and black lichen up here is old.’

‘Perrin’s book draws it as a triangle with a dot in the middle. It is one of a chain. One on the shoulder, one on the tongue, and a chain of them all the way to the top. He was walking the chain, and the chain is a survey.’ She closed the compass and put it back in the sock and the sock back inside her coat, and the brass was warm again within four steps. ‘That is what he was doing. He was checking a stake he could not see from any other stake.’

‘Then he was standing at that cairn when he died,’ Vell said, ‘or he died walking to it. The compass has given you the same place for two questions, which is the only arithmetic it has.’

He stood up and put the glass away. That was the thing that stayed with her afterwards, when she went back over the day for the third and fourth time: he said nothing at all about whether they would go. He looked at the slope until he had finished looking, and then he put his pack on and turned up towards it.

They left the line Perrin had drawn and cut up across the rubble rib that ran along the foot of the rock band.

It cost them an hour and it was worth it, because the rib was solid and the ground either side of the rib was not. They moved together, thirty metres of rope between them, Vell in front, and Wren watched the rope and not the view, which was the skill and the whole of the skill.

The fan began where the rib ended. It came down at thirty-five degrees, a great grey tongue of old snow lying on ice, and the sun had been on it for an hour and the top of it was glazed. She could see the runnels in it where meltwater had cut channels in the summer and frozen after, and the channels were the line of least resistance for anything coming down. Below the fan the apron flattened onto the tongue, and on the apron, at the left, the rock rib came up out of the ice again with four stones standing on it.

The ticking started when they were halfway across the apron. It was the same small ticking she had heard on the black shoulder, the frost letting go of the rock, one click and then another and then a pause.

Vell stopped. He put his hand up, flat, without turning round.

She stopped and looked at his back and did not look up, because looking up was the rule, and because she had already paid once for that particular lesson and the ringing in her ear had not settled for three hours.

The ticking went on, and then it stopped, and then there was a sound she had never heard: a hard crack, like a gate slammed in a stone frame, so loud that it came through the soles of her boots as much as through the air.

‘Down,’ Vell said. Not loudly. ‘Right. The rib. Now.’

She went right and down across the slope, on the points, in short steps, her weight over her feet, and behind her the second sound came, which was the hiss, and the hiss was the whole face of the fan letting go at once and coming down it in a sheet, and inside the hiss there was a lower noise, a sound like a cart on cobbles, and that was the stone.

Vell had her by the strap of her pack and put her into the lee of the rock rib, into the small angle where the rib met the ice. He stood on the outside of her like a man standing on the wrong side of a door, and then the air came round the rib and hit them.

It was not wind. It had no direction in it. It was the cold breath of a great deal of ice arriving, full of ice dust and grit, and it took her helmet and shook it and filled the angle of the rib with white, and in the middle of it a piece of something struck Vell on the left shoulder and turned him off his feet and into the rock.

Wren held the rib with both hands and counted, because counting was all she had. Eight. Nine. Then the air went out of the corner the way water goes out of a tub, and there was only the sound of small stuff still arriving, and the same flat sunlight, and everything the same colour it had been before, except that the fan was white and where the fan had been grey it was now clean.

Vell was sitting up against the rock with his right hand on his left shoulder and his eyes open.

‘Name,’ she said. Her voice came out flat and high.

‘I am not concussed,’ he said. ‘Say the rule back to me about stopping.’

‘Stop somewhere that is not under you. You did not do that.’

‘I was not under myself. I was under the rock. There is a difference and you will learn it in about ten years.’ He took his hand off his shoulder and looked at his glove. No blood. He moved the arm forward, to the height of his chest, easily. Then he tried to lift it, and got it to the level of his shoulder and no further, and something in the joint clicked like a latch, and he stopped and put the arm down. ‘Rib, or the muscle over it. It will be better in a fortnight, which is four days after we are home.’

‘We are not going home in four days.’

‘No,’ he said.

The slide had crossed their old line and taken the top off the apron and gone over the step below, and where it had gone over the step, the ice had folded and cracked and the surface beneath was broken into blocks. In among the blocks the snow was churned to the depth of a man. It would take two hours to cross. Wren stood and looked at it and understood that the line they had walked in on was gone, and that they would be crossing the rubble of it, slowly, for the rest of the day.

Then she looked at her own pack, because a strap had pulled at her when the air hit them, and she had felt the pull and not the reason.

The food sack was gone.

It had been lashed under the top lid with two cords, because with eight days of food for two people there was more of it than there was room for inside. One cord was still there. It had been cut clean through, not untied, and the end of it lay flat and slightly frayed, the kind of edge a sharp thing leaves when it goes by fast. The other cord had gone with the sack, and so had the sack. Below the rib the broken ice went down fifty metres to a line of open crevasses, and a drawstring sack weighs two kilos and moves like a leaf in that kind of air.

Wren sat down on the ice and unpacked everything and laid it out in rows, because rows were a thing she could control. The stove. The pot. The spoon. Vell’s sack and the fuel. Her own pocket of emergency food and the biscuit tin. The rope. The screws. The slings.

Five days of the food had gone.

Three days of it was on Vell’s back.

‘Eight becomes three,’ she said.

‘Three and the fuel and the tin. Say the rest of the arithmetic, because you have already done it and I would rather hear the number out loud.’ He was still sitting against the rock. His left arm hung at his side now, and he had stopped moving it at all.

‘Two people, three days of proper food, moving at this pace on this ground, which is slower than we planned, on a tongue that gets steeper for two more kilometres. That is two days short if the weather is good. Five days short if it is not.’ She wrote the number in the book and underlined it twice, and her hand was steady and she was not. ‘The cairn is up there. Four hundred metres up, an hour and a half away, and we can see the stones from here, and the whole fan that just came down is underneath it, and there is more hanging above it. We cannot go up that way today or any day, and there is no other line to that rib that does not run under the hanging ice. The compass pointed at the one place on this tongue I cannot get to.’

‘Then it answered you.’

‘It answered me and it took five days of food and a shoulder on account. It did not take from itself. It took from the nearest two bodies.’ She said it level, and it came out level, and it sounded, when it was in the air, much worse than it had in her head.

They found a hollow behind the rock rib on the ice side, a pocket where the wind had scoured a bowl and the old snow was deep and dry. Vell cut a level bench into the slope with the shovel, one blade at a time, working with his right arm and his body and not with the left, and it took him forty minutes and he did not say one word about it.

They got into the bothy bag together with the shovel for a floor and their boots for a pillow, because two bodies in one bag is warmer than one body in one bag, and heat at three thousand three hundred metres is a tool and not a comfort. The floor of the bench settled under them and stopped settling. The ice creaked somewhere below like a door in a big house, twice, and then was quiet.

Wren lay with her hands inside her coat and her palms on the brass case, which was the one part of her that was warm. After a while she said, ‘How many years have you known what it does?’

‘Nine,’ Vell said, in the dark. ‘The first time I saw it was on the col, in the hands of a man who would not put it away, and I watched it swing all night, and I did not ask him a question about it, which is the same thing as asking and is how I know what it does now. The price is always paid by somebody. It is never paid by whoever asked. That is the whole of what I learned from it, and it cost me nothing, because it was paid by somebody else, and I have had nine years to think about that and have not found anything to do with the thought.’

‘He knew.’

‘He knew. He wrote it in his book and carried it up here anyway and asked it questions with the price written on the front page. He was a man who would take a pair of answers off anyone who would give him one, and he was the best surveyor this town has had, and those two facts are one fact and not two, and I am the last person alive entitled to complain about either.’ A pause. ‘There is one more thing about the price that you will want to write down in the morning. It was five days of food and my shoulder. The shoulder belongs to me and the food belonged to both of us. Ask yourself what it will charge you when it is your turn to be the nearest body. Then do not ask the compass anything else until you have an answer you can carry. Now sleep, because in the morning you are carrying the heavy pack, and it is not a punishment, it is because I have one arm that works and it is the arm I tie knots with, not the arm I belay with, and we will need both those jobs done properly for the next three days, and I would rather you were strong on Thursday than clever tonight.’

She had no answer to that, so she lay still and worked the count.

The morning was clear and colder by four degrees, and the glass had dropped three millimetres in the night, which is not a storm but is the edge of one.

Wren did the rope check herself, both hands, five passes, turning the rope a quarter at every pass. She did her own harness buckles and then Vell’s, because his left hand could not reach the buckle on the leg loop that sits behind the thigh. She tied both knots. She checked the chin strap of his helmet with two fingers, and he stood and let her do it, looking at the ice, and neither of them said a word about it.

Then she lifted his pack for him.

It was heavier than hers by four kilos, because the fuel was in it and the shovel and what was left of the food. She got it up onto his right knee from the ice, and then up onto his good shoulder in one movement with her knees and not her back, the way she had been taught to lift a press plate, and he put his right arm through and settled it with a shrug.

The left strap hung. She pulled it up over his bad shoulder, taking the weight of the pack in her right hand as she did it, so that the strap came up light and lay down light, and buckled it where the buckle had been.

He had not asked her to. He said, into the wind, ‘The day after tomorrow you will be better at that than I am,’ which was as close as he came all crossing to saying thank you. They put their crampons on and stepped onto the tongue.

The first ten metres were grey ice and her points did not bite, and then the wind-scoured white began and they crunched, and the rope came up off the ice between them and lay in a shallow curve on the snow.

Four steps up and two breaths out. Three days of food, two bodies, one working shoulder between them, and eight hundred metres of tongue to cross to a frozen mirror neither of them had seen. The needle lay still inside her coat, against her ribs and the bottle, and for the first time since the workroom she did not want to know which way it pointed, and she did not ask it anything, and she walked.
