The Guide's Terms

The barograph in the shop window had lost two millimetres overnight, and Wren was on the lower terrace before the mill wheel started turning.

Kessick opened his door at seven with a tray under one arm and a lamp still burning behind him, because his shop faced the river and took its light late. He was seventy, half deaf in the left ear, and he sorted other people's instruments into trays labelled in his own cramped hand: LEVELS, SPIRIT. BAROMETERS, GOOD. GLASS, CRACKED. THINGS WITH NO NAME.

He looked at Wren over the tray. 'The rule came back to you, then.'

She had the folding rule in her coat pocket. Word travelled down the terraces faster than water did.

'I need a favour,' she said, 'and I will pay for it in work. The strip behind your back wall, the one the tannery says is theirs. I will survey it. Two days with the chain and the level, a proper sheet, and a copy for the magistrate, and if the strip is yours the paper will say so in ink.'

Kessick put the tray down. 'And what do I do for that?'

'If a man named Deller comes asking after a brass compass in the Ashgrove effects, you bought the loose instruments from the estate three weeks ago, the level and the two bars and the lot, and you sold the compass on. You do not remember who to. You do not remember the price. It went down the valley in a cart.'

'Sold it to who?'

'Anybody.'

'Did I?'

'No.'

'Then it is a lie,' Kessick said, comfortably, the way another man might say it is raining. He reached across and tapped the back of her hand with one finger. 'You have gone red, girl. You should know that about yourself, if you are going to do this sort of thing.'

'I know it.'

'Then mind the lamp when you speak. A man who lies and looks at the mantelpiece is a man who lies badly.' He began to lift the trays onto the shelves. 'Ask me the other thing. You came down here for something else as well.'

Wren took a breath and said it plainly, because there was no way to dress it. 'I am going up the Icemark. I need a man who has been past the second moraine and come back with his fingers. Who?'

Kessick did not answer at once. He set a tray of glass on the shelf with both hands and turned it so that the cracks faced the wall, out of the window light.

'Tibbs,' he said. 'At the mill. He will not talk to you. And the woman at the post house, Ostler, she has been up twice, but never past the glacier nose. And then there is Vell.' He wiped his hands. 'Only, you will find that everyone ends at Vell. Ask anybody in this town who has been high and come home. They will give you a name, and the name will send you on, and at the end of it there is one old man on the top bench with a barrel of screws and a bad opinion of everybody. He has been up more than the rest of them put together. He also came down alone once, and did not say why, and this town has been chewing on that for eleven years.'

'Where does he live?'

'Top bench, past the third stair, with the quarry behind him. You will smell the rope.'

The mill was the loudest building in Lowmarch and Tibbs worked inside the noise the way another man worked inside a coat.

He was sixty, wide across the shoulders, and he fed the hopper with one hand and swept the meal with the other and had no intention of stopping either for a girl with a notebook. Wren stood where she could be seen and asked her question twice, and the second time he shouted an answer across the drum.

'Vell brought us down.'

'From where?'

'The high glacier. Three of us came down. Four went up from Lowmarch and one of them was the man who led it, and he did not come down at all, and I left two toes in Vell's yard because they had gone black and he took them off with a chisel and told me to stop looking at them.' He tipped a sack into the hopper. 'That is all the talking I do about it. Ask him. He is up the terraces. He does not lie but he does not answer either, which is near enough the same from where I stand.'

The post house was warmer and no more helpful. The woman behind the counter, Ostler, had a register open and a pen in her hand and eleven years of practice in not saying the thing.

'Vell took me up the tongue and back in three days,' she said. 'He is worth what he charges and he charges more than anyone. Whatever you have heard about his last crossing, it is not mine to tell, and I do not have it anyway. He came down. Nine went up. He came down. That is the whole of what the town knows and the whole of what he will ever say, so if you want the rest you had better want it from him in person.'

'Which house?'

'Past the third stair, where the road gives up and the quarry begins. He keeps a yard with a gate that sticks. Lift it, do not shove it, or you will have it off its hinge and he will make you put it back.'

The gate stuck. Wren lifted it and did not shove it.

Vell's yard was on the top bench with the quarry wall behind it, and it was the tidiest piece of ground she had ever stood on. Rope hung in coils on pegs, each coil the same size as the next. A sled stood against the wall with its runners greased, and next to it a bench, and on the bench a vice, and in the vice a length of steel rod that he was turning into a hook with a file.

He did not look up when she came in. He was older than Kessick and there was less of him. His hands were the hands of a man who had spent forty years putting them into cold places: flat, broad-knuckled, with two fingertips gone at the second joint on the left.

'Speak,' he said.

'My name is Wren Ashgrove. I keep the chart shop on the second bench. I am going up the Icemark and I need a guide who has been past the moraine and come back.'

The file went on. Shave, turn, shave. He held the rod up to the light, turned it, and took two more passes at a burr she could not see.

'Who sent you?'

'Kessick. And Tibbs. And Ostler, who told me not to shove your gate.'

'Then you have spoken to everyone in this town who has been high and come down, and you have come to the last name on the list, which is what people do.' He set the rod in the vice and wiped the file on his sleeve. 'How old are you?'

'Nineteen.'

'How many seasons on rock?'

'Two, in the quarry and the lower crags. I can lead a rope on easy ground and second on hard. I have never been above the snowline for more than a night.'

He looked at her then, a long flat look, and did the thing she had been expecting and dreading: he looked at her boots.

'Those are town boots.'

'I know what they are. I will have proper ones by Thursday.'

'You will have proper ones by Thursday or you will have nothing.' He took the rod out of the vice and put it on the bench and picked up a second rod, and for a while there was only the file again. 'Perrin Ashgrove was your master.'

'Yes.'

The file stopped. He set it down and put his thumb on the edge of the bench, and said nothing at all for the length of four or five breaths. In the pocket over her ribs, under its sock, the compass turned once against her and settled, and she felt it the way you feel a coin shift in a coat, and she kept her hands where they were.

'Why did you turn around?' she asked.

She had meant to lead up to it. She asked it because the silence was there and she wanted it filled.

Vell picked the file up again.

'The wind came up from the south-east on the fourth day,' he said. 'It came up hard and it brought wet snow with it and the snow sat on the slope above the couloir like porridge on a plate. That is why a party turns around and that is the whole of it. Have you boots that will take a crampon?'

The pause had been there before the answer. She had counted it: long enough for him to look at the bench, long enough for the file to go down and come up again. He had answered before she finished the question, which was the wrong order for a true answer, and then he had moved the conversation to her feet, which was the second thing a man did when he wanted a door shut.

'Not yet,' she said.

'Then get them. Stiff soled. They must take a strap crampon and the strap must be a strap, not a lace.' He turned back to the bench. 'Two hundred and forty crowns for the crossing. Half before we leave and half when we come back down.'

The shop made a hundred and forty in a good season. She had the estate money and the stock, and the number was the number.

'Agreed,' she said. 'Half on the day.'

'And one condition.' He said it without heat, the way he had said the price, as if it were a measurement. 'You carry your own weight. All of it. Your food, your fuel, your share of the rope, your own metal. We go light and we go fast, up in days and not weeks, which means no porters and no crates and no cook. Everything you need goes on your back. Everything you want stays here. If you cannot carry it from this yard to the quarry gate and back, then you cannot carry it up the valley, and I will know on the first morning and I will turn us both around, and you will still owe me the first half.'

'I understand.'

'They all understand. Then they get to the second bench and start leaving things in ditches.' He put the finished hook down beside the first one and took up a third rod. 'Thursday. Seven. Bring the boots and bring the chart.'

The workroom smelled of cold paper and lampblack, and Vell stood in the middle of it with the unfinished sheet spread on the bench and his ruined hands flat on the border.

He did not read the margin. He went straight to the drawing, and he went over it the way she went over a coastline, thumb and forefinger together, walking up the sheet: down the valley, up the tongue, out along the long ridge above Kell's Mirror, and then to the pencil that thinned into dots and stopped.

'Where did he draw this?'

'Most of it in the third week of the closing season. The lower part in the shop, from notes, in the winter before.'

Vell put his finger on the glacier tongue and moved it an inch sideways.

'This is right for the year it was drawn,' he said. 'It is not right for this one. The ice fall that was here is here now'—the finger moved—'and the stream he put in the second valley comes out where it likes, and the col above the mirror is twelve metres lower than the pencil says. He knew that. He wrote it in the book. But he drew the old shape because the old shape was the survey he had.'

'A chart is a record of what was measured on the day,' Wren said. 'That is what a chart is.'

'Then you have a chart of a day that has gone.' He took his hands off the sheet. 'The range has moved since he drew it. That is not a fault in the drawing. You will want to sit with that on the way up, because you will not have time to sit with it at the top.'

She looked at the empty quarter, at the neat rectangle with four words in it.

'There is no stair on here,' she said.

'No.'

'Perrin wrote that there was no agreement about it and to redraw when able.'

'Then that is the only finished sentence on the sheet,' Vell said. He pulled on his coat and went, and the door shut behind him and the lamp leaned in the draught, and Wren stood in the cold room and looked at the place where a stairway should have been, and where there was only a ruled box and a promise.

She laid the chart on the floor of the workshop and put the kit on it in rows, the way Perrin had taught her to lay out a drawing set before a job. Two ice tools, the long one and the short one, with the adze on the long one freshly filed. Crampons, ten points, with the straps oiled and threaded and the whole thing fitted and refitted to the new boots until the front points stood level with the welt. Ten ice screws, which she tested by running the thread under her thumb; two of Kessick's bit less than the others and went back into the tin. A helmet, secondhand, with one small crack she could see and no other. Six webbing slings, shock-absorbing. Karabiners, steel, nine of them, and a spare gate in a paper twist. The harness, which was the only truly new thing in the shop: light, with detachable leg loops, cut for winter layers. She put it on over her trousers and her down jacket and stood in front of the shop mirror in the dark, and tightened the waist belt and shortened both leg loops by two holes and rethreaded them, and hung her whole weight on the newel post with both hands and let her feet off the floor before she would trust it. Then she loaded the pack, and to make the weight honest she put in two flat irons from under the press and the bag of shot she used for holding down curling paper, and hauled the whole thing onto her back in the hall and stood up under it.

The spring balance in the doorway said twenty-two kilos. Vell had said nothing about twenty-two kilos. He had said the quarry gate and back.

The barograph had lost six millimetres by then. She took the lamp off the hook and put it out, and shut the door behind her, and went up the terraces in the dark with the pack on.

Bench two, past the shop. Bench three, past the stair, where the road went from paving to cobble to broken stone. Her shoulders began to burn at the third arch, and by the fourth bench she was counting her steps against her breaths, four and one, four and one, the way you count a pulse. At the top of the second stair she passed the corner where the road turned, and Deller's horse was standing there in the shadow of the wall with its head down and no light on it, and she kept her eyes on the stones and went on up.

The quarry gate. She put one hand on the cold iron and turned and came down.

At the shop door she shrugged the pack off onto the step, and it landed with a sound like a dropped anvil, and she put both hands on her knees and stood in the dark under the window until her breathing came back to a rate she could count.
