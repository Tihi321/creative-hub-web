The Witness

They came off the stair at the fourth landing and went down the last four pitches in the dark. Wren sent the porters first. Juno went second, because Juno carried the copies inside her coat and had a surveyor's signature to put under them. Wren went last, because there was no one left to send.

The storm went on all night. They cut a shelf into the slope under the lee of the bottom flight and lay on their packs while the snow built a roof over them. Wren did not sleep. She did not think about the landing above, because there was a way of thinking about it that stopped the hands from working in the morning, and she needed her hands. She thought about the arithmetic instead. Thirty metres of line, four pitches, five people, one cracked boot sole. It came out the same both times, so she let it go.

At first light the snow stopped as suddenly as it had begun, the way it does above the weather, and the bowl lay below them in the new white with Hollowmere not in it. The street was a smooth field. Three roof poles stood out of the snow at an angle, and one of them was the ridge pole of the house above the wall, and that was all that was left of the village, and it was enough to find the place by.

They went down through the debris fan and across the bowl. The snow was two feet deep and loose, and they sank to the knee the whole way, and four hundred metres took them two hours, and Wren counted every one of them. Where the shelf had stood at the shrine end there was a new wall of broken ice sixty metres high, black and blue and white in layers, still settling, and small pieces came off it and ran down and stopped.

Juno stood at the foot of it with her field case open.

‘I cannot measure this,’ she said. ‘There is nothing left on this side of the hollow to measure it against, and the ice is not done moving, and a number I set down today will be a different number tomorrow. I will not write a lie with good intentions.’

She wrote nothing. She closed the case, put the strap over her shoulder, and they crossed to the far rise, which was the way they had come in and was now the way out.

The four porters took the ramp down to Kell’s Mirror in pairs, roped, Bourdon leading. They were good on the ice, better than Wren had been on her first day, and they went down the two hundred and eighty metres in an hour and waited for her at the bottom. Juno went with them. Wren came last, coiling the line and undoing the anchors behind her, and the final anchor was a nut in the crack at the top of the ramp where Vell had placed it on the way up. She took it out with the nut tool and put it in the tin with the names, because there was no reason to leave it and one good reason to keep it.

At the mirror the lake had changed. The crack that had begun at their own hole now bent across the whole white surface, and the far end under the hanging glacier was broken into blocks and open water in places, and the glacier had let go in the storm and left a fan of debris across the last hundred metres of the crossing.

Bourdon went out with an axe and probed fifty metres and came back.

‘Not the way we came,’ he said. ‘The near end is sound and the far end is not. We go by the left shore, under the rock, where the ice is thin and the bottom is close, and we rope up and go one at a time and don’t stop.’

Three hours for six hundred metres. The ice creaked and boomed under them the whole way, and twice Wren felt the surface flex beneath her boots, and each time she set her weight back on her heels and went on. The hanging glacier leaned out over nothing above the far end, two hundred metres of blue, and the sound in the crack at its base was like a mill wheel turning very slowly, and none of them looked at it.

At the near shore, on the flat rock where Wren had asked the compass two questions on the way up, Crane’s expedition had left a depot. Two crates of food, a bag of fuel, a coil of line, and a note in a soft pencil weighted under a stone, offering the food to any party that would leave the note in place. Wren looked at the writing and remembered wondering about the pencil grade on the moraine, and there was no longer anyone to ask, and it did not matter any more, and she put the note back under its stone.

They ate at the depot, and it was the first time in two days. The food was good and there was enough of it, and Wren ate slowly and kept it down, and her body did what bodies do when the air thickens and gave her some of her oxygen back, and her left hand warmed and she could feel it again for the first time since the lake. Her rest count was twenty-two. She counted it twice, wrote it in the margin of the last page she had left, and shut the book.

Bourdon sat down beside her and pulled off his boots.

‘Where does the young mistress go from here?’ he said.

‘Up,’ said Wren.

He looked at the rim.

‘It is another four hundred metres and it is bare ice and there is nobody to climb it with you. The tall one with the case says you have been above four thousand for eight days, and your blood has gone thick with it, and you will not know when it happens. You will decide a thing twice and remember it once. She said that with respect.’

‘She has the numbers right.’

‘Then take one of us. I have carried for eleven years. I am slow now, but I am slow the whole way and I do not stop.’

‘No,’ said Wren. ‘You four have an appointment with the valley. Down the tongue and down the moraine and into Lowmarch, and you tell Kessick at the chart shop on the lower terrace what happened, and you give him the tin, and he will know what to do with it. I did not make that plan in order to have it argued with.’

Bourdon looked at her for a moment.

‘And the mistress with the case?’

‘She has her own road,’ Wren said. ‘It goes the other way, and it goes sooner.’

She left at first light and climbed alone. The way up was the way they had come down, but without the line and without the tracks, because the storm had taken both. She cut her own steps for the first hour until her calves burned, then found her pace and did not stop again for four hours.

The wall above the bowl had come down in the collapse and left a new line, a stripe of grey rock where the ice had been stripped away, and she took the stripe because it was the only way up with any rock in it at all. She climbed it in the soft shoes with the ice boots on her pack, and changed back where the rock ended and the ice began. Twice she placed a screw and rested, and once she took a screw out and moved up and put it back, and the thought came to her slowly and she recognised it for the slow thought Juno had warned her about, and she stopped and drank and sat down with her back to the slope for five minutes and let it finish arriving, and what arrived was that she should go back down, and she got up and did not.

Above the stripe the slope lay back and became the snowfield, and the snowfield ran north in the long shallow waves she remembered from the other side, and she went up it in the flat new light with her shadow going on ahead of her, and the rim came towards her for a long time and then suddenly did not, and she was on it.

She stood on the rim of the bowl and looked back. The white field with the three poles in it. The black bent line of the lake. Her own track coming up the face, a line of small holes going down the mountain and stopping. That was all the mountain showed of the nine days, and it would be gone by afternoon.

She turned and went on.

Above the rim there was no feature at all. The slope ran up in the same white wave and the wind came across it from the west and cut the surface into long runnels lying north and south, and she walked in one of the runnels and it was hard enough to carry her. She was above the last trough now, high enough that the horizon had begun to curve, and the air was so clear that she could see the roofs of Lowmarch as a grey smear and the terraces below them as three thin lines and the river as a pale thread that did not move.

Her count was thirty. She counted it, because numbers behaved the way she wanted them to.

The compass lay against her ribs inside her coat and had been quiet since the top of the stair, and she did not take it out.

She walked two more hours in the runnel. Twice she stopped and drank and ate nothing, because the food was at the bottom of the pack and getting at it meant taking her gloves off, and each time she put the gloves back on and went on. The sun came over the western shoulder and stood flat on the snow, warm and without any heat in it, and the runnels stopped running north and south and began to curve round and come back on themselves, and the slope lay back, and then it was flat, and she walked out onto the top of it.

No peak. No crest, no last step up. A field of snow lying nearly level for two hundred metres, with the wind going across it in long clean swaths. The swaths had been cut through by a great many straight lines, some recent and some not, and each line ended in a pile of stones.

A cairn field. Perhaps two hundred of them, low and flat-topped, four stones and a cap, built of the local grey rock in the local way, standing in lines from the western edge to the eastern edge in rows about twenty metres apart. The lines were not quite parallel and did not need to be, because every cairn in the field was its own opinion, and no two of the opinions had ever been placed in the same spot twice.

Wren stood at the western edge and looked, and then she took off her pack and sat on it, because she was at the top of the world and there was nothing above it but weather, and she was going to need both hands.

She opened the first cairn. Four stones with a flat cap, and the lichen on the cap was the small tight grey sort that takes decades, and under the cap a slot was cut between the stones, and in the slot lay a folded strip of something that was neither paper nor hide nor fabric but had been made from all three.

She worked it out with her fingernails on the snow, because her gloves would not go into the slot and it was too cold for bare skin for long. It opened to the width of her palm and did not tear. Inside was a survey of the range, hand-drawn, with a scale and a north line and a date, and the date was one hundred and eighty-nine years old, and the hand was not one she knew.

She put it flat on the snow with a glove on each corner and looked at it.

It showed the range from above. The tongue, and Kell’s Mirror, and the rock rib and the fan, and the stair on the west face of the cut cliff, exactly where she had climbed it.

The next cairn in the line was newer. The slot was cut the same way and the sheet was folded to the same width and the scale and the north line were the same, and on it the tongue was two hundred metres short of where the first had drawn it, and the stair sat forty feet higher on the face, and the mirror was longer at the near end. One hundred and thirty years old.

A third. The stair was not on the west face at all. It was on the east, and Kell’s Mirror had moved eleven metres, and the fan had grown, and the date was ninety-one years old, and there was a small square signature in the corner.

A fourth. No stair. In its place a ruled box, drawn with care, and inside the box, in a hand she knew, four words: not on this crossing.

She sat down on the snow then, which she had not intended to do, and put her hands under her arms, and looked at the field of cairns running east across the top of the world in their long shallow lines, two hundred of them, three hundred, each with a sheet in it, each sheet a survey.

The artifact was not in the shrine at Hollowmere.

It had never been in the shrine at Hollowmere. The chest in that cell had been one drawer of it, two hundred years of ridge drawings kept in one place, and one ridge is only one ridge. The real thing was this field. It was cairns and sheets going back past the oldest date she had found, every one correct when it was made and every one wrong now, and the reason for that was the reason Perrin Ashgrove had been walking the chain.

The Icemark was not being mapped wrongly. It was moving. Kell’s Mirror had changed its shape in a hundred and thirty years and the stair had walked forty feet in the same time, off the west face and back onto it and off again, and the range was doing what deep ice does when it lies on a floor that is not flat: it was smoothing its own features, slowly, the way a family eats the edge of a table over two generations.

The artifact was the practice. The field, the cairns, the length of time they stood for, and the truth that the Icemark could not be mapped once. Two hundred dated sheets were proof that the range had shifted under every surveyor who had ever stood on it, and that her master had been the one who worked it out, and had died on the range carrying the proof down.

She sat with the sheets and looked down over the western edge. There lay the whole Icemark: the glacier and the tongue, the long ridge above the mirror, the cut cliff, the bowl where nine people and one more lay in the ice and the snow was closing over them. She could trace all of it on the white. It did not answer the question she had brought up here, and she had been carrying the wrong question for nine days.

She took off her gloves, blew into her hands, put them back on, and stood up.

The highest cairn stood at the eastern edge of the field, on the last line, where the snow ran off the top into the long east fall of the mountain. It was larger than the others, a metre high, flat stones in courses, with a cap wide enough to sit on, and it had been rebuilt more than once, by three sets of hands, all of them knowing what they were doing.

She got the cap up at the end by putting both hands under the lip and lifting it back. The slot below was cut in the shape of a spine, a narrow shallow trough fifteen centimetres wide, and in the trough lay a bundle: not a folded strip but sheets rolled together and tied, wrapped, and wrapped again.

Oiled cloth, and inside that waxed paper, and inside that a survey, and when she had it open flat on the snow it was the biggest sheet she had ever seen, two arms wide, drawn on a single piece of something made to be drawn on and not made here.

The range, the whole of it, from the terraces of Lowmarch to the far rim, on one sheet. She found the tongue and the mirror and the rib and the fan, and the stair stood on the east face forty feet up, and the bowl of Hollowmere was a bowl and not a village, and the western edge of the sheet was the edge of the paper and nothing else.

At the top right, where a survey carries its title, there was a date and a name. Nine years old, worked out in the cold and exact. Perrin Ashgrove.

At the bottom left, in the corner where a surveyor leaves his working notes, in pencil and small: this is today’s.

She looked at those four words for a long time. Third sheet in the same hand that day, after the margins of her own chart and the lid of a case under nine metres of lake ice, and she had been reading the man’s writing for nine days to find a man and had found a method instead. He had drawn the whole range, dated the drawing, set it above every other sheet of the range anyone had ever drawn, and written four words under it to say what it was worth.

She took off her gloves and put them under her arm, laid her own paper on the snow beside the original, weighted both with stones, and got out her pencil.

Her hands would not work. She held them against her ribs under her arms and counted to a hundred twice and got them going, and then she started at the eastern edge, which was the top, and worked west and down.

She could not copy all of it. Not enough paper, not enough hands, not enough light. She copied the lines she could reach, ridge by ridge, shortening by eye and trusting the eye less with every line, and she drew the tongue and the mirror and the rib and the fan and the stair, and the stair stood on the east face forty feet up, and she drew it there. At the western edge of the cut cliff, in the place where the stair had stood on every other sheet in the field, she wrote four words.

It was slow and it was cold, and her count climbed to thirty-four and she stopped counting it, because it had stopped being useful. She drew for an hour and a half. At the end of it her copy held about a third of the original, close in the figures, nearly true in the scale, and already wrong.

Wrong because the man beside the original had written this is today’s nine years ago, and nine years is a long time. She could see one place where she herself had drawn the rock rib eleven metres south on the way up, and the original had it north, and there was no knowing which of them the mountain agreed with this morning. The truth on that sheet had been correct when it was drawn and had been going quietly out of date ever since, the way the tongue had gone and the stair had gone, under a river of ice the width of a country, so slowly that no one had noticed except a cartographer with an instrument that answered questions about loss.

She finished the third ridge, and looked at her copy and at the original, and her sheet had the whole shape of the range on it, and none of it was exactly true, and all of it was what she had seen.

She stood up, slowly, because she was at the limit of what her body would give her and she knew it, and she had known it since the thought had come to her on the ice and she had climbed on anyway.

She looked at the original on the snow. The last and best sheet, the whole range on one paper, the summit opinion, drawn with the finest pencil work she had ever had under her hands, each ridge a run of short reliable strokes, the western edge of the paper the edge of the paper and nothing else.

A hoarded truth saves no one.

She was not sure where she had heard it. It might have been Vell on the second night. It might have been a margin in her own chart. It did not matter who had said it. It was arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out even, the way it had come out on the stair when Vell cut the line behind every man and made the line hold by making it shorter.

She picked the sheet up by two corners. It was light. Two arms’ width of paper is not heavy, and it had come a long way to reach the top of this field, and it was going out of date in her hands, and there was no place on earth where it would be true tomorrow.

The wind was coming across the field from the west, off the long fall, towards the valley and the terraces and the town.

Wren let go of it.

It went up and turned and flattened and went out east over the edge of the field and down the face, and it stayed whole for a long time, twenty seconds, thirty, a white sheet getting smaller over the white range, and then it came apart in the middle and went into three pieces, and the pieces went on and got smaller and came apart again, and by the time she had lost them against the grey of the valley there were nine or ten, and they were falling all afternoon, she thought, all the way down the east side, into the hollows and the couloirs and the long snow, and none of them would come back.

She was turning to go when she heard the second set of steps.

Juno Rell came up over the western edge with her field case strapped high and her hood back and her face grey with the altitude, and she stopped when she saw the cairns, and then came on between the lines of them, and stopped beside Wren, and stood looking east over the last line at the empty snow.

She looked at the open cairn and the empty slot and did not need to be told.

‘How many were there?’ she said.

‘Two hundred and something, in lines. Each one a sheet. The highest had the whole range on it, drawn nine years ago, signed P A, and under it, this is today’s. I let it go east.’

Juno looked at her a moment, then out over the edge.

‘You let it go,’ she said. ‘The last one. On purpose.’

‘On purpose.’

Juno said nothing about it. She knelt in the snow, opened her case, took out a sheet of her own paper, weighted the corners, and began to draw the field: the lines, the spacing, the cairns, the count. She worked fast and exactly, the way she did everything, and once she stopped and checked a count and started again, and she did not look once at the eastern edge.

Wren got her copy out and laid it on the snow with a stone on each corner.

‘Third ridge is wrong,’ she said. ‘I drew it from below the rim with the light behind me and I have shortened it about a hundred metres everywhere. The tongue on mine is two days old and the ice has moved since. The west figures are from the way up. Put R on it.’

Juno studied Wren’s sheet for a long time with her pencil up. Then she took it and put it inside her coat against her ribs, with the field book, and made a note in her own case.

‘One half of one sheet of the range, drawn by the only person now living who has stood on the top of the field and seen what it is, wrong in the third ridge by an honest margin,’ she said. ‘Reference. It goes north with the copies, and it gets read, and it gets redrawn, and every hand that redraws it writes its date on it. That is what a survey is. I have been saying so for eleven days and nobody would hear me, and now there is one sheet of it that anybody can check, because it admits what it is.’

She took out her copy of the shrine sheets and they knelt on either side of it with their gloves off, and Juno marked the shrine figures against what could be seen from the field, and Wren took the pencil when her hands allowed and corrected the third ridge from the original she was carrying in her head, and by the end the west side of the range stood down in one place on one paper, and it was a good deal of the whole.

Juno put it away and looked at the open cairn.

‘It should be closed. It is the top of the field and it is where a surveyor works. It should be left as it was found.’

They put the cap stone back, one on each side, and it settled into its courses with a flat sound, and the field lay as it had, except for the two sets of tracks across the new snow and one cairn in the last line with Wren’s initials cut into the underside of the cap, which she did after all, because she had drawn three ridges and lost a place, and there was a way of saying so that did not go into the paper.

Then they turned and went down.

On the western edge Juno stopped and took out the compass, because Wren had handed it to her while her hands worked, and she held it flat on her palm.

The needle swung as soon as it was clear of the coat. North and east, then round, then steady, and it was not pointing north, and it was not pointing back east at the field, and it was not pointing at the open snow where a man had stood.

It pointed west and down. Down the long fall and the tongue and the moraine and the terraces, and past all of those, at the town in the valley where the river did not move and where a chart shop on the lower terrace took its light late.

Wren looked at the needle for a while.

‘That is not up,’ she said.

‘No,’ said Juno.

‘It has never once pointed at the way down.’

‘No,’ said Juno.

Wren took the compass back and put it inside her coat against her ribs. She put on her pack and tightened the strap and took the weight, and it came up onto her hips the way it always did, and the packet of names and the tin were in the top pocket, and the coil of cut line was on her harness, and the copies were on Juno, and about a third of the range was inside Juno’s coat, dated, with an honest margin on the third ridge.

She turned west, and went down.
