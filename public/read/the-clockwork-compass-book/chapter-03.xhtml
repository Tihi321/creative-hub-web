Alpine Style

At five in the morning Vell’s yard was colder than the shop cellar in January, where she had put a thermometer before, and she had thought at the time that the cellar was the coldest place a person could stand.

The gate stood open. The lamp was turned down to a bead on the bench, and her pack stood beside it, and the spring balance hung from the gate post and swung a little in the wind coming down off the quarry.

She had the load list out of her coat before he looked up. Two columns in pencil, item and weight in grams, totalled at the foot between two ruled lines. Twenty-one kilos six hundred.

Vell read it, handed it back, and made no remark on the ruling, which had taken her twenty minutes and two attempts. He lifted the pack onto the balance hook. The spring gave, the pointer steadied.

‘Twenty-two,’ he said.

‘My list is right.’

‘Your list is right. You have not written the four hundred grams you are wearing.’

She stood with the paper in her hand and worked it out. Helmet on her head. Harness on her hips. Field book in her coat. Compass on its cord. The list was what went into the bag. The load was what she would be carrying at four thousand metres, and the load did not care which pocket it lived in.

‘Write it as it is worn,’ Vell said, ‘or you will tell yourself a story halfway up about how light you are.’ He took the pack off the hook and set it down on the bench. ‘Now the rules. Say them back to me at the top of the moraine. If you have to stop and think about one, we turn around where we stand, and you still owe me the first half.’

He did the rules with his hands rather than his eyes.

The rope came off its peg first. He checked it there, on the bench, end to end, in five passes of both hands, turning the rope a quarter at each pass so that every metre of it was felt four times over. Then he said it would be checked again at the point where they roped up, on the rope itself and not on the memory of the first check. A rope could be dropped on stone between the shop and the ice and take up a cut from something nobody saw. ‘Checked twice,’ he said. ‘Checked, not looked at.’

Rule two was helmets, and helmets were not for the climbing. They were for everything above you, which included stone, ice, a dropped ice screw, a karabiner off the rack, and the leader’s spare tool if it came off its clip. ‘From the intake up there is something above you,’ Vell said. ‘A man who takes his helmet off on a ledge to cool his head has decided to trust his luck, and his luck is not a surveyed line, it is a guess.’

Rule three was the slings. Six nylon slings, shock-absorbing, wound into an oiled cotton bag that lived inside the pack and not on the harness, because a wet sling freezes to the stiffness of a stick, and a stick cannot be run through the fingers to find the cut that matters. ‘You cannot see a fray in nylon,’ he said. ‘You feel for it. Frozen, you feel nothing, and then you trust it anyway.’

Rule four was anchors, and he put a nut on the bench with a sling through it and took both off again while he talked. ‘We bring all our metal home. Nothing of ours stays on the mountain. Not for the next party, not for a memory. A party that leaves its anchors behind it has decided something in its head about how this is going to end.’

Wren wrote the four in the back of the field book and said them back wrong twice. The first time, she put the rope check before the slings, and he made her start again from the beginning. The second time she said ‘checked twice’ without saying where. The third time she had them, and he closed the gate behind her and lifted the bar into its keeper.

Then the numbers, which were the fifth rule and were not written down anywhere, because they were not a rule. They were a bill.

Eight days of food. One stove, one pot, one spoon, one rope, one shovel between them. A day of spare fuel and no more. No tent: a bothy bag, the shovel, and their own heat, and on the glacier they would dig in for ten minutes or lie on the ice and be cold until light. No reserve.

‘If it sits on us for three days at the col,’ Vell said, ‘we lie in it and eat and come out thin, and nobody in this town will ever hear about it. If it sits for five, we come down thin and hungry and I hear about it for a year. That is the trade. We move in days, so we carry days. That is the whole of the method and there is no cleverness in it anywhere.’

‘And the slow way,’ Wren said.

‘The slow way carries weeks.’ He shouldered his pack and settled it with one shrug, the way another man puts on a hat. ‘A party with porters can sit out a storm and eat hot food and hold its judgement together at altitude, and patience is the best tool in the box. It buys that patience with other people’s backs. It also never finds out what it can carry, because it has never carried it. Both are true at once. Hold both. You will meet him.’

‘Crane went up a week ago.’

‘Crane went up the valley a week ago with eleven men, four mules and more fuel than this town burns in a winter. If he is at the top before us it will not be because he is quicker. It will be because he did not have to be.’

They went down through the terraces at ten past five, with the lamp off and no light anywhere but the mill and one window at the post house.

Wren knew the paving by its sound. The second bench was flagstone and rang flat under a nailed boot. The third was cobble and scraped. Above the third stair the road broke into loose stone that shifted under the foot and never settled again, and she counted her way up that stretch the way she had counted it three nights before with the pack on, and the count came out the same.

They passed the shop with its shutters over the window. She stopped and read the barograph through the glass by holding the lamp to it: seven hundred and forty-one millimetres and falling. Six down since the evening. She wrote it in the field book by feel, in the dark, and Vell did not stop, and she had to jog four steps to be behind him again.

At the corner before the second stair the frost lay thick in the shadow of the wall, and there were boot prints in it, coming out from the wall and going down towards the mill. A man’s print, town soles, smooth as a plate. No horse.

Wren stepped over them and looked at the next stone and said nothing. Vell did not ask. He had not been told and did not need to be.

Below the mill the road ran beside the river and the river ran under ice, and at the last bridge Wren went across first, because Vell said the plank had a spring in it and he wanted to watch how she took the spring with twenty-two kilos on her back. She took it with short steps, without gripping the parapet, and he said, ‘Again,’ so she went back and did it a second time. He did not say why. She did not ask, because she had learned how far that went.

Above the bridge the road turned up the valley and began to climb, and they climbed it at a rate she could hold. Vell set the pace by his breathing and not by hers. That was rule six, if there was a rule six: you walk at the rate of the slowest man, and the slowest man had better be able to keep breathing through his nose and walk at the same time.

The water in the bottle in the pack lid was slush by the second hour. She learned to carry the bottle inside her coat, against her ribs, where it stayed liquid and where it sat next to the compass and warmed it, and after that the two of them travelled together, the bottle on one side and the brass case on the other, and she could feel both of them with every step.

The intake came up in the grey. It was a stone lip with a hatch and a chain and a slot of black water going down the hill to the town, and past it the road stopped being a road and became a track of flattened stone where carts had once gone, and then a track of no kind at all. There was no light left anywhere below them. Vell stopped and put his helmet on, and she put hers on, and he checked the chin strap with two fingers under it and did not tell her why, since the rules had already been said.

Then the moraine, which was the valley’s rubbish heap: stone on stone, grey and wet and loose, rounded where the old ice had carried it and split where the frost had been at it since. It went up in ribs and gullies, and a rib was solid and a gully was not, and the difference was not visible until she had her hand on it.

Vell made her call the ground every half hour. He would stop, and point with his chin, and say, ‘That slope. Will it move?’

The first one she called wrong. It was a pale slope above a band of rock, and the angle was gentle, and she said it would hold. He put his hand flat against the slope and pushed, and a slab the size of a door moved away from the hillside and came to rest four feet down, and the sound it made was a sound like a drawer opening.

‘Say why it moved,’ he said.

She looked at it properly. ‘The smooth side is facing out. It has slid before. It will slide again, on the same plane, and it will take the new snow with it.’

‘That is the answer. Say the answer first next time and you will not have to push anything to find it.’

At noon they came to the top of the second moraine, which was a bench of flat stones and old ice, and there the chart and the ground stopped agreeing in the way Vell had promised they would.

The second valley ran east of where Perrin had drawn it. She stood at the stream, which was running water under a shell of ice, and set her eye down the line of it to the bend and the next bend, and laid her thumb on the sheet, and put a pencil to the paper and moved the stream twenty metres. It was the first correction she had made to his chart, and she made it small, in a dotted line with the date in the margin, and her hand did not shake, and she was pleased about that until she noticed that she had been holding her breath.

Crane’s marks began above the second moraine.

The first was a ford. The stream went through it in a shallow sheet, and somebody had cut steps in the ice on both banks, square edged and clean and cut for other people. Wren put her boot in one and it held; a week of wind had not filled it.

The second was the tracks. They lay in the snow of a long flat rib, frozen hard enough that the detail had set like cast metal: a line of boot prints up the middle, mule hooves on the outside of the line, and the wide furrow of a sledge dragged between them, the furrow polished where the runners had gone over wet snow and it had frozen after. Eleven men. She could see where the heaviest of them had planted his heels and where the line had stopped for a rest, because there the snow was printed all in a mess the size of a room.

The third was a depot under an overhang of black rock at the head of the moraine. Four crates. Two drums of kerosene. A tarpaulin of oiled canvas over the top with stones on its edges. A sack of grain for the mules, opened and tied at the neck.

And on the flat slate, weighted with a stone, in pencil, in a soft hand:

To whoever is behind us — take the fuel, we have plenty. Wood is dry under the slate. Leave the biscuits, they are better than they look. H.C.

Wren read it twice. Vell read it once and turned the slate over and put it back.

They took nothing. Not the fuel, not the biscuits, not the wood. Wren wrote the position in the field book, and the height by the barometer, and the contents of the depot, and the words of the note, and under it she wrote the pencil grade, because the pencil was soft and wide in the same way as the margin notes on Perrin’s chart, and a soft pencil in a wet coat pocket makes a mark like that and no other pencil does.

‘Write the rock and not the crates,’ Vell said, when he saw the page. ‘The crates will be firewood by the spring. The rock will still be here and it will be somewhere else, and either way we have written a survey of a depot, which is a survey of a decision somebody made about the weather.’

She sat on the flat slate with the pack off her shoulders for the first time in seven hours, and ate three biscuits, and thought about the pencil, and the compass turned once against her ribs and the cord pulled at her neck.

It pulled like a hand on a sleeve. One short tug and then nothing.

She got it out of the sock, because she wanted to know whether the fall she had taken on the scree below the ford had cracked the case. That was what she told herself. She held it flat in her palm and opened the lid, and the needle woke up and swung slow, left and back, left and back, so that she could not have said which way it wanted to go, except that it was not north and had never been north.

Vell watched it for four or five seconds. He watched it the way he watched weather, which was longer than he watched her.

‘Put it away,’ he said.

‘Do you want to know what it does?’

‘No.’

She closed the lid and put it back inside her coat. Nobody in Lowmarch had seen it and let it go unasked. Kessick had asked, and Fennick had asked, and Deller had offered more money than the shop made in a season to have it, and this man had watched it swing against every known bearing and asked nothing at all. A man with no curiosity was a man who did not know where he was going, and this one knew where he was going to within a foot. So it was an answer, not a silence. He had seen it before.

They put their packs on. The compass went back against her ribs with the bottle.

Above the second moraine the ground rose to the black shoulder, and the black shoulder was a face.

It leaned back from the ice at the angle of a steep staircase, three hundred metres of wet rock and frozen rubble with a band of old ice in the middle of it that ran the whole width like a stripe painted on. Everything on it was loose. The sun came onto it in the afternoon and let the frost out of the cracks, and the frost had been holding what the winter had left up there, and what the winter had left up there was a great deal of stone.

They put their crampons on at the foot of it and kept their helmets on, and Vell rapped her shell with a knuckle and then put two fingers under the chin strap to check the fit.

‘You go behind me,’ he said. ‘Not alongside. A single stone can take two men who are walking abreast, and it cannot take two men who are ten metres apart, one behind the other, and out of the other man’s line. If I stop, you stop somewhere that is not under me. If you hear anything at all, you do not look up. You look at your feet and you step in under the rock and you wait.’

‘Understood.’

‘Say what I said about stopping.’

‘Stop somewhere that is not under you.’

They went up.

The first hundred metres were the worst of it, because the rubble moved under the crampons and the points had no purchase until she found the trick of it, which was to set the foot flat and let the whole sole take the stone instead of the points. Her calf began to burn on a count of four steps up and two breaths out, and she held that count and did not change it, because changing the count was the beginning of going too fast.

The sun came round onto the face as they climbed. She heard the frost letting go of the rock all around them, a small ticking sound like a clock being wound, and the ticking was stone coming loose and settling into the next crack down, and the next one, and one day the next one.

The sun was on her back and then it was gone behind the shoulder, and the light went grey and flat, and the ticking stopped.

The click came from above and to the left. It was small and separate from the other sounds of the mountain, and after the click there was a pause of a quarter of a second, and then the whine.

‘Stone,’ Vell said. Not loudly.

Wren looked up.

It was the size of a loaf of bread and it was turning as it came and it had no noise of its own except the whine, and the whine was the air going past it, and she saw all of that in the time it takes to bring a hand to the face, and then it hit her on the crown of the helmet.

The helmet rang. Not a thud: a ring, like a cracked bell held against the ear, and the ring went through her jaw and down into her shoulders and took her to one knee. Grit came down on her face and into her mouth, wet and fine. The rope came tight across her chest with a snap and a weight on the other end of it and she put both hands flat on the rubble and stayed there, and the rubble was moving a little under her palms, and the ringing went on and on.

Vell was beside her before the dust had gone past, with his hand flat on the crown of her helmet, turning it gently to check under the rim for blood.

‘Name.’

‘Wren Ashgrove.’

‘Day.’

‘Tuesday. It was Tuesday when we left the intake and it is still Tuesday.’

‘What did you eat at the depot?’

‘Three biscuits and a piece of the hard cheese, and I did not drink enough water, and I know that, and it is written in the book.’ Her own voice sounded to her as though it were being spoken one room away.

He turned the helmet in his hands and looked at the shell under the lamp of the sky, and found the mark. A white scar the size of a thumbnail, with a ring of crazed paint round it like the ring a hammer leaves on a stone. He checked the inside. He checked the straps. He gave it back.

‘Put it on,’ he said. ‘You looked up.’

‘Yes.’

‘There is nothing up there worth looking at. There is only the thing that is coming down. Look at your feet and step in under the wall and wait for it.’

He turned and went on up. The stone had struck the rubble behind her and broken into pieces, and two of those pieces were lying on the ground, each the size of her fist, wet and grey, in the line they had just walked.

She put her own weight back on her right foot, and then her left, and she put her feet where his feet had been, and she counted four steps to a breath, and the ringing in her left ear did not settle, and it did not settle all the way up the face.
