Dinner on the Vltava

"We could not go," said Dec, on the embankment, at 19:55.

"We could not go," Inès agreed. "And then we would sit in the pension all night, wondering what she did instead."

So they went.

The paddle steamer Vyšehrad lay against the quay below the Rašínovo embankment with every light on board burning. White and long and old-fashioned, a tall funnel, two great paddle boxes with her name painted on them in gold, a gangway carpeted in red. Up on her deck a string quartet sawed at something Viennese. Beyond her the river ran black and wide and fast, and away downstream, high over the water, the Castle sat on its hill, lit gold from below like a toy in a shop window at Christmas.

Dec had come as he was, because the card said he could and he did not want to give her the satisfaction of a hired suit. He had compromised to the extent of a clean shirt under the green jumper and a tie lent by Vilém Šťastný, a brown knitted object from about 1975 that Šťastný had handed over with the words, "For funerals. Bring it back." Inès had gone into a shop in Pařížská at five o'clock in her trench coat and come out at half past in a plain black dress that had cost, she informed him on the tram, more than the bus to Prague and back for both of them, and she would be making him hear about that as well. She had kept the mustard scarf. She said it was the only thing in Prague she trusted.

He had spent the day on his back in front of the bronze cabinet, and had learned that every lever in it rang a different note when touched. He had a headache in the exact shape of a bell.

At the foot of the gangway a young woman with a tablet asked their names, found them, and smiled at Dec's tie with great professional kindness.

"Mrs Hollerová is so glad you came."

The saloon ran half the length of the boat, panelled in pale wood, with brass rails, three chandeliers that swung very slightly as the boat moved, and long windows on both sides full of the lights of Prague going by. There were men in dinner jackets and women in silk, and the air was perfume and canapés with coal smoke and hot oil from the engine room underneath.

Dec did not bother with the faces. He looked at wrists, a habit he had got into in the spring. Most of them had ordinary watches, gold and steel, expensive and dull. But a grey-haired man by the bar wore a plain silver watch with a white face and no hands, and so did the woman he was talking to, and a young man with a beard by the piano, and an old man in a wheelchair with a medal on his lapel.

"Eleven," said Inès quietly beside him. She had been counting too. "Twelve. The waiter with the tray."

"Grand," said Dec. "So we're at a party with the whole parish."

"Monsieur Hartley."

He knew the voice before he turned, because no one else in the world said his name like a diagnosis.

Commissaire Albert Pichon stood by a buffet table in a rumpled dinner jacket that had been bought for a thinner and happier man, with a napkin tucked into his collar and a small plate in his hand on which lay a single dumpling the size of a walnut, speared on a cocktail stick.

"They put a dumpling on a stick," he said, before anybody could say hello. "It is an outrage against the dumpling. I have had eleven." He ate the twelfth. "What are you doing here?"

"We were invited," said Inès.

"So was I. The PragClear Foundation is paying for the conference lunch, and so the conference is invited to the boat. A policeman must never refuse a lunch." He dabbed his moustache. "I have refused very few. But I always ask why it was given." He considered Dec's tie, and visibly decided not to ask about it. "I told you both to stay out of this."

"You told us in Paris," said Dec. "This is Prague."

"Ah. Then you are right, and I have no authority here at all." Pichon turned. "Which is why I have brought someone who has. Major Kroupová. Madame Varga, Monsieur Hartley, of whom I have spoken."

The woman behind him had been there all along, and Dec had not seen her, which he suspected was the point of her. She was thin and dark, about forty, with her hair pulled back in a knot so tight it looked like a reprimand, and she wore a grey parka, zipped to the chin, over a dark suit, on a boat full of evening dress. She held a glass of water and nothing else.

"Mr Hartley," she said. "The commissaire says you are a locksmith who finds bodies."

"Not bodies. Not as such. More sort of the things near them."

"I see." Her English came out in clean, short pieces, like something read from a manual about English. "And tonight?"

Dec told her, as fast and as quietly as he could, about the man on the balcony and the hole in the glass, the twins at both ends of the square, and the woman on the tape who said again, from the beginning.

Kroupová heard him out without moving her face.

"Was a complaint filed about the window?"

"There wasn't really time. We were leaving through the kitchen."

"So. A man cuts a window. Also glaziers cut windows." She sipped her water. "Two men stand on a square. Many men stand on squares. A tape of an old woman reading names. I am taking note, Mr Hartley. I am not taking action." She glanced at Pichon's plate. "Commissaire. You will be ill."

"She does not eat," said Pichon to Dec, with real distress. "I have watched her for two days. Not one dumpling. I begin to think she is a hallucination."

"I eat," said Kroupová, "at home."

Renata Hollerová found them by the long windows as the boat came round under the Charles Bridge, its statues going over their heads black against the floodlit sky.

She was taller than Dec had expected, nearly his height, with ash-blonde hair cut off sharp at the line of her jaw and eyes the grey of a pigeon's wing. Her dress was charcoal silk cut as plainly as a suit, and at her throat on a fine chain hung a single small bronze bell. On her left wrist was a plain silver watch with a white face and no hands.

"Mr Hartley," she said. Her voice was low and warm and in no hurry at all, and her English was better than his. "Madame Varga. I'm so glad. You're the only two people on this boat I actually wanted to meet." She held out her hand. Dec shook it, because his mother had raised him, and it was cool and dry and strong. "And you came as you are. I hoped you would. That tie is wonderful. Is it Vilém's?"

Dec said nothing at all, which Inès told him afterwards was the cleverest thing he did all night.

A waiter came by with a tray. Hollerová took two glasses of champagne from it and gave one to each of them, and took a third for herself.

Dec held his up to the chandelier and turned it. He sniffed it. He dipped the tip of his little finger in and touched it to his tongue, while the president of a bank looked on in mild horror and Hollerová watched him with grey, delighted eyes.

There was something. Or there was nothing. There was the ghost of a spice at the very back of his tongue, or there was six months of tasting cloves in every cup of coffee in Paris. He could not have sworn to it in a court of law, and that was worse than if it had been plain.

"You're very thorough," said Hollerová. "I like that in a man who opens doors."

"I like to know what I'm drinking."

"So few people do." She smiled. "Mr Hartley, I'll be honest with you, since you're Irish and it will confuse you. I haven't asked you here to hurt you. I've asked you here to offer you a job."

He had not expected that. It must have shown.

"The Foundation is building something in Pankrác, next to the tower," she said. "A place for families. We call them experiences. You lock people in a room for an hour, and they pay you for it, and they find their way out together, and they come out happier than they went in." She turned her glass slowly by its stem. "You've built a whole business on the most religious idea in the world, and you don't even know it. An hour, given back. Shall I tell you what I believe?"

"I've a feeling you're going to."

She did. She told it beautifully, in that low unhurried voice, as the lights of the Old Town went by in the long windows behind her. Time was not theirs. It had never been anybody's. It had been lent, every hour of it, by the Clockless One, who had made the world and set it going and stepped outside it, and every hour a person lived was an hour owed back. Most people never thought about it. They spent their hours like children spending a stranger's money. But at the end of the world, at the Ninth Hour, the loan would be called in, and only the ones who had paid ahead would stand clear of the debt.

"And you pay ahead," said Inès.

"I sleep four hours a night, Madame Varga, and I give the rest away. I have done it since I was nineteen." She said it simply, without pride, as another woman might mention that she did not eat meat. "I don't ask anybody to do what I don't do. I only ask them to pay what they owe."

"Pensioners," said Inès.

"Everybody owes. A pensioner more than most. Think how long they've had." Hollerová touched the bronze bell at her throat. "My family has cast bells in Bohemia for three hundred years, Mr Hartley. Do you know what a bell is? It's a mouth that tells people the time. That's all. Whether they want to hear it or not."

Dec opened his own mouth to say something about bells, and he never afterwards knew what, because at that moment Inès's heel came down on his foot with the whole of her weight behind it, and what came out was a sort of cough.

"Mr Hartley?"

"Lovely boat," said Dec.

A pink man with a moustache was hovering at Hollerová's elbow, a minister of something, with a voice made for opening bridges. She turned to him with a little apology, and gave him her hand and her whole attention, and put her full glass down on the window ledge beside Dec's elbow as she did it.

Dec looked at his glass. He looked at hers. Then, with a small smooth movement he had practised on a great many keys, he set his down beside hers on the ledge and picked hers up.

When Hollerová turned back, she studied the glass on the ledge. Then she looked at Dec, and she smiled the way a grown-up smiles at a child who has hidden behind a curtain with his shoes sticking out. She did not pick it up. She did not drink anything at all for the rest of the evening. Neither did Dec. He stood holding her champagne like a man holding somebody else's baby at a christening.

"I have something for you," she said, and took a slim phone out of her bag, touched it once, and held it out to him. "Somebody would like to say hello."

He took it. He did not want to, and he took it anyway.

"Mr Hartley." The voice was warm oil, every consonant laid down in its place. "Good evening. Auguste de Vaulmont. It's raining here in the Sologne. My magistrate will ring on Tuesday, and I shall tell him so."

"You said you weren't going to travel," said Dec.

"I haven't. You have." Vaulmont sounded pleased with him. "I only rang to ask after your lock-picks. The little roll you keep in your satchel. The cutlery, I believe you call it. Do you still have it with you?"

Dec's hand went to the satchel on his shoulder before he could stop it.

"Good," said Vaulmont softly. "Keep it close, Mr Hartley. Sooner or later somebody always asks a locksmith to hand over his tools. Give my regards to Madame Varga. Tell her the geraniums in Lyon are still dry."

The line went dead. Dec gave the phone back. His hand was perfectly steady and he was proud of it.

"He's very fond of you both," said Hollerová, putting the phone away. "Auguste is fond of everyone he's going to outlive." She lifted her hand to the minister, who was waving from the bar, and then turned back to Dec, and lowered her voice as if she were telling him where the good cake was.

"On Sunday everybody in Prague gets an extra hour, Mr Hartley," she said. "Except those who owe."

He went out on deck because he needed air that she had not breathed.

The night was cold and the river smelt of mud and leaves. The paddles thrashed and thundered on either side of him in their great wooden boxes, and the deck shook under his boots, and the Castle went by overhead, gold on black, with the cathedral spires stuck up out of it like the hands of a clock that had stopped. Inès came out after him and stood at the rail with her scarf wound twice round her neck.

"You swapped the glasses," she said.

"I did."

"Was it in yours?"

"I don't know." He looked at the black water. "That's the thing. I don't know. And she knows I don't know."

Inès put her hand on the rail beside his, not touching it, the way she had put it on the armrest of the bus.

Dec looked forward, along the length of the boat, past the funnel and the lit windows of the saloon, to the bow. A man stood right at the point of it, against the rail, in a black overcoat and a grey scarf, with his shaven head pale in the light from the deck lamps. He was facing the stern. He was facing Dec.

Dec turned his head and looked the other way, aft, to the stern.

The other one was there. He stood at the very back of the boat, against the flag, with his hands folded in front of him, facing the bow.

Neither of them moved. They had not come aboard while he watched, or arrived from anywhere. They had simply been at the two ends of the boat all evening, like the two weights of a scale, with the whole of the PragClear Foundation's charity evening balanced between them, in full view of a major of police.

"Inès," he said. "Don't look round."

"I have already looked round," said Inès. "Both ways."

His phone rang in his pocket.

He nearly dropped it into the Vltava. The screen showed a number he did not know, a Prague number, and when he answered it there was a long wheezing breath on the line and a noise of traffic, as if somebody were running down a street with the phone held out in front of him like a torch.

"Hartley!"

"Mr Šťastný?" Dec put his hand over his other ear. "You said you'd never ring."

"Shut up. Listen." The old man's breath was coming in great dragging heaves. "Seven o'clock I go to my clock. Friday, the oiling, two hours, like always. I come back to Kožná now. The door is open. The chain is cut, like with scissors." He stopped, and gasped, and Dec heard him swear in German. "The two. The same. The neighbour sees them on the stair at half past seven."

"Where's Dagmar?"

"Gone." For a moment the gruff voice cracked straight down the middle. "She is gone, Hartley. Her coat is on the chair. The coat with the fox. She does not go to the bathroom without that coat."

Dec lowered the phone.

Through the long lit windows of the saloon he could see Renata Hollerová by the piano, listening to the minister with her head on one side. As he watched, she glanced down at the watch on her wrist, the one with no hands, and smiled, as if it had told her the time.
