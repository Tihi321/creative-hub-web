Partners

Sunday the twenty-fifth of October had twenty-five hours in it. Inès slept through five of them in a narrow bed in Liliová with her boots still on.

She woke at noon to church bells and to the smell of paní Benešová's coffee, which was served at seven and not five past, and had been waiting for her since then out of pure disapproval. Through the wall Dec was singing in the bath, badly, something about a girl from Spiddal. Her hair was full of clay. The bent hairpin was still in it.

She lay and looked at the ceiling and did the sum again, because she could not help it. One hour given. One hour given back. Somewhere in Paris, Madame Ardouin had spent hers listening for her eleventh key.

Her phone had a message from her mother, sent at nine. Are you eating? Is the Irishman eating? I have watered the geraniums. Somebody should.

Pichon had chosen the restaurant, which meant a window onto the square, starched cloths, a waiter who had been told who Dagmar was and kept coming back to refill the water just to hear her say děkuji, and a smell of roast duck that reached into the street and pulled people in by the nose.

He was already eating when they came in. Duck, red cabbage, sauerkraut, and on the side of the plate, in a little row like loaves in a baker's window, the bread dumplings.

"I am on my second dumpling," he said, without rising. "I wished you to know that I waited for the first."

Major Kroupová sat across from him in a dark suit with no parka over it, which made her look like someone else's sister. She had a plate of duck in front of her. She was eating it.

Dec stopped in the doorway.

"I have decided to eat," said Kroupová, without looking up. "It is Sunday. I have been awake for thirty hours. And the minister with the pink moustache rang me at nine to congratulate me. On Friday he drank PragClear's champagne." She cut a piece of duck with great precision. "So I am not in trouble on Monday. Sit down."

They sat. Inès took a photograph of her plate and sent it to Lyon. Then she ate, for the first time in what felt like a week, and listened.

It came out between mouthfuls, in Kroupová's flat voice and Pichon's round one, the way the plan had come out in the beer hall: in pieces.

PragClear's emergency stop had held. Jan's brake, once pulled, could not be released by anyone at the bank, and nobody had tried very hard, because by three in the morning winter time there had been eleven policemen in the glass tower in Pankrác and a night supervisor in tears. Every one of the forty-six thousand two hundred and twelve payments had gone out once, at the first two o'clock, to the people they belonged to. Not one crown had gone twice.

"Annex C," said Kroupová, "is a list of three hundred accounts. Two hundred and ninety are in this country. Ten are not." She looked at Pichon.

"Four in Liechtenstein," said Pichon, "belong to a foundation that has a very short name and a very long memory. In the spring it paid the bail of a gentleman in the Sologne." He wiped his moustache. "I rang his magistrate this morning. The magistrate rang him at half past two, the first time, out of spite. He answered on the first ring and spoke about mushrooms for twenty minutes. He has not left France. He has not touched a crown. He cannot be touched." He sighed into the sauerkraut. "Auguste is the only man I know who could be an accessory to a theft that did not happen while discussing mushrooms in another country."

"And Ferrand?"

"Ah." Pichon brightened. "Mademoiselle Ferrand was collected at six this morning in the rue des Rosiers, in a dressing gown, by two of my officers. She said, Oh, how lovely, and asked whether she might bring her own coffee. They said no. Her cousin, it appears, maintains the screens at Bercy." He folded his napkin over a fragment of dumpling, for later. "A family of talent."

Hollerová had been charged at seven, and had asked for a lawyer, a pot of tea and the Financial Times, in that order. She had said nothing else. The twins had not said a word between them, which surprised nobody, but they had had to be cut apart by the fire brigade at five, because nobody at the station could undo the knot, and the fire brigade had wanted to know who tied it.

"Each of them had a stick of chalk in his coat pocket," said Kroupová. "Worn down to the same length. And their passports went to Paris twice this year. In May, and last week."

"The grave," said Dec. "They searched the vase. They were looking for Jan's stop phrase."

"They were looking for Jan's logs." Kroupová laid down her fork. "We found them in PragClear this morning, where he left them. Last October, the night the clocks went back, the bank ran two hundred payments twice. A test. Small sums. Jan watched it happen from a hotel in Paris and wrote to Dagmar, and at half past two, the second time, his heart stopped." She paused. "The doctor in Paris was right. Nobody killed him. They only let him watch."

There was a silence at the table, which Pichon respected by eating very quietly.

"The recording of the Thursday is evidence," said Kroupová. "The reel from the cabinet, the reel from her clock, and the three copies from the radio archive, which paní Vaňková had hidden in Mr Šťastný's bread bin, next to his telephone." She almost smiled. "And this morning eighty-one people came to my station by themselves, with their watches in envelopes."

Šťastný came at two, in his black Sunday jacket and his cap, walking stiffly, and sat down at the end of the table as if it were his, which by then it was. Dec stood up and held out the brown knitted tie from the paddle steamer, folded.

Šťastný looked at it.

"Keep it," he said. "For funerals. The way you live, you will need it." He took the beer the waiter had brought without being asked. "I forgive you."

"For what?"

"For letting a woman shoot my clock while you lay on the floor." He drank. "On one condition. When you are in Prague, every Sunday, you come up my stair at seven and you oil it with me. Not the wrong oil. My oil."

"Every Sunday," said Dec. "I swear it."

"And the bell of Jan," said Šťastný, to Kroupová. "When your court is finished with it. I hang it in my tower. Never rung." He tapped the table. "I will ring it once a year. At the second two."

Kroupová wrote something in her notebook, and it looked, from where Inès sat, very like yes.

Dagmar arrived at half past two, which she said was the correct time to arrive anywhere, and she did not come alone.

Her hair was up again in a French twist so high and so perfect that the waiter stepped back from it. Her lipstick was the red of a pillar box. She wore a black coat with a velvet collar that she said made her look like a widow, which, she added, she was, very nearly. And on her arm, upright and small and furious at being helped, in a man's cardigan buttoned to the throat and a camel coat with a fox collar, came Božena.

"She called me Mami at the door, because I came in our mother's coat," Dagmar said to Inès, in French, and her voice wobbled once and was mended. "Then she told me Mami never comes on a Sunday. She was very surprised. She has decided to keep the coat."

Božena looked round the table. She looked at Pichon and said something short in German.

"She says, you are the fattest German she has ever seen," said Dagmar, "and she has seen several."

"Tell her," said Pichon, with deep courtesy, "that I am working on it."

Božena sat down beside Dec, whom she had decided to recognise, and ordered dumplings. When they came she leant over to Inès and spoke behind her hand, and Dagmar translated, looking at the ceiling.

"She says her pension came this month only once. She says nothing ever comes twice. She says that is how you know it is real."

Dec took a small brass ring out of his jacket and laid it by Dagmar's plate. Ten new keys hung on it. He had spent the extra hour of the morning, while Inès slept, kneeling at a tall cream double door on the fourth floor in Vinohrady with his cutlery, and then in a key-cutter's shop whose owner had opened on a Sunday because Dagmar had telephoned him.

"I dropped your only one down a stairwell," he said. "I promised you ten."

Dagmar picked them up and counted them, jedna, dvě, tři, the way Madame Ardouin had counted hers, and put them in her pocket. For a moment she said nothing at all, which for Dagmar was the same as a speech.

"Bohouš has asked me to read the time on the radio," she said at last. "On Thursday. At noon. Live, from Studio 4, with a gong. One more broadcast." She lifted her chin. "Then I retire properly. I shall be insufferable."

"You were insufferable already," said Šťastný.

"You were early already."

Across the square, the little windows above the dial opened, and the apostles went by in the afternoon light, and Death rang his bell, and the clock struck three. Šťastný took out his pocket watch, looked at it, and put it away with the face of a man whose wife has come home.

"And my HUIT," said Dagmar to Inès. "It is on your shelf, I think, being looked at."

"Between the glue pot and my father."

"Put it back in Jan's stone. Turn it until the nine is at the top. And on the first of November take him chrysanthemums. The French do that for their dead, and I have never once been there for it." She sniffed. "The florist will overcharge you. Argue."

Dec put the brass token on the cloth beside her plate, Death and his bell face up. Dagmar touched it with one ring.

"I put that in the drawer with my number," she said. "It had the clock's Death on it, so I thought the man who keeps the clock would trust whoever brought it. And the number was so that you would hear my voice first, before anybody's."

"Number eight of twelve," said Šťastný, and pushed it back to Dec with one finger. "I gave it to Jan on my step. Keep it."

The duck was gone, and the dumplings, and the light in the window had turned the colour of weak tea, when Dagmar put down her glass and leant across the table, and dropped the velvet voice until only the five of them could hear it.

"I have one more thing to give you," she said. "The only thing the Eighth knows that nobody else does."

Pichon put down his fork, which Inès had not seen him do all week.

"Once a year," said Dagmar, "on the last night of the year, at the last hour, my telephone rang. A man's voice. Old. He never gave a name. He said, Read, and I read him the debts. The names of who owed what. All of them, the Nine's and everyone's. It took an hour." Her rings turned on the cloth. "He was the Keeper of the Last Hour. I never saw him. But every year, behind his voice, a great bell was tolling. Very deep. So deep I felt it in the telephone, in my hand."

"What note?" said Inès.

Dagmar closed her eyes, and hummed.

Low. Much lower than a woman's voice should go. The glasses on the table shivered and began, faintly, to sing with it. The waiter stopped halfway across the room.

Šťastný did not even wait for her to finish.

"Pummerin," he said. "Stephansdom. In Wien. The big bell of the cathedral." He looked round at them, astonished that they needed telling. "She rings only for the great days. Easter. Christmas. The new year. Every clockmaker in Europe knows her voice."

"So he is in Vienna," said Dec. "Or he has the radio on."

"On the radio she is a little bell," said Šťastný scornfully. He pointed a thick finger at Dagmar's throat. "That is a bell in the next room."

Nobody said anything. Then Pichon reached into his raincoat, which was on the back of his chair, and took out a plastic sleeve, and laid it on the cloth between the plates.

Inside it was a letter, folded in three, and a broken seal of grey wax. In the wax, very small and very clear, was a crown.

"In her desk at the foundry," said Pichon. "Under the blotter. The major opened it this morning, in my presence, like a civilised woman."

Inès read it through the plastic without touching it. It was in French, written with a steel nib in an upright old-fashioned hand, the kind of hand that had been taught by someone with a ruler.

Fifth. Auguste was impatient, and Paris was spoiled. Prague will not be. Send the Keeper his share at the last hour of the year, as always. Do not trouble yourself about the restorer and her locksmith. I know them well. Their sign is very small for something that is not a joke. IX.

Dec read it over her shoulder and frowned.

"Who's been looking at our sign?"

"Half the Marais," said Inès. "It is very bad." But she read the last line again, and something in it nagged at her, the way a signature does that you have seen somewhere before and cannot place, and she could not have said why.

They walked up to Vyšehrad in the last of the light, because Dec wanted to see the river from the rock before they left, and because the train was not until ten. The wind came up off the water smelling of coal smoke and leaves. Below them Prague lay in its bend of the river, with the lamps coming on, one hour behind where it had been yesterday and perfectly content about it.

"When we get home," said Dec, "I'm repainting the sign."

"It does not need repainting."

"It does. Et serrures the same size as the rest. Proper letters. I'll get a man in."

Inès looked at the river without saying anything for a while.

"We are not partners," she said.

Then she turned her head and looked at him, properly, with her glasses pushed up on her nose and the clay still in her hair and the bent pin holding it there.

"We are," she said. "Professionally."

Dec looked back at her. The wind moved his hair about. He did not make a joke, which she noticed, and which she would think about later on the train.

"Purely," he said.

Neither of them said the next thing. It stood there with them on the wall in the cold for a while, quite comfortably, as if it had all the time in the world, and then they went down to catch the train.

This time they took the train. Dec did not argue. He paid. The refund on the tickets he had made her cancel on Wednesday had come back nineteen euros short.

The compartment had four berths, two of them theirs, and a window that would not quite close, and the lamps of the suburbs went by in it like slow sparks. Inès sat with her notebook on her knee and wrote a postcard of the Orloj, addressed to La Santé, care of Maître Carrère. She wrote one word on it, in capitals, as carefully as Kessler, in a prison sweatshirt two sizes too small, had once written one for her: DANKE.

"You owe me nineteen euros," she said, without looking up.

"Nobody owes anything," said Dec. "I heard it on the radio."

The door slid open. A ticket inspector in a navy jacket looked in, checked their tickets, and then, filling in a form on his clipboard, glanced at Dec.

"Excuse me. You have the exact time?"

Dec pushed back his sleeve to look at his watch.

Before he could, a voice spoke from the lower berth opposite, where an elderly woman sat reading under the little lamp with a rug over her knees. It was a dry voice, precise and in no hurry at all, an English learnt from governesses, with old Paris underneath it. It put each word down on the counter like exact change.

"Twenty-two hours, fourteen minutes," she said, "and ten seconds."

The inspector wrote it down. Inès looked at her phone, and it was.

The woman turned a page. She was not wearing a watch. Her coat had fallen open over a grey cardigan, and at her throat, in the lamplight, lay a short string of pearls.

Inès looked at the pearls, and at the cardigan, and something turned in her mind like a key in a lock that is nearly the right one. She had seen that cardigan before, in a doorway, on a landing that smelt of floor polish, and heard that voice say something about letters.

Then the train went into a tunnel, and the window went black and roared, and she lost it. When they came out the other side the woman had turned another page.
