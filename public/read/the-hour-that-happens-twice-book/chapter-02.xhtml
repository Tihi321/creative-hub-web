The Second Half Past Two

The keeper came out of the fog with a cat under each arm and a third walking ahead of him like a lawyer.

Sixties. Green cemetery uniform. A paper bag of cat biscuits poked out of his jacket pocket, and his moustache had the shape and bristle of a broom head. He looked at the three of them standing round Jan Vaněk's grave exactly the way Inès looked at people who leaned on her easels.

"Monsieur Bastide," said Pichon. "These are the experts I mentioned."

"Experts." Bastide set the cats down. The thin grey female went straight to the slab and sat on it beside the chalk circle, guarding it. The large orange one lay down where he was put. "Mimi. Titus. And that's Balzac," he said, nodding at the third, which had found Pichon's shoes and was investigating them. "He's fat. He was named before he was fat, but he grew into it." His moustache drooped. "Chalk. On a man's grave. In the night, like vandals. Thirty-one years I've been here, Commissaire. I've had Satanists. I've had drunks. I've had a German who wanted to dig up Chopin with a spoon. But they leave the quiet ones alone. Nobody bothers the quiet ones."

"Was he quiet?" said Inès.

"Nobody comes." Bastide shook three biscuits into his palm and held them down to Mimi, who ignored them. "Nobody. Not at Toussaint. Not on the anniversary. There was a lady in the spring, when the stone went up. Small. A fur collar, rings on every finger. She stood here with the masons for an hour arguing about a centimetre. Spoke French like the radio." He sniffed. "And the flowers. Somebody pays the florist on the corner to put chrysanthemums in that vase every month. This morning I find the vase on its side and the earth all over the gravel. Look at it."

Inès looked. The vase was granite, heavy, and somebody had not simply knocked it over. They had tipped the earth out in a heap and gone through the heap with their fingers, then raked the gravel round it into little furrows. Somebody had been looking for something small, in a hurry, and had not found it.

"They searched it," she said.

"They desecrated it," said Bastide.

"They searched it," said Inès, "and then they desecrated it. In that order."

Bastide turned and looked at her properly for the first time. Then he nodded, very slightly, as if she had passed a test he did not usually set.

Dec was still crouched in front of the stone, wet patches spreading on his knees. "Half past two the second time," he said again. "How do you die at half past two the second time? Was it a typo? Did he die, and then...?"

"Then what?" said Pichon. "Got up and did it again?"

"You know what I mean."

"Last October," said Inès, "on the last Sunday of the month, summer time ended." They both looked up at her. "At three in the morning the clocks went back to two. So the hour from two to three happened twice. Everybody who was awake that night lived through half past two, and then an hour later they lived through it again." She looked at the line cut into the granite. "If you died in that hour, the doctor has to write which half past two. There are two. The registry would just write two thirty. Somebody who cared would add the second time."

Pichon took his reading glasses off their cord, put them on, and looked at the stone through them for some time, as though it had deceived him personally.

"I always sleep through that," he said at last.

"Everybody does," said Inès. "That's what it's for."

She had not known she was going to say that last part, and she did not like the sound of it out loud in the fog. Pichon looked at her over the glasses and said nothing.

She made herself look at the whole stone, the way she would look at a canvas before touching it: top to bottom, edges first.

At the very top of the black granite, above the name, there was a round recess. She had taken it at first for the kind of hollow where families fix a photograph of the dead in a little ceramic oval, and many of the graves around them had exactly that. But this one was empty and had never held a photograph. It was cut cleanly into the stone, the size of a saucer and perhaps a finger deep, and round its rim the mason had carved twelve short ticks. At the top, where twelve should be, he had cut a small, careful IX.

An empty clock. A clock with nothing in it.

Under the dates and the impossible hour there was one more line, at the bottom, in the same capitals but larger, as if it were the thing the stone was really for.

VOX TEMPORIS NON MENTITVR.

"Latin," said Pichon, in the tone of a man who has found a hair in his soup.

"Vox, the voice. Temporis, of time. Non mentitur, does not lie." The V in MENTITVR stood for a U, the old way, as it did on every church in Paris. "The voice of time does not lie."

Dec stood up very slowly. She could see him thinking it, and she could see him trying not to say it, and failing.

"The woman on the machine," he said. "Telling us the time. Commissaire, I told you, on the phone, she rang the atelier last Wednesday at nine o'clock exactly and..."

"A woman telling you the time, Monsieur Hartley, is not a crime. In Switzerland it's a profession." Pichon put his glasses back on their cord. "Madame Varga. The clock face, please."

She took it out of its padded envelope and held it up, in its tissue, beside the recess. Not touching. The white enamel, the twelve marks, the IX at the top, HUIT in grey ink. Against the black granite they looked like a question and an answer, or two halves of something, and she could not tell by eye whether it would fit. She thought it might be a little large. She thought it might not.

Dec reached for it.

"No," said Bastide and Pichon together.

"I was only going to..."

"You were going to put a thing into a grave in front of a commissaire of police," said Pichon, "and its keeper, who has a cat on it. You will not." He took the napkin out of his collar at last, looked at it in some surprise, and folded it into his raincoat pocket. "The commissariat of the twentieth will photograph this stone tomorrow, and nobody will touch it before then. Monsieur Bastide is going to lock the gates at six, as he does every day. And now I'm going to tell you something kindly, because I like you both, and because I've just had a very good lunch."

Inès put the clock face back in its envelope. She took out her phone, and from her trench coat pocket the little grey scale card she used in every photograph she took at work, a strip of card printed with a centimetre rule and a band of greys, and she laid it flat against the granite just under the recess and photographed the two together, three times, straight on. Pichon watched her do it and did not stop her.

"This man," he said, "was a Czech citizen. He's buried in Paris, but he was born in Prague and he worked there all his life, according to his file, which I read over the fish. If somebody has drawn a chalk clock on his grave, that's a matter for the Czech police, and for Europol, and I'll write them a very good letter. It's not a matter for a restorer and a locksmith." He looked at Dec. "Especially not for a locksmith."

"Understood," said Dec.

"You'll stay out of it."

"Absolutely."

"Good." Pichon turned his collar up against the fog. Then, in the voice of a man mentioning the weather: "I'll be away from Thursday, as it happens. Europol has a conference on heritage crime. Stolen chalices, forged icons, that sort of thing. Four days of slides." He paused. "In Prague."

Inès and Dec looked at each other.

"I don't go for the slides," said Pichon. "I go for the dumplings." And he went away down the path into the white, and Balzac followed him a few yards, hopefully, then thought better of it and came back.

She could not sleep.

At ten she gave up on bed. At a quarter to eleven she was at her bench in the atelier in her coat, under the one lamp, with the skylight black above her and the clock face out of its tissue on a clean sheet of blotting paper.

It weighed almost nothing. Copper with white enamel fired onto it, a little crazed at the edge where somebody, long ago, had dropped it. On the back, the bare copper was dull brown, and near the rim stood its two dial feet, the stubby copper pins that had once fixed it to the movement of its clock. Every one of Roux's eight clockmakers had turned it over and looked at those feet and said carriage clock and twelve euros. Nobody had said anything else about them, because there was nothing else to say. They were dial feet. Every clock dial had them.

She took her steel calipers out of the drawer and measured the face across, three times, at three angles, because enamel dials were not always truly round. One hundred and thirty-eight millimetres, and a half.

Then she opened the photograph of the recess on her laptop, with the grey scale card lying flat beneath it, and enlarged it until the centimetre rule on the card filled the screen, and counted. She did it twice. She checked the angle of the shot against the card's edges, and corrected for it, and did it a third time, because the first two had made her heart go too fast to trust.

One hundred and thirty-eight millimetres, and a half.

The same. To the millimetre. To the half.

She sat back. In the dark corner of the atelier the old answering machine glowed its small red 0 at her, empty now, because Dec had cleared it. Vox temporis non mentitur. A woman's warm voice telling a restorer's atelier that it was nine o'clock, and nothing minutes, and nothing seconds. A courier in April with a parcel. A stone that went up in the spring, and a small woman with a fur collar arguing with the masons for an hour about a centimetre.

Not a centimetre. Half a millimetre.

She rang Dec.

He answered on the first ring, which meant he had not been asleep either.

"It fits," she said.

"I knew it fits. I said at the time. I said, give it here, and the pair of them nearly arrested me." He was already moving; she heard a door, and stairs, and the bakery's shutter rattling under him. "Where are you?"

"The atelier."

"Give me fifteen minutes. Bring your gloves. And your torch. And Inès?"

"What?"

"Wear something dark. That scarf can be seen from the moon."

The wall on the rue du Repos side of Père-Lachaise was not high, by the standards of walls, but Inès had never thought of walls as a category. She thought of them one at a time, and this one was high.

Dec went up it first, using a plane tree and a drainpipe and an iron bracket that had once held a lamp, and sat astride the top in his old leather jacket as if he were on a horse. Then he leaned down and held out his hand.

"Don't look down," he said.

"I'm not looking down. I'm looking at the wall."

"The wall's down."

"A fenébe," said Inès, which was what her father had said whenever the car would not start, and took his hand, and went up, and did not look at anything at all until she was on the other side with her boots in the wet leaves and her heart going like a drum in a closed room.

The cemetery at night was larger than in the day, and the fog had thickened. It lay so close on the paths that their torches made two white rooms around them and nothing else, and the tombs came into those rooms one at a time and went out again behind. Somewhere a long way off a dog barked, and Inès, who was afraid of dogs, decided it was a fox. They found the eighty-ninth division by its painted sign on a post, and the path by the furrows in its gravel, and the grave by the cat. Mimi was still there, a grey shape sitting on the slab in the middle of the chalk circle. She looked at them without surprise, as if they were late.

Inès put on her white cotton gloves and took out the clock face.

It went into the recess as if it had been waiting there all along. It went in so exactly that she felt it before she saw it: a little suck of air as the enamel face met the stone, and the copper back settling into the hollow with the faintest grating sound. But it went in crooked, with the IX not at the top but off to one side, at about the place where ten o'clock would be.

"Wait," said Dec.

He took off his own glove, and put his bare fingertips on the white enamel, very lightly, the way she had seen him touch a lock before he did anything to it. He closed his eyes.

"There's something behind," he said. "The feet. The dial feet. They're not sitting on the bottom, they're in something. Slots. A pair of slots, and they go round." He opened his eyes, delighted, in the middle of a graveyard at midnight. "It's a bayonet. Like a light bulb. Push in and turn. Every clockmaker in Paris looked at those feet, Inès, and said twelve euros."

"Turn it, then."

"Which way?"

"Until the nine is at the top," said Inès. "Where else would it go?"

He turned it. It moved stiffly at first, stone and copper against each other, then more easily, a quarter-inch, half an inch, the careful IX on the white enamel climbing slowly round toward the top of the stone until it sat exactly where the mason's IX was carved above it, one nine over another.

Something clicked inside the granite. Low and heavy, the sound of a big latch dropping.

Under the inscription, below the Latin line, a seam in the black stone that she had taken for a polishing mark became a line, and then a gap, and a granite plate about the size of a hardback book slid forward out of the face of the grave on a spring, a finger's width and then a hand's, and stopped.

Neither of them said anything. Mimi got up off the slab, stretched, and went away into the fog with her tail in the air, as if this part were no concern of hers.

Behind the plate there was a shallow drawer cut into the stone, lined with lead. In it lay a small plastic bag, folded over twice and taped.

Inès took it out with her gloved fingers and held it in the torchlight.

Inside was a brass token the size of an old telephone jeton, worn smooth at the edges by handling. On one side it was stamped with a little skeleton, very finely done, holding up a bell on a rope in one bony hand as if about to ring it, and under the skeleton a number in Roman figures: VIII. With it, a white card, the kind you buy in packs of fifty at a stationer's, and on it, in blue ink, in a round, generous, slightly old-fashioned hand, a telephone number.

+420 2 and eight more digits.

"Four-two-oh," said Dec. "That's Prague."

"Four-two-oh is the Czech Republic," said Inès. "The two after it is Prague."

"That's what I said."

"It's not what you said."

He was grinning at her across the open grave, his hair wet with fog, absurd. She found she was smiling back at him, and stopped.

Somewhere on the gravel path behind them, a foot came down.

Then another. Two sets of footsteps, unhurried, coming toward them up the path out of the fog, and the strange thing about them, the thing that made the hair rise on her arms under her coat, was that they were perfectly together. Two people walking at exactly the same pace, the same length of stride, so that each step on the gravel was two steps landing as one.

Through the white, low down, a torch beam came on, and began to swing slowly from grave to grave toward them.
