The First Two O'Clock

Inès picked up the cap.

Warm inside still, and it smelt of machine oil and of the tobacco he claimed to have given up in 1989. She held it longer than she needed to. Then the keys went to Dec, the thermos went under her arm, and the cap went inside her trench against her ribs, where nothing could happen to it.

"He left them for us," said Dec. "He saw them coming and he put his keys in his hat."

"Or they let him," said Inès. "So that we would come down."

Dec looked at the black door. "Yes," he said. "There's that."

The right key turned up on the third try. His hands were not quite steady. They went down.

The council's bulbs were off. Her phone lit the first vault, the fat column, the window full of earth, and then in the second room there was light ahead of them: candlelight, low and yellow, and a murmur like a church before the service, when everybody is pretending not to talk.

In the third room, grey robes with deep hoods lay folded on a long table in piles, like laundry in a good hotel. A stout man in a camel overcoat was pulling one on over his head, grunting. He surfaced, saw them, and gave them the polite, private nod of a man meeting his neighbours in the queue for confession.

Inès nodded back. She took a robe and put it on. Cedar, and somebody else's cupboard. She pushed her mustard scarf down inside the neck, where it lay warm against her collarbone, and pulled the hood forward until the world was a grey tunnel with candlelight at the end.

Dec's robe stopped at his shins. Jeans and boots stuck out beneath like a confession of their own.

"I look like a monk who grew in the night," he whispered, when the stout man had gone ahead.

"Then stand like a short one."

He bent his knees. It did not help, but it made him look as if he were praying, which was something.

"Inès." His mouth came close to her hood. "Give me your hairpin."

She looked at him.

"The levers in that lock are thin as pages," said Dec. "I spent a whole day in front of it on Friday. My picks are too fat. I knew it then and didn't want to know it." He held out his hand, palm up, like a twin. "It's the only thing I've got that's thin enough."

She took the pin out of her hair, clay still on it, put it in his palm and closed his fingers over it.

"Dec," she said. "Look."

Through the arch, in the chapel, the black cable ran along the foot of the wall in its clips as it had on Saturday morning. At the grey steel machine in the corner it no longer went in. Someone had pulled the plug, coiled the end and taped it up. The chrome microphone stood beside the machine with nothing on the end of its wire.

"Whatever speaks tonight speaks upstairs," said Dec.

"Then she has to be upstairs."

"Then bring her." He checked his watch: a minute to two. A laugh got out of him before he could stop it. "The Orloj says a minute to one. I hope you're listening, Vilém."

The chapel was full.

On Saturday morning she had stopped counting the cups at a hundred. More people than that now. They knelt in rows on the bare swept stone, hooded, grey, shoulder to shoulder, and the candles had warmed the air until it was thick as a sacristy's. At the front, under the painted clock with IX at its top, the nine black chairs stood in their curve.

Seven of them empty.

On chair V sat Renata Hollerová. Grey robe like everybody else's, but cut, and fitted, and her hood was back. The bronze bell hung at her throat on its fine chain. Her ash-blonde hair shone as if it had been polished for the occasion. On her left wrist, where the sleeve fell back, the plain silver watch with no hands.

On chair VIII sat Dagmar.

Somebody had dressed her in grey and put her hair up badly, so that the French twist leant like a tower in a flood. Her lipstick was gone. Her hands lay in her lap, rings and all, palms up, as if somebody had put them there and she had forgotten to take them back. She was looking at the floor in front of the chairs with an expression of polite attention, the way a guest looks at a speech in a language she does not know.

Inès knelt at the end of a row near the side wall, as close to the chairs as she could get without being in the front. Dec knelt behind her. She did not look round at him. In a room full of hoods, the one that turns round is the one people remember.

In the corner furthest from the candles, beyond the trestle table, something lay against the wall under a grey robe thrown over it like a dust sheet. A boot stuck out from under it: a workman's boot, black, laced with string. Over the robe, carefully folded, someone had laid a black Sunday jacket.

Inès closed her eyes for exactly one second. When she opened them, the robe was rising and falling. Slowly. But it was.

On the trestle table, beside the stone urn and the rows of cups, somebody had set up a laptop, open, its screen turned towards the room like a hymn board in a village church. One line of white figures on black: the time, to the second. 01:59:52. Under it, smaller: P-1.

The murmuring stopped. Hollerová rose.

She did not raise her voice. She did not need to. The vault took her low, warm voice and laid it gently on every hood in the room.

Czech first, and Inès understood one word in ten. Then Latin, and Inès understood all of it, and wished she did not.

"Hic est dies longior ceteris."

This is the day that is longer than the others.

"Hora data est. Hora reddetur." The hour was given. The hour shall be given back.

"Reddetur," said the room, in a hundred voices, softly.

On the laptop the figures turned over. 02:00:00. The small line beneath them changed and began to count. It went very fast, faster than Inès could read it, a blur of white, and stopped.

P-1. 46,212.

Far off, in a glass tower in Pankrác, forty-six thousand two hundred and twelve payments had just gone out to whoever they belonged to, as they did every month: pensions, savings, a woman's room in a yellow house on a hill. Nothing stolen yet. That was the next hour's work. The faithful watched the number as if it were a sunrise.

"Prima hora data est," said Hollerová. The first hour is given.

And then the cup.

They went up row by row, without hurry, to the trestle table. Each took a small stone cup from the rows, dipped it in the urn, which a hooded figure held open, drank, and knelt again. Inès went up with her row. She took a cup and dipped it. Grey and thin, the stuff in it, and it smelt of cloves, of the slate roof of a church after rain, of everything she had promised herself in a hotel room in the Jura never to taste again.

She raised it to her hood. Then she walked back past the dark place behind the machine where the drip fell from the crack in the vault, and as she went by she let her hand drop to her side, and the cup emptied itself into the little stone channel in the floor. Plink, said the drip, as if it agreed.

Behind her, Dec did the same, more clumsily, into his own sleeve.

Then, as his row knelt again, he did not kneel. He went on, quite slowly, knees bent, along the side wall past the end of the table. At the grey heap in the corner he crouched and lifted the edge of the robe, and Inès saw Šťastný's face, the moustache, the mouth fallen open, and between his teeth a tongue the grey of a pencil lead. Dec laid the robe back over him as gently as a blanket. Then he went on through the low round arch at the far end of the chapel into the dark, where Šťastný had said the old prison stair went up into the Town Hall. Nobody turned. The cup was working. All around her the faithful were settling onto their heels, heads bowed, hands loose, giving their hour.

Inès watched the black arch until she could not see the shape of him in it any more. Then she looked at her phone, under her sleeve. 02:09.

Up on the tower the Orloj was saying nine minutes past one, and was not in any hurry at all.

At twenty-five past two Renata Hollerová stood, smoothed her robe, said something low in Czech to the hooded figure by the urn, and went out by the way the faithful had come in. She did not look at Dagmar. Nobody looks at a chair.

Inès gave it a slow count of sixty. Then she moved.

On her knees, along the front of the rows, the way she had crossed the crane girder, one hand and then the other. Nobody looked up. The people in that room had drunk the Grey Communion and were looking at the inside of their own heads. She reached the curve of chairs and knelt at the foot of chair VIII, as a woman might kneel to the Mouth of the Brotherhood, and unscrewed the thermos under her robe.

Black and tarry and so strong that the steam alone made her eyes water. Šťastný made coffee the way he spoke English.

"Madame," she whispered, in French. "Madame Vaňková. It's Inès. The restorer. Drink."

Dagmar's eyes came down from the middle distance and rested on her with mild interest.

"It is two hours, twenty-six minutes and ten seconds," she said, in the velvet voice, very quietly and perfectly correctly.

"Yes. Drink this."

She drank. She made a face like a cat given medicine, and Inès made her drink again, and then a third time, and the velvet voice said "Mon Dieu, that man," and something came back into the eyes behind it, like a light in a house where nobody has lived for a week.

"Božena," said Dagmar suddenly. "It is Saturday."

"I went," said Inès. "In your coat. She said I was too tall."

Dagmar looked at her. A tear came out of one eye and ran down through her powder and dropped onto her grey robe, and she did nothing about it, which for Dagmar was terrible.

"Listen to me," said Inès. "There is no time. There is a line on Jan's bell. You told yourself to read it like the clock in Josefov. I did. It is the speaking clock. Gong oznámí, then a hole, then hodin, nula minut, nula sekund. Someone cut the hour away. Twenty years ago. It is ten letters wide. Perhaps nine and a space."

Dagmar's lips moved over it. Gong oznámí. Hodin.

"And you told yourself something else," said Inès. "Remember the day that is longer than the others. Renata just said it. In Latin. Dies longior ceteris. That's from your own rite, isn't it? You read it every year. This is the day that is longer than the others."

"Every year," whispered Dagmar. "On the night the clocks go back."

"Because tonight has twenty-five hours."

She heard herself say it, and the whole week went quiet in her head, the way a room goes quiet when a picture is hung straight. The longer day was not in June. She had looked in the wrong month. It was tonight, the one day in the year a clock could honestly say twenty-five, and no clock ever would. Bohouš had cut Dagmar's hours in 1986 from nought to twenty-three, because a speaking clock never needs a twenty-four, let alone a twenty-five. Whatever said that number tonight could not be a tape. It would have to be the living voice, in the room.

"Madame. Twenty-five. In Czech. How do you say it?"

"Dvacet pět," said Dagmar at once, the way she would have told anyone the time.

Inès counted it on her fingers under the robe. D, V, A, C, E, T. A space. P, Ě, T.

Ten.

She said it, very low, in her terrible Czech, with Hungarian vowels and a French R, word by word, like a woman reading a signature aloud to make sure it was really there.

"Gong oznámí... dvacet pět hodin... nula minut... nula sekund."

Dagmar's hand closed on her wrist. All the rings bit at once.

She did not say anything. Her face was doing something Inès had seen on paintings when the varnish came off: there had been a face underneath all along, and it was coming up into the light.

"Jan," she said. "In my kitchen. On the Wednesday. He wrote it on the back of an envelope and read it to me, and laughed, and said, Dagi, it's the only time I ever got you to say something that isn't true." The tears were coming properly now, both eyes, silent, ruining everything. "Twenty-five o'clock. He said a clock that strikes twenty-five has to stop and think. And I burnt the envelope on the Thursday, in the sink, and I said it to myself in the mirror until I forgot it on purpose." She held Inès's wrist so hard it hurt. "Gong oznámí dvacet pět hodin, nula minut, nula sekund."

It was the voice. Not the slurred voice on the tape in the drip room. The voice that had told a whole country the time for thirty years, low and round and absolutely certain, as if twenty-five o'clock were a fact like rain.

Then Dagmar let go of her wrist and held out her hand, palm up.

Inès looked at it.

"Lipstick," said Dagmar. "You went in my coat. It lives in the right pocket. I know you, Madame Varga. You keep things."

Inès had kept it. She did not know why she had. She took it out of her trench pocket, under the robe, and gave it to her, and Dagmar Vaňková, the Eighth of the Nine, the Mouth, put on bright red lipstick in a cellar full of drugged believers by candlelight, without a mirror, perfectly, and pressed her lips together once.

"So," she said. "Now I can be afraid properly."

On the laptop the figures said 02:41:15. Around them the faithful were beginning to stir, lifting their heads, getting stiffly to their feet, as if some signal Inès had not heard had passed among them. Somewhere at the back a man began to sing, low, and then a woman, and then the whole room, a slow grey hymn with no tune Inès knew, going up into the vault. When the hour comes again, the faithful go up to the square.

They were getting ready to go up.

Inès put her arm under Dagmar's elbow. And the hooded figure at the end of her own row, the one who had knelt there all through the cup, perfectly still, with a full stone cup on the floor in front of him that he had never touched, put back his hood.

Shaven head. Pale, calm, polite face. On the wrist that came out of the grey sleeve, a black leather glove, and above it a watch on the right wrist.

He looked at Inès, and at Dagmar's red mouth, and inclined his head very slightly, as if they had met at a party.

Inès held his eyes. Then she looked across the chapel.

By the low black arch where Dec had gone into the dark half an hour before, a second hooded figure stood quite still against the wall, with its back to the singing. As she watched, a gloved hand rose and pushed the grey hood back.

The same head. The same face.

He did not look at her at all. He turned, unhurried, and went through the arch, and up.
