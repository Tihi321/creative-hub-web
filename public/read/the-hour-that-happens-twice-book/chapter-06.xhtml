The Voice of Time

Inès had known a great many vain people. Restorers meet hardly anyone else: owners, curators, grandsons who want their grandfathers' medals made shinier than God made them. A vain person will shut a door on a stranger. A vain person will not shut it on an admirer.

So when Dagmar Vaňková started to close the tall cream door, Inès said, in French, "Madame, you are the voice of time."

The door stopped.

"Who told you that?"

"An entire country," said Inès. "For thirty years."

Dagmar studied her through the gap. Then the door swung back the width of a hostess.

"Five minutes," she said, in French as round and polished as a doorknob. "Wipe your feet. The Irishman twice. It is seven fifteen and ten seconds."

The flat ran a long way back from the street, room into room, each one a stage set for the same play.

Microphones everywhere. A silver one in a wire cage, twin of the one in the newspaper photograph, stood on a chrome stand by the window like a guest who had arrived early; two more waited in a corner with their cables coiled at their feet. Framed records hung on the walls, and photographs of Dagmar shaking hands with men in suits, cutting a ribbon at a tunnel, a hospital, a supermarket. Over the sofa a large oil portrait showed her at thirty in a dark blue dress, leaning toward a microphone, lips parted, about to say something important about the time. A poor painting, that. Inès noted the bitumen in the shadows, which would crack, and kept it to herself.

White lilies stood in a tall vase on the piano, heavy enough to scent two rooms.

And the clocks. Inès counted them because she could not stop herself: the case clock and the station clock in the passage, the gilt one on the mantelpiece, a carriage clock beside the lilies, a kitchen clock through an open door, a cuckoo clock with its bird shut in, a brass ship's clock, a travelling clock on the bureau, and a black marble one on a bookcase like a small tomb. Nine of them. Every hand on every one stood at nine.

"You haven't restarted them," said Inès.

"Why would I?" Dagmar sat down on the sofa under her own portrait and arranged the dragon across her knee. "I know what time it is. It is seven seventeen and fifty seconds. Sit."

Dec sat carefully on a small gilt chair that complained.

"Tea," said Dagmar, and rang a little bell, and remembered that nobody would come, and made it herself, talking through the kitchen door the whole while. She brought it in glasses in silver holders. Inès watched Dec hold his glass up to the window, turn it, sniff it, dip the tip of his little finger in.

Dagmar watched him too. Something went out of her face.

"You taste them as well," she said quietly. "The cloves."

Dec put the glass down. "Do you?"

"In everything. Since Friday." She lifted her own glass and looked into it. "I woke on Friday morning, here, on this sofa, in my clothes. In my shoes. Every clock in the flat stopped at nine. My tongue was the colour of a pencil, and it stayed that colour until Monday. Everything I drink tastes of cloves and of a wet roof." She drank anyway, like a woman drinking a toast to her enemies. "I have lost a Thursday. The fifteenth. I have looked for it everywhere. It's gone. It is what happens at my age, they will tell you. One misplaces a day."

"It isn't your age," said Inès.

"No," said Dagmar. "It is not my age." She set the glass down in its holder with a click. "And that is all I know. I don't know who you are. I have never heard of you. I have nothing to say about Paris, or about anything. You have had your five minutes and two of mine, and now, if you would be so kind..."

Inès looked at the rings, one on every finger, a garnet, a cameo, a plain gold band on the wrong hand. She looked at the French twist that a woman who had not slept had put up at dawn, and at the eyes, which kept going to the passage and the front door and back.

"Jan Vaněk," said Inès.

Dagmar did not move.

"Père-Lachaise," said Inès. "The eighty-ninth division. Black granite. Deux heures trente," and she said it slowly, in French, as it was cut in the stone, "la seconde fois."

For a moment nothing happened. Then the voice of time put her face in her hands, and her rings clicked together like a handful of keys, and the shoulders under the green silk began to shake.

Dec looked at Inès in a panic. Inès shook her head very slightly, and waited.

When Dagmar took her hands away her lipstick was perfect and her eyes were not.

"I argued with the masons," she said, "for one hour, about a centimetre. The recess had to be a hundred and thirty-eight and a half millimetres, and their drawing said a hundred and twenty-eight and a half, and they thought a centimetre did not matter to a dead man. They thought I was mad. A little old woman with rings, shouting at them in my good French." She looked at Inès. "It fitted?"

"It fitted," said Inès.

"Of course it did. I measured it four times." She took a lace handkerchief from her sleeve and pressed it under each eye, very precisely, so as not to move anything. "You are the woman with the glasses. From the Panthéon. And the Irishman who opened the lock. I saw you in the newspapers in April, and I thought, those two. Those two are fools enough." She sniffed. "I lied to you at the door. I am sorry. I have been the Eighth for thirty-two years. I lie before breakfast."

She told it her own way, which was the way of a woman who had spent a life being listened to: with pauses, with gestures, with the time thrown in whenever she felt the story needed a gong.

In 1994 a charming man had taken her to lunch and told her that her voice was the most trusted thing in the country, more than the President, more than the Church, more than the beer, because when Dagmar Vaňková said it was eight o'clock, nobody in Bohemia checked. A voice like that, he said, was not a voice. It was an office.

"And I said yes," said Dagmar, "because nobody had ever called me an office before." She held up one hand and turned it to the light. The ring on her little finger was a flat oval of silver, and engraved on it, worn almost smooth, was a tiny open mouth and the numeral VIII. "The Eighth. The Mouth. The Voice. Once a year, sometimes twice, I put on a grey robe and went down into a cellar full of candles and important men, and I read out names. The debtors. People who owed time to the Clockless One, and would pay." She shrugged, and the dragon shrugged with her. "I thought it was theatre. I read names, and nothing happened to anybody. Nobody dies of being read."

"Somebody did," said Inès.

The hand came down.

"Jan," said Dagmar. "Yes."

They had been married eleven years and divorced thirty-four, and the divorce, she said, had been the best marriage in Prague. He was an engineer, a quiet man with ink on his cuffs who mended other people's toasters on Sundays. In 1996 he built the time system for PragClear, the new clearing house in the glass tower in Pankrác, which settled the payments for half the banks in the country: every salary, every pension, every rent, passed through Jan's clocks on its way somewhere else.

"He built it to settle in local time," she said, "because the banks wanted it so. He told them it was foolish. Twice a year the clocks change, he said, and once a year an hour happens twice, and a machine that settles in local time will not know which two o'clock it is. They told him to build a lock on it. So he did. Jan's clock would accept no correction, no change of the hour, except from the official voice of time." She put her hand flat on her breast. "Mine. My voice, saying the hour, in the form of the speaking clock. He was very romantic, for an engineer. It was the only love letter he ever wrote me, and it was in a computer."

Last October, PragClear had given Jan a present for his retirement: a week in Paris, a hotel near the Opéra, a dinner. He did not like presents. He took his laptop. And on the night the clocks went back he sat up in his hotel room and watched his old system through the hour from two to three, and then through the hour from two to three again.

"In the second hour," said Dagmar, "a batch of payments that had already gone out went out again. The same names, the same sums. And the copies did not go where the first ones went. They went somewhere else, and by the morning the system had tidied itself and there was nothing to see. A test, Jan said. Somebody was testing his hole in time." She looked at the lilies. "He wrote to me at twenty-one minutes past two. Dagi, they ran it twice. I have the logs. I'll ring you in the morning. At half past two, the second time, he died. In his hotel. Of his heart, they said." Her voice did not shake at all. It went lower, and very clear, the voice for a platform announcement in the rain. "Jan had the heart of an ox. He climbed the Petřín tower on his seventieth birthday and ran down."

"Who gave him the week in Paris?" said Inès.

"The head of PragClear." Dagmar turned the ring on her finger. "Renata Hollerová. The Fifth. The Bell."

Nobody said anything. Down in the street a tram went by, ringing.

"I buried him where he fell," said Dagmar, "so that no one from the bank would bring him a wreath. I had the stone made with his clock in it, and a drawer, because Jan liked a drawer that nobody could find. I put his lucky token in it, which he carried in his pocket for nearly thirty years and never told me who gave it to him, and my number. Our number. The old speaking clock, on a line Jan kept for me, with a little extra sentence on it for the right people." She looked at Dec. "And in April I read about you, and I sent you his clock face, with HUIT on it, because I am the Eighth and you are French. More or less. I sent it by a courier who would not say who sent him." She sighed. "And then I lost my nerve for six months, and rang you on a Wednesday night, and when your machine answered I could not say anything but the time. It's what my mouth does when I'm frightened."

"What happens on Saturday?" said Dec.

Dagmar went to the bureau and came back with a sheaf of paper as thick as a telephone directory, bound with a black clip, and dropped it on the low table in front of them. A list. Names, dates of birth, account numbers, column after column, in small type.

"The Collection of the Doubled Hour," she said. "On Saturday night, when the clocks go back, Renata will run Jan's hole for real. In the first two o'clock, the pensions and the savings go out as they always do. In the second two o'clock, they go out again, and the copies go to the Brotherhood. Thousands of people. And I am to stand in front of the Orloj in my grey robe and read the names, so that it is holy." She turned the pages without looking at them and stopped at one, and put a red fingernail on a line. "Page three hundred and twelve."

Inès read it. Vaňková, Božena. 1953.

"My sister," said Dagmar. "She lives in a home in Žižkov. She thinks I am our mother and the nurses are Germans. Her pension pays for the home." She took the fingernail away. "I am a vain, silly old woman, Madame Varga, and I have done very stupid things in a grey robe. But I do not read out my sister."

"Then don't," said Dec.

"It's not so simple. Jan built a way to stop it. A sentence. A phrase in the form of the speaking clock that only the official voice can say, and when the system hears it, it stops. Everything. An emergency brake." She sat down again, suddenly, as if her strings had been cut. "I knew it on Wednesday. I knew it as well as my own name. And on Thursday I hid it from myself."

"Why?" said Inès.

"Because I know myself," said Dagmar simply, "and I knew they were coming. If they have the phrase, they can take it out of the system. They'd make me tell them, sooner or later, with the cloves. I am not brave. So I made sure I couldn't." She spread her hands, every ring catching the light. "Thursday night, they came. And now I don't know where I put it. It is eight o'clock and three seconds."

Dagmar talked for a long while after that, about Jan, about the masons, about a stationmaster in Brno who had once proposed to her over a loudspeaker, and the tea went cold in its silver holders. Then Inès asked for the diary.

It was in the bureau, a fat appointment book bound in burgundy leather with the year in gold on the spine. Inès turned to Thursday, 15 October.

One line on the page, in blue ink, in a round, generous hand that she had seen before: on a white stationer's card in a plastic bag, in a drawer under a grave in Paris.

Šťastný. Jak židovské hodiny. Ten delší den.

"Your hand," said Inès.

"My hand." Dagmar leaned over and read it, and frowned at it, as if she had found a stranger's handwriting in her own book. "I don't remember writing it."

"What does it say?"

"Šťastný. Happy. Lucky. Jak židovské hodiny. Like the Jewish clock. Ten delší den." She hesitated. "The longer day. The day that is longer."

"Šťastný stands on its own, with a full stop after it," said Inès. "It is not a sentence. Is it a name?"

"It is a word. It is also a name. Half of Bohemia is called something that means happy, and none of them are." Dagmar's mouth tightened. "The only Šťastný I know keeps the clock on the square. A horrible old man. Once, on the radio, he told the whole nation that my twelve o'clock was too slow."

Dec had wandered into the passage with his hands behind his back, so that nobody could accuse him of touching anything. Inès heard him stop by the tall case clock.

"Inès," he said. "This one's wrong."

"They're all wrong."

"No, this one's wrong differently." He opened the glass door of the case with one finger. The pendulum hung dead still, and behind it, wedged upright against the back board where the weights should have swung, was a flat grey cardboard box. He lifted it out. Inside lay a reel of brown tape on a metal spool, and on the box lid, in the same round blue hand, was written: 15. 10. ČTVRTEK.

"Thursday," said Dagmar, in a small voice.

There was a big grey tape machine in the corner of the sitting room, under a cloth like a parrot's cage. Dagmar threaded it without looking and pressed a key.

The spools turned. A hiss, and then her voice came out of the speaker.

Her voice beyond doubt, the same velvet, the same grand vowels. But it was saying nothing. The sounds swelled up out of nowhere and stopped dead, as if each word were being sucked back into the mouth instead of let out of it. Breaths in the wrong places, gulps where there should have been endings. On and on it went, smooth and urgent and meaningless, a sleep-talker's patter in a language never spoken anywhere.

Dagmar stared at the machine with her hand at her throat.

"That is me," she said. "I don't speak that. Nobody speaks that."

Inès listened. Something in it nagged at her, the way a forged signature nagged before she knew what was wrong with it. She made Dagmar play it twice more, and on the third time she shut her eyes, and the tune of it was almost a tune she knew.

"It is eight twenty-two," said Dagmar, "and forty seconds."

The trumpet bell rang at the front door.

At the very same instant, without a hair's breadth of time between them, somebody knocked on the glass of the balcony door behind the sofa.

Dec was nearest the passage. He went to the front door on his toes and put his eye to the spyhole.

"There's a fella," he whispered. "Shaved head. Black overcoat. Grey scarf."

Inès was not looking at him. She was looking at the balcony door, four floors above the street, where a man stood on the other side of the glass with his gloved knuckles still raised from knocking. Shaven head. Black overcoat. Grey scarf.

He looked at her politely. The man Dec had described, down to the knot of the scarf.
