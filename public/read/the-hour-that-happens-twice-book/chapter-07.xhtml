The Keeper of the Orloj

Dec had built thirty-one escape rooms in his life, and in every one of them the way out was the door nobody looked at.

"Is there a back stair?" he said. "Old houses like this always had one. For the coal. For the maids."

"There is a stair for the coal and the maids," said Dagmar, in the voice of a woman asked whether she owned a broom. "I have not used it since 1989."

"Grand. It'll be glad of the company."

Behind the sofa, the man on the balcony set something small and silver against the glass by the handle. It made a thin singing noise, a wet finger round the rim of a wine glass. He was cutting a circle. He did not hurry.

Inès had the reel box under one arm already and the burgundy diary under the other. Dagmar stood up at last and went the wrong way, toward the bedroom.

"I am going to put on my coat," she said over her shoulder. "I have been taken from this flat once already, Mr Hartley, and I fear I went without my lipstick. I will not go a second time like a woman fetching milk."

At the balcony there was a small, clean click, a biscuit snapping.

She came back in a camel coat with a fox-fur collar, buttoned to the throat over the green dragon, and walked straight into the kitchen. There she took the lid off a copper saucepan hanging on the wall and did her mouth in it, two strokes to each lip, without a tremor. In the sitting room a gloved hand came through a neat round hole in the glass and felt for the handle.

"It is eight twenty-four," said Dagmar, hanging the lid back on its hook, "and ten seconds. The key is in the sugar."

The key was in the sugar. The door to the service stair stood behind a curtain printed with lemons. Beyond it a tight brown spiral went down into a smell of coal dust and cabbage. Dec locked the kitchen door from the stair side and dropped the key down the gap between the treads, where it went ringing a long way down.

"That was my only key," said Dagmar from below.

"I'll make you ten. Mind the step."

They went down fast, Dagmar holding her coat like a duchess leaving a burning opera. Above them the kitchen handle went down and came up again, very gently, with no rattling and no swearing. A man who swore at a locked door was a man you could do business with.

Then light feet crossed the kitchen overhead, going away.

"He's going round," said Dec. "Front stair, street door, into the yard. So we don't stay in the yard."

"There is nothing but the yard," said Dagmar. "And the door into Slezská, which has been locked since the Velvet Revolution. Nobody has the key."

"I'll not need it."

The stair let them out into a courtyard the size of a squash court, all drainpipes and bins, with a dead chestnut tree in the middle. A dark carriage arch led back to the street. In the opposite wall hung a small brown plank door with a big black box lock and no handle.

Dec was on his knees in front of it with the cutlery unrolled before the women had crossed the yard. Four-lever box lock, the kind his father called a church lock, because every vestry in Connacht had one. He blew the dust out and put in the wrench and the long hook. The first lever lifted, and the second, and the third stuck, as levers always did, out of spite.

"Dec," said Inès quietly.

A man had stepped into the carriage arch off the pavement, a black shape with a pale head, and he came down the tunnel without any hurry at all, swinging one arm, as if he had bread under the other.

"Talk to me," said Dec.

"Eight twenty-five and thirty seconds," said Dagmar.

"Not you, Inès."

"He is halfway," said Inès. "He has taken off one glove."

The third lever came up, and the fourth went up with it, meek as a curate. The bolt slid back with a clunk Dec felt in his knees. He pushed both women through and turned in the doorway. The man had come out into the courtyard and stopped by the dead tree. He tilted his head and regarded Dec with mild approval, as if Dec were a dog that had done something clever with a ball.

Dec shut the door in his face, put the hook back in, and locked it again.

"What are you doing?" said Inès.

"A good locksmith leaves a door the way he found it," said Dec, and ran.

They fell onto a tram on the corner. Every old woman on it turned round when Dagmar remarked, to nobody, that it was eight twenty-seven.

"You can't do that," said Dec. "You're the most famous voice in the country."

"Then I will say it softly." She settled her collar. "The brothers Dvořák. Renata's. They do not speak. Renata says it is a vow. I think nobody has ever asked them anything." She watched the lime trees go by. "I was at their confirmation. When the bishop came to the first one, the other one said amen."

Inès had the reel box on her lap and the diary open on top of it. "On the day you knew they were coming," she said, "you wrote down one name. Šťastný."

"I may also have written to the Pope. I was not myself."

"Where does he live?"

"In the clock," said Dagmar, with deep contempt. "Where else?"

Round the side of the Old Town Hall tower, away from the crowd, was a door so narrow Dec would have taken it for a cupboard. A card over its bell push said several fierce things in Czech and German, and one in English: NO TOURIST. NO.

Dagmar leaned on it with her whole thumb. Nothing happened.

"He hears it," she said. "He hears everything in this tower. He is like a bat, only less charming."

She pushed open the letterbox with one red fingernail, put her mouth to it, and let out the other voice, the one that had told a whole country the time for thirty years. Slow and enormous, and Dec felt it in the backs of his knees.

"Přesný čas. Devět hodin, deset minut, nula sekund."

Feet came down a stair at a furious rate, and the narrow door was flung open by a short, barrel-chested old man in blue overalls and a flat cap, with a white moustache like a walrus that had been through a hedge. He thrust an open pocket watch under Dagmar's nose with an oily hand.

"Čtrnáct!" he shouted. "Fourteen minutes! Always slow! Thirty years slow!"

"Your watch is fast, Vilém."

"Four minutes! On purpose! So I am never late for my clock!"

"Then you are four minutes early for everything else, and it has made you unbearable." Dagmar stepped past him as if he were a coat stand. "Somebody is trying to kill me, and you are the only person I wrote down. These two are with me."

Vilém Šťastný looked Dec over from his boots to his hair and back again, and seemed to be checking his teeth without touching them.

"Turist," he said.

"Locksmith," said Dec.

"Ha," said Šťastný, which seemed to be a whole sentence, and went back up the stair and left the door open.

The stair was stone, worn into dips by six hundred years of feet, and so narrow that Dec went up it sideways. At the top was a low room that was all clock. Wheels of black iron and yellow brass, some as big as a cartwheel and some as small as a coin, stood in frames one behind another, and the sound of them was not one tick but a hundred, a room full of people breathing in their sleep. It smelt of oil and cold iron. Beyond the frames were a small square window, a second door and an old stair climbing into the dark.

Dec stood in the doorway with his mouth open, and knew it, and could do nothing about it. Inès set the reel box and the diary on the one clean corner of a bench.

Šťastný slapped something heavy down on a bench. It was an iron padlock the size of two fists, shaped like a little chest, with a riveted hasp and a rosette of iron leaves on its face, and no keyhole at all.

"Tower door. Sixteen hundred, something," he said. "Key, I have. The council's man tries two days. Then with a hammer." He held up the pocket watch. "Five minutes."

A puzzle lock, then. The kind made in Nuremberg for rich men's chests, a lock that lied to you on purpose. Dec tapped the rivets along the hasp with a fingernail, listening. The third from the left sounded a hair duller than its brothers. He pushed it in a millimetre and turned the rosette at the same time, and it swung round on a pin and showed him, under its leaves, a small square keyhole with one long lever inside. The lever's spring argued with him and lost. The hasp jumped out of the body with a sound like a cough.

"Three minutes and some," said Šťastný. His moustache moved as if something underneath it had changed its mind. "Ein Schlosser," he told the clock, in German. "Not a turist. Sit. Don't touch."

Dec sat on a crate, and because the moment seemed to ask for it, he held out the brass token on his palm.

Šťastný took it the way a man takes his own wallet back off the floor. He rubbed the skeleton with his thumb, looked at the VIII on the back, and sat down heavily on the bottom step.

"Vaněk," he said.

"It was in his grave," said Dec. "In Paris."

"I know he is dead. I am old, not stupid." He held it up. "Brass from the old wheel of the moon, from 1865, worn out. I cut twelve tokens from it, with Death on them, for people who understand clocks. In forty years I give eight! Everybody who comes up my stair asks, can you make it go faster? Vaněk sat on this step one hour in 1996 and said nothing. Then he said, the wheel of the moon makes a different sound. It was dying. Nobody else heard." He pressed the token back into Dec's hand and closed Dec's fingers over it with his own, which were rough and warm. "Number eight. A keeper's token. For the man who understood clocks."

By the window, Dagmar had gone very still. "He carried it thirty years," she said. "He never told me who gave it to him."

"He was a man who did not talk," said Šťastný. "Unlike some."

Dagmar opened her mouth, looked at the token in Dec's fist, and for once shut it again.

He showed them the clock because he could not help it. Dec had known men like him in Galway, who would curse you down the street and then spend four hours showing you a carburettor.

From a slit behind the great dial he named it all with an oily finger: the earth in the middle, blue for day and black for night, the sun on the gold hand, the moon a little ball, half silver and half black, that turned itself over as it went. Below hung the calendar plate, with the months painted as peasants killing pigs.

"And the gold numbers round the outside," said Inès. "The Gothic ones."

Šťastný looked at her with something close to respect. "Old Czech time. Hours from sunset. At sunset, twenty-four, then one, two, three in the dark. Nobody uses it for three hundred years." He shrugged. "My clock uses it."

On the hour, by day only, the apostles went past the windows above the dial, forgiving nobody, and Death rang his bell. At night the apostles slept. "Like Christians," said Šťastný. "Like everybody."

Then he took them across the clockwork room, and his face changed.

Against the back wall stood a cabinet the size of a fridge, cast in bronze going green at the edges and bolted to the old stone with eight bright steel bolts. From its foot a black cable as thick as Dec's wrist went straight down through a raw round hole in the floor.

"In April," said Šťastný. "The sponzoři. Nadace PragClear, the foundation. They pay for paint for my apostles, so the council gives them what they want. Six hundred years, no hole in my floor! For the electric, they say. I ask for the key. No key."

The lock sat in the middle of the door. Its escutcheon was a little bronze bell the size of an egg, hanging mouth down, with a mark cast in its shoulder: a capital H inside the outline of a bell. Dec lay on his back and shone his penlight up into it. Where the clapper should have been was a curved keyway, and beyond it more levers than he had ever seen in one lock, thin and close as the pages of a book.

He touched one with the tip of the thin hook.

It rang: a tiny clear note, high and pure, that hung in the bronze and faded.

"At night," said Šťastný, "I hear it. Sometimes. It sings to itself."

Dec drew the hook out very carefully. "I'd want a day with that," he said. "And a lot of tea. And I wouldn't want to be wrong."

"Also this," said Šťastný.

Near the window turned an iron drum set with pegs, the one that set the apostles going, too slowly to see. Somebody had clamped a collar of new brass round its end with a single extra peg, and screwed to the beam beside it a grey plastic box with a red light and a small steel arm, wired back to the cabinet.

"For two in the night," said Šťastný. "The council says, a gift from the sponsors, for a special event." His moustache bristled. "My apostles sleep at night."

"Which two?" said Inès. "On Saturday there will be two of them."

The old man turned and smiled at her for the first time. He had been waiting forty years for somebody to ask him exactly that.

"Not for my clock," he said. "My clock does not do summer time."

The Orloj, he told them with his hands, had kept Central European winter time since 1912. It did not go forward in March or back in October, because it could not: to make it jump an hour you would throw out every dial on its face.

"So since March my clock is one hour behind the whole city." He flicked his watch open and shut. "Except me. Four minutes."

"I saw it at dawn," said Dec. "It struck six and my watch said seven. I thought it was slow."

"Slow!" Šťastný went purple. "It is the city that ran away. On Saturday night, at three o'clock, everybody puts their clocks back to two, and the city comes back to my clock. My clock does not move one second. It never has to say sorry." He jabbed a finger at the grey box. "So. The first two o'clock, my clock says one. The second time, it says two. They set this thing for my clock's two. Ta druhá. The second one."

"La seconde fois," said Inès softly.

Dagmar said nothing. She was looking at the bronze cabinet as if it were somebody she had once been married to.

Above their heads the works gave a deep iron shudder, the rod to the skeleton's arm jerked, and through the stone came a small bell and the slow rumble of the apostles setting out.

"Nine," said Šťastný, with satisfaction.

Dec looked at his watch. It said 10:00.

When the rumble had died, Šťastný wiped his hands on a rag and said, without looking at anybody, that Dagmar would stay with him. His flat was in Kožná, the lane behind the square: third floor, no lift, a sofa, and no telephone that he would admit to.

"You have a telephone, Vilém," said Dagmar. "Your niece gave you one for Christmas."

"It lives in the bread bin."

Dec wrote his number on an atelier card. Šťastný looked at it as if it were a parking fine and pocketed it. Inès gathered up the reel box and the diary.

"I will not ring," he said.

Dec went to the small square window. The glass was old and greenish, and it made the Old Town Square look like the bottom of a pond. Somewhere beneath him the cockerel crowed, the crowd gave its little sigh, and it began to come apart, drifting off toward the cafés.

All but two.

They stood at opposite ends of the square in black overcoats and grey scarves, one by the dark church with the two spires and one by the big statue. Dec only saw them because they were the only two things on the square that were not going anywhere.

As he watched, they checked their watches together, the one by the church on his left wrist and the one by the statue on his right, as if the square between them were a mirror.

Then both of them lifted their faces at the same moment, as if one rope had pulled them, and looked straight up at Dec's small green window.
