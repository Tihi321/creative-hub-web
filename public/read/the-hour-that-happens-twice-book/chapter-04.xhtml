Kessler's Word

At La Santé they took everything from her except the envelope.

Her phone went into a grey locker. Then her keys. Then her scarf, because a scarf that long will hang a person, and the scale card from her coat pocket, which the guard turned over twice, could not classify, and locked away to be safe. The metal detector had nothing to say about her. Everything else in the building had a view.

The guard on the other side opened the envelope and took out the two photographs she had printed that afternoon at the copy shop on the rue des Archives. He held the first at arm's length.

"C'est un pigeon," he said.

"Yes."

He turned it over in case there was something on the back, frowned at the second one, and handed the envelope back with the face of a man who has decided that whatever this is, it is somebody else's evening.

Dec had not been asked for. He had been left on the boulevard Arago under the plane trees with strict instructions to drink nothing, eat nothing and touch nothing, and he had promised all three with the face of a man already looking for a café.

Maître Carrère was waiting at the end of the corridor: about sixty, navy suit gone shiny at the elbows, grey hair pinned up with a pencil she had evidently been allowed to keep, a yellow legal pad under her arm.

"Madame Varga." Her handshake was brief and dry. "In thirty years I have never got a magistrate to sign a visit in two hours. She did it on the telephone, in the car, collecting her children. Nobody wanted to wait and see whether he changed his mind." She pushed open a door. "I stay. The guard stays. He writes on my pad and I keep the pages. You may show him what you've brought. You may not hand him anything. Do you understand?"

"Yes."

The visiting room was white and too warm. A table bolted to the floor, three plastic chairs, two on one side and one on the other, windows so high up the wall that all they showed was a square of evening going violet. Two doors, one at each end: the one Inès had come in by, and one at the far end with a small wired window in it, for the other side.

Maître Carrère sat beside her and laid the yellow pad on the table between them and the empty chair, a black biro across it, square to the edge.

The far door opened and Brother Kessler came in.

He had to turn his shoulders to get through it. Inès remembered him very big, on a roof on the quai d'Orléans with rain running off his bald head, and at the top of a spiral stair in the Panthéon, filling it. Prison had not made him smaller. It had made him paler, the colour of bread dough, and somebody had given him a grey sweatshirt one size too small, so that his wrists came out of the cuffs like the ends of logs. On the side of his thick neck the little blue cuckoo clock sat where it had always sat, its tiny doors shut.

He sat down. The chair made a sound. He put his two hands flat on the table, looked at Inès, and nodded once, as if confirming an appointment.

"Good evening," said Inès.

No answer. She had not expected one.

She opened the envelope and took out the first photograph and laid it on the table in front of him, turned the right way for him to see.

An ordinary Paris pigeon on a windowsill, slate-grey, a black bar on each wing, a ring of green and purple at its throat that the camera had caught in a lucky light. On its right leg, a narrow white plastic band.

"It lived," said Inès.

Kessler looked at it.

"In April, when they took you out of the Panthéon, you were still holding it. A gendarme took it from you and put it in his cap. It went to the wildlife hospital at Maisons-Alfort, and I telephoned them every Monday until they were tired of me. One wing was hurt in the netting, not broken." She kept her eyes on the photograph. Beside her she could feel Maître Carrère looking at her. "They let it go on the ninth of May, from the roof, and sent me this. The white band is theirs."

Kessler looked at it so long that the guard by the door shifted his weight from one foot to the other and back again. He did not touch the photograph. One enormous forefinger moved toward it across the table and stopped a centimetre short, at the edge of the white border, and stayed there. The cuckoo clock on his neck rose and fell as he swallowed.

Inès waited. She was good at waiting. She had once waited four hours for a varnish to tell her whether it was eighteenth century or a very good liar.

At last Kessler drew his hand back, pulled the yellow pad toward him, and uncapped the biro.

He wrote slowly, in capitals, the way a child copies from a blackboard, each letter a separate object to be set down carefully in the right place. His hand covered most of the pad. When he lifted it, there was one word.

ZWEI

Inès read it the way she read any hand: the hand first, the word second. The Z had a short bar through its middle, the way children are taught to cross it in German schools and in Alsace, so that nobody would mistake it for a 2. He had pressed hard enough to emboss the word on the sheet underneath. No hesitation anywhere in it. He had known the word since before he sat down, possibly since April.

"Zwei," she said. "Two."

Kessler looked at her.

"Two what?"

He wrote nothing. He turned his great head, slowly, and looked at the door he had come in by, at the far end of the room; then, just as slowly, at the door behind Inès, the one she had come in by; then back at her. His face did not change at all.

"Two doors?" said Maître Carrère, pen ready.

Kessler put the cap back on the biro.

Inès took out the second photograph and laid it beside the first. The brass token on her grey scale card, photographed flat under the atelier lamp: the worn rim, the VIII, the small bony skeleton with its jaw open, holding up a bell on a rope.

Kessler's face changed then. Not much. Something closed in it, the way a shop shutter comes down halfway at lunchtime.

He reached out and put the tip of his forefinger on the skeleton with the bell. Held it there. Then he lifted the finger and, before the guard could move, laid it on the back of Inès's hand, on the table, very lightly, for about one second. Warm and heavy, and no threat in it. She knew the difference. It was the touch you give someone at the edge of a platform when the train is coming in.

"Pas de contact," said the guard, stepping forward.

Kessler had already put his hand back flat beside the other one, as if nothing had happened.

Inès did not move her hand. She found she did not want to.

"Prague," she said.

Kessler closed his eyes.

Not tiredness. The way a man shuts his eyes when he is told the name of a hospital. He sat there, enormous, eyes shut, hands flat on the table, and breathed.

"Monsieur Kessler," said Maître Carrère.

Nothing.

Inès asked him who had given Vaněk the token, and what was in Prague, and whether he knew a woman called Dagmar Vaňková, and what the Brotherhood wanted with the hour that happened twice. She asked it quietly and in order, the way she would have asked a painting, and he gave her exactly what a painting gives you when you talk to it. After ten minutes he opened his eyes, looked once more at the photograph of the pigeon, then at the guard, who understood him and opened the far door.

At the door he stopped and turned his head toward Maître Carrère.

"He wants the pigeon," said Inès.

"He may have a photograph," said Maître Carrère, "once it has been examined." She sounded faintly astonished to hear herself say it. "I'll see to it."

Kessler nodded to her, and to Inès, and ducked his head under the frame and was gone.

Maître Carrère tore off the sheet, and the one beneath it where the word had come through in ridges.

"Six months," she said. "Four letters." She slid both sheets into a plastic sleeve. "I shall frame them."

Dec was on a bench on the boulevard Arago with his satchel on his knees and a paper cup of coffee beside him that he had very obviously not touched. Dark now, and starting to rain, and the high blank wall of the prison ran away under the plane trees with a lamp every twenty metres, like bored sentries.

"I bought it so I'd have something to look at," he said, before she could ask. "The man in the tabac had a beard. I didn't like his beard." He stood up. "Well?"

She took the pencil out of his satchel, turned his hand over and wrote it on his palm in capitals, with the bar through the Z, exactly as Kessler had made it.

Dec looked at his palm for some time with the rain falling on it.

"Zwei," he said.

"Two."

"I know zwei. I've been to Munich." He closed his hand on it. "Six months of silence and the man writes two? He didn't even give it a full stop."

"He is not a man who wastes full stops."

They walked toward Denfert-Rochereau, because Dec walked when he thought, and if she did not go with him he would walk off still talking.

"Right," said Dec. "Two. Two what? It could be two o'clock. It could be two of something, or the second of something, or the second time." He was counting them off on his fingers. "The grave. La seconde fois. That's a two. Half past two, that's another two."

"Yes."

"Or the two in the cemetery. With the shoes. The two lads walking in step." He looked at her. "That was a two."

"Everybody who walks in a cemetery at midnight walks in twos," said Inès. "Nobody goes over that wall alone. We didn't. And Kessler has not seen a cemetery since April. He has seen a yard with pigeons in it."

"Fair," said Dec, less happily. "Or it's a beer. There's a beer called Zwei, I'd swear to it. Or a band. There's definitely a band."

"He did not break six months' silence for a band, Dec."

"You don't know what he's into."

She stopped under a lamp, so that he had to stop too, pushed her glasses up with one knuckle, and laid it out for him the way she would lay out the layers of a painting, bottom to top.

"Jan Vaněk dies in the hour that happens twice, and someone cuts the second time on his grave. The voice on the telephone says come. A girl puts the Grey Communion in your coffee to stop you coming. And on Sunday morning, at three o'clock, the clocks go back, and the hour from two to three happens again, in Paris and in Prague and everywhere else." She held up two fingers. "Two o'clock. The second two o'clock. That is when it happens. Whatever it is."

Dec looked at her two fingers, then back at the long wall where Kessler was being walked to his cell with a pigeon on his mind.

"He shut his eyes when you said Prague?"

"Yes."

"Like he was praying?"

"Like he was sorry for me," said Inès.

Dec said nothing to that, which in six months she had known happen perhaps three times.

"Two o'clock," he said at last. "The second time round. Sunday."

"Yes."

"Grand," said Dec. "So now we know when. We just don't know what, or where, or who, or why." He put his hands in his jacket pockets. "We're flying."

The call came at twenty past nine, in the atelier, while Dec was eating a ham baguette at the bench and examining the inside of the ham.

Her phone said Numéro masqué. At that hour it was either her mother on somebody else's phone or a man selling heat pumps, and she was in the mood to be rude to one of them.

"Oui?"

"Madame Varga." A man's voice, unhurried, warm, every consonant oiled and set down in its place. "Good evening. I do hope I'm not interrupting your dinner. Auguste de Vaulmont."

Inès said nothing. She put the phone on the bench and touched the speaker, and Dec stopped chewing with the baguette in the air.

"You need not say anything," said Vaulmont pleasantly. "I know you are a woman who prefers to listen. I'm exactly where the court would like me to be. It is raining in the Sologne. My magistrate rang this morning at nine, as he does every Tuesday, and I told him so. We spoke of mushrooms." A small sound, perhaps a glass set down on wood. "I telephoned only to give you some advice, as a friend of the arts."

"You are not a friend of anything," said Inès.

"Ah. She speaks." He sounded pleased, the way a host sounds when a shy guest finally takes a canapé. "My advice is very simple. Don't travel this week. Prague is lovely in October, I know, the light on the river, the lamps, but it's crowded, and the weekend will be very busy. Stay in Paris. Restore something." A pause. "And remember that on Sunday morning everybody is given an extra hour of sleep. It's the one gift the State makes us without asking anything in return. I would take it, if I were you. Sleep through it. Most people do."

Dec put the baguette down very carefully on the paper, as if it might go off.

"How is your mother?" said Vaulmont. "Hélène. Still in Lyon, I think, at the top of the Croix-Rousse. The third floor, with the green shutters and the geraniums she never remembers to water. Give her my regards. Tell her she has a daughter to be proud of."

On the shelf above the bench, in its nest of grey tissue, the HUIT clock face looked at nothing. Beside it, her father looked out of his photograph from under his good Hungarian hat.

Inès picked up the phone, took it off the speaker and held it to her mouth, and said, clearly and not loudly, one sentence in Hungarian.

"Ne vegye a szájára az anyám nevét."

Then she hung up.

She put the phone face down. Her hand was steady, which surprised her, and she put it in her lap where she could not see it.

"What did you say to him?" said Dec.

"I told him not to put my mother's name in his mouth."

"That's very good. Is that a saying?"

"It is now."

She rang her mother. Hélène answered on the second ring, delighted, and talked for six minutes about the neighbour's terrier, which had eaten a glove, while Inès stood with her forehead on the cold glass of the skylight and said oui, Maman in the right places. At the end Hélène asked whether the Irishman was eating.

Inès looked at Dec, who was holding the ham up to the lamp.

"He is examining his food," she said. "Lock the door tonight, Maman. Both locks. Yes. Because I asked you to."

Then she sat down at her laptop without a word and opened the railway site. She found the Wednesday night train and two berths in a compartment for four, typed in her card number from memory, and pressed the button hard enough to hurt it.

"Inès," said Dec.

"Do not tell me not to go. He told me not to go. That is the only reason I need."

"I wasn't going to tell you not to go." He was looking at his own phone, with an expression she had learned to distrust, the expression of a man who has been useful without permission. "I was going to tell you I've already booked us."

She looked at the screen, where the railway was thanking her for her purchase.

"Booked what?"

"The night bus. Wednesday, from Bercy. Leaves at six in the evening, gets into Prague tomorrow-and-a-half in the morning." He turned the screen round to show her two seats, side by side, in a little diagram of a coach. "Forty-one euros each. Your train's three hundred and something and you have to change twice and sleep with a German."

"I would not have to sleep with a German."

"In the same room as a German. On a shelf."

"The train has a restaurant car, Dec. It has a bed. It goes on rails."

"The bus has wheels," said Dec, with dignity. "And it has a toilet and a film."
