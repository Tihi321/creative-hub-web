The Night Bus

Inès argued for the train until one o'clock on Wednesday afternoon. The bed. The rails. The dignity of arriving in a foreign capital having slept lying down. She was winning, too, right up until Dec opened the atelier's bank account on her own laptop and turned it round to face her. Rent was due on the first. The train cost eight times the bus.

Inès studied the screen. Then she said something in Hungarian to the skylight and cancelled the train. The refund came back nineteen euros short, and she informed him, with deep satisfaction, that he would be hearing about those nineteen euros for the rest of his life.

She lost on price and won everything else. The window seat, for a start. She chose the food: two rye sandwiches from the good bakery on the rue des Rosiers, taped shut so that Dec could see nobody had opened them. And she took the armrest, in advance and in writing, on the back of an invoice.

The coach station at Bercy was a concrete hall under a road. Half past five on Wednesday evening, and it was full of people sitting on their luggage eating crisps: students, grandmothers, a hen party from Lille in pink sashes, a monk. Beyond the glass, coaches backed in and out of the bays with their hold doors open like mouths, and the whole place smelt of diesel and hot dogs.

Dec found their bay on the screen. PRAHA 18:00. Quai 14. He was reading it a second time, out of pleasure, when every screen in the hall went black at once.

Then they all came back, and every one of them said the same thing.

21:00.

No departures, no bays, just the time in big white figures, even over the door of the gents.

The hen party cheered. By Dec's watch it was 17:38. Inès was looking at the screens with her mouth pressed flat.

"Count," she said.

He counted, though he knew the answer before he started, and watched the seconds go by on his own wrist. At 17:47 exactly, the screens went black again and came back with the departures on them, as if nothing had happened.

Nine minutes.

"It's a joke," said Dec. "They're making a joke."

"It is a message," said Inès. "It says, we know which bus."

The coach to Prague stood at the far end with its engine running, and its driver, a Czech with a grey moustache and a thermos, checked tickets at the door with the air of a man who had seen everything twice. Dec hung his leather jacket over seats 23 and 24 to keep them from a Belgian who was eyeing them, and came back down in his jumper for Inès's suitcase.

"Water," said Inès. "There's a machine. Don't let the Belgian sit on your jacket."

"I'll defend it with my life."

She went back into the hall. Dec carried the suitcase round to the open hold on the far side of the coach.

A man in a high-visibility vest was stacking bags there. About fifty, with a neat grey beard and the slow, patient hands of somebody paid by the night and not by the bag, and he nodded at the suitcase and then at the back of the hold, which went in under the coach a long way, dark and low.

"Prague? At the back, please," he said, in good English. "The front is for Strasbourg. They get off first."

That was reasonable. Most dangerous things were. The suitcase stuck against something, and Dec got one knee up on the lip of the hold to shove it further, and then the other knee, and he was in up to his waist in somebody's rucksacks when a voice behind him said, brightly, in polished English:

"Oh, that's lovely. Thanks ever so much."

He knew the voice. He twisted round.

She stood beside the man in the vest, just out of the light of the bay. Late twenties, neat as a pin, and very pretty in the way a well-made envelope is pretty, with her chestnut hair in a low knot and a cream trench coat belted tight. She had a phone in each hand, one at her ear and the other pointed at him, its little white light on.

Mademoiselle Ferrand smiled at him, the way she had smiled at him in the café.

"Bye-bye now," she said.

The man in the vest shoved him in the backside with a flat, practised hand, and Dec went head first into the rucksacks. Behind him the door of the hold came down. As it fell he saw the man's wrist go by, and on it, under the cuff of the vest, a plain silver watch with a white face and no hands at all.

Then it was dark, and a latch went home with a clunk he felt in his teeth.

He shouted, of course. He hammered on the door with both fists and shouted Inès and Help and, for some reason, Garda, which was not even the right country. Then, at 18:00 exactly by the glowing hands of his watch, something hissed, and the whole floor of the world lurched and began to move, and he was lying on his back on a Belgian's golf bag in total darkness, going to Prague.

His phone was in the pocket of his jacket, on seat 23, where he had left it to guard the seats from the Belgian.

Then, because his father had always said that a locked-in man is only a man who has not yet looked at the lock, he found the penlight he kept with the cutlery in his satchel, and turned it on.

The hold was the size of a coffin with ambitions, full of suitcases, a golf bag and a child's bicycle in bubble wrap. He crawled to it with the light.

The latch was a simple one. Through a gap at the bottom of the inner panel, with his cheek on the floor, he could see a steel rod that ran from the outside handle to a hook, and the hook sitting on its catch. Lifting the rod would take the hook off the catch. A lock made to stop the door flying open at speed, not to keep anybody in.

He tried it at once. With the longest hook from the cutlery he could just reach the rod, and he lifted it a little. The whole door rattled in its frame, and through the steel he heard the hiss of wet tyres at a hundred and ten kilometres an hour.

He put the hook away. Opening the door now would be one way of getting out of the hold, but not one he would be around to enjoy.

So he made a plan: get the rod ready, wait for the bus to stop, lift it and roll out. He took off his boots, pulled out both laces and knotted them together, and lay on his stomach with the penlight in his teeth, feeding a loop of lace through the gap and over the end of the rod with the long hook, the way you thread a needle with a bad hand. It took him an hour and ten minutes. He timed it. When it was done he could lie with the end of the lace round his finger and feel the rod at the other end of it, like a fish.

Then he waited, and wondered whether Inès thought he had been murdered or had simply gone home, and which would make her angrier. He ate one of the rye sandwiches she had made him carry, and decided not to tell her it was hers.

At 03:10 the engine went down through its gears, and the coach swung, and swung again, and stopped, and the engine died.

Dec pulled the lace.

The rod lifted, the hook came off its catch, the door swung out into cold air and orange light, and Dec rolled out of the hold of the coach to Prague and landed on his back in a puddle on the tarmac of a German motorway service area, in his socks.

Four people looked down at him.

A tall father, a tall mother and two tall blond children, all four in matching orange cycling clothes and helmets, stood round him in a ring holding paper cups of hot chocolate. Behind them, under the floodlights, was a camper van with four bicycles on its back, and beyond that the motorway went past in the dark with a sound like the sea.

"Goedemorgen," said the father, after some thought.

"Morning," said Dec. "Where am I?"

"Near Nuremberg," said the mother. She looked at his socks. "Why were you in the bus?"

"I wasn't in the bus," said Dec. "I was under it."

The younger child offered him her hot chocolate. He took a sip before he remembered to sniff it, and it tasted of nothing but hot chocolate, and he could have kissed her.

Then the coach's engine started.

Dec was up and running before he had decided to. The coach was already swinging out toward the slip road, with his boots in the hold and the hold door flapping open on its hinge like a dog's ear. As Dec came round the back of the coach the Dutch father overtook him in his cycling shoes, clattering like a pony, and slammed the hold door shut with the flat of his hand and gave Dec a thumbs-up without breaking stride.

Dec ran along the side of the coach in his wet socks, hammering on the windows. At the front, through the glass of the door, he could see the driver, and beside him in the aisle, one hand on the rail, Inès, talking.

He could not hear what she was saying, but he could see it: one thing with her finger pointing at the road, another with her other hand, and a third, quite slowly, close to the driver's ear, while the driver looked at her the way a man looks at weather.

Then the driver looked out of his window, and saw Dec, and the brakes hissed.

The door folded open. Dec climbed the steps on legs that had been folded up for nine hours, and stood at the top, dripping, with a bootlace still knotted round his finger.

"Your wife," said the driver, in English, with feeling. "French. German. English. Nine hours. Stop the bus. I say, madam, I cannot stop on autobahn for a man who is in Paris."

"She's not my wife," said Dec.

"Then why she shouts?" said the driver, and shut the door, and drove on.

Inès stood in the aisle with his jacket over her arm. Her glasses had slipped down her nose and she did not push them up.

"You are late," she said.

"I was in the hold."

"I assumed so. Eventually." She held out his jacket. "Your phone rang nine times, in your pocket, on the seat beside me. It was me ringing it. I was not worried."

"You told the driver in three languages to stop the bus."

"I told him in three languages that he was a bad driver," said Inès. "It is not the same. Where are your boots?"

"Under us."

"And my suitcase?"

"Also under us. With a Belgian's golf clubs." He sat down in seat 24. "Ferrand," he said. "She was there. I saw her. Inès, I saw her face this time. And a fella with no hands on his watch, in a yellow vest, putting the bags in."

Inès sat down by the window.

"Did you eat my sandwich?" she said.

"I did, yes."

She nodded, and put her head against the glass, and to his great surprise, after about a minute, her hand came over and lay on the armrest that was hers in writing, next to his arm, and stayed there all the way to the Czech border.

The coach came into Prague at 06:40, in the last of the dark and a fine cold rain, and Dec got his boots back and laced them with one bootlace cut in two by Inès's scalpel.

They took a red and cream tram two stops, ringing its bell at corners, and walked the rest. Inès had the address on her phone: a street in Vinohrady, found at two on Wednesday morning in the online newsletter of a residents' association, which thanked paní Vaňková for reading the Christmas poems so beautifully in the courtyard.

"We'll go to the square first," said Dec. "Just to see it. It's on the way."

"It is not on the way."

"It's on a way."

At 06:56 they came out of a narrow street onto the Old Town Square, and he stopped, because it was impossible not to. The square was huge and pale and wet, the colour of the inside of a shell, with towers like a child's drawing of towers and cobbles shining under the lamps. At the foot of the Old Town Hall tower a small crowd stood in the rain, a school party in anoraks and a couple in wedding clothes with a photographer, all looking up.

The clock on the side of the tower was not like any clock Dec had ever seen: a great blue and gold disc of circles inside circles, with a gold sun on a hand, a little silver moon, and a ring of curly golden numbers round the edge that did not seem to count anything he recognised. He tried to read the time off the inner dial and made it a few minutes to six, an hour slow, and thought that a clock six hundred years old was entitled to be an hour slow if it liked. Above it were two little shuttered windows. To the right of the dial, on a narrow ledge, stood the painted figure of a skeleton.

The gold hand reached six, with Dec's watch at seven, and the clock kept its own hour without the least embarrassment: the skeleton moved.

It raised one bony arm and pulled a rope, and a little bell rang. In its other hand it turned an hourglass over. Above the dial the two windows opened and wooden figures in robes slid past them one after another, looking down at the square, and the school party filmed it on fourteen phones, and the skeleton went on pulling its rope and nodding its skull at them, its jaw opening and shutting, as if it were telling them something they were not listening to.

Dec put his hand in his jacket and took out the brass token, and held it up in the rain.

The same skeleton, with the same bony arm raised, the same bell on its rope and the same open jaw. Whoever had struck the token had stood where he was standing and looked at this.

"Inès," he said.

"I see it."

The windows shut. A cockerel above the clock crowed, and somewhere high in the tower a bell struck six times. The crowd clapped. Dec looked at his watch again, and it still said seven.

"It's ringing somebody in," said Dec.

"Taxi," said Inès.

The building in Vinohrady was tall and cream, with stone faces over its doors and iron balconies, in a street of lime trees that dripped on them. The street door had been propped open with a folded newspaper by somebody going out for bread. There was a cage lift neither of them trusted and a stone stair with a worn red runner, and on the fourth floor a tall cream double door with a card beside it, Vaňková, and a brass bell push in the shape of a little trumpet.

Dec rang it.

Nothing happened for a while. Then slippers came down a passage on parquet, and a chain rattled, and a bolt went back, and the door opened the width of a hand, and then the width of a woman.

She was small and upright, in a dressing gown of dark green silk with a dragon on it, and her silver hair was already up in a high French twist, every pin in place, at seven in the morning. Her lipstick was on, and very red. There was a ring on every finger of the hand that held the door. She looked at Dec's wet boots and Inès's suitcase with polite and absolute bewilderment, as if two strangers had walked out of the rain into the middle of a play she was in.

Behind her, down the long passage, Dec could see clocks: a tall clock in a case, a carriage clock on a table, a round station clock high on the wall, a little gilt one on a mantelpiece in the room beyond. All of them had stopped, and every one of them said nine.

"Paní Vaňková?" said Inès.

"Yes," said the woman. "And you are?"

Her voice came out into the cold stairwell like a warm coat put round his shoulders. The velvet voice from the answering machine and the speaking clock, and here it was in a silk dressing gown, with no idea who he was.

"We came from Paris," said Dec. "You asked us to come."

"I asked nobody to come. I don't know you. I've never seen you in my life." She did not look at her wrist, where there was no watch, nor round at any of the dead clocks behind her. She looked at Dec as if he had done something unforgivable to the hour. "And it is seven fourteen and twenty seconds in the morning."

Dec looked at his watch.

It was.
