Cloves at Forty Paces

There are things a locksmith does without thinking. Closing what he opened is one of them.

Dec had the clock face turned back and out of the recess before the second footstep landed. The heel of his hand pushed the granite plate home. Deep in the stone, the latch caught. He killed his torch. Inès already had the plastic bag inside her coat, her own torch dark, and the fog came down over the two of them like a lid on a pot.

Up the path, the other torch came on, held low, swinging from grave to grave to grave.

"Split," Dec breathed.

"Why?"

"I don't know. It's what you do."

She went left. He went right, on his hands and knees, through wet leaves and the sodden remains of a wreath, and fetched up behind a large bad-tempered tomb with railings round it and a bronze bust on top. His back against the cold stone, his knees against his chin, he tried to make himself the size of a schoolboy.

In the grey light he could just read the plaque under the bust. ACHILLE LAMOUREUX. TÉNOR DE L'OPÉRA. 1811-1869. Above it, the bronze head of Achille Lamoureux was thrown back, mouth wide, singing a high note into the fog for a hundred and fifty years. The most exposed face Dec had ever seen. Any second now it would give him away.

The footsteps came on. Crunch. Crunch. Each one landing twice at once, so that it sounded like a single heavy man with a very good sense of rhythm.

Something warm and enormous pressed against his shin.

Balzac. The fat cemetery cat had appeared from nowhere and was rubbing the whole length of his body along Dec's leg, and then, without so much as a by-your-leave, climbed into the gap between his knees and his chest and settled there like a sack of flour, and began to purr. Not a small purr. The purr of an outboard motor at full throttle in a quiet harbour.

"No," Dec whispered. "Balzac. Not now. Please."

Balzac purred louder.

The torch beam swung round the side of the tenor's tomb and lay across the gravel a yard from Dec's boots. It stayed there. Dec held the cat against his chest with both arms, which only encouraged it, and looked at the beam, and at the path beyond it where two pairs of legs had stopped: black trousers, black shoes polished so well the torch shone back off them, toes pointing exactly the same way. One of the hands that held the torch wore a black leather glove.

Above him Achille Lamoureux went on singing silently.

The beam moved on. Across the next grave, slow, then up Jan Vaněk's black granite, and stopped on the round recess at the top of the stone. It rested there longer than anything should be looked at in the middle of the night. Then down to the knocked-over vase, and the raked gravel, and back up to the recess.

Then it went out.

The footsteps turned and went away down the path the way they had come, together, unhurried, crunch, crunch, until the fog had them, and there was nothing left but water dripping off the trees and a cat purring like a boat.

Dec gave it a full five minutes by his watch. Then he set Balzac down, which Balzac resented, and stood up, and his knees cracked like pistol shots.

A rusted iron door creaked across the path. Inès came out of a little Gothic chapel with cobwebs in her hair and her glasses in her hand.

"There is a family in there," she said. "The Duponts. Since eighteen seventy-two." She put her glasses back on. "I think I sat on one."

Tuesday morning, a little after nine, in the atelier. Inès laid the white card on the bench beside her phone, and Dec dialled the number.

He had lain awake for most of what was left of the night in his room over the closed bakery, holding the brass token up to the street light from the window and turning it over. The skeleton on it was very finely struck, a little bony thing with its jaw open and a bell held up on a rope as if it was about to ring someone in. By five he had decided it was the creepiest object he had ever owned, and he owned a set of Victorian handcuffs from a magistrates' court in Sligo.

He put the call on the speaker.

It did not ring. It opened, the way a door opens onto a room that is already talking, and there she was.

"Gong oznámí devět hodin, čtrnáct minut, dvacet sekund."

And a gong, soft and deep, like a brass bowl struck in another room.

The voice from the machine, beyond any doubt: the same warm, low, unhurried voice, older than his mother, with the nap brushed the right way. The hair stood up on his arms the way it did at home when the wireless played the old songs at the end of the night.

"Gong oznámí devět hodin, čtrnáct minut, třicet sekund." Gong.

Inès looked at the clock on her laptop. It said 09:14:30. She looked at Dec.

"Gong oznámí devět hodin, čtrnáct minut, čtyřicet sekund." Gong.

It came every ten seconds. Hodin was hours, a word Inès knew, then minut and sekund, and the gong fell exactly on the second, every time. The most reliable woman Dec had ever listened to.

"It's a speaking clock," he said, very quietly, as if she might hear him.

"Yes," said Inès.

"A real one. Like at home. A woman you ring for the time." He counted the gongs on his fingers without meaning to, a habit from building escape rooms, where everything that repeated was a clue until it was proved otherwise. Four. Five. Nine fifteen and nothing. Six. Seven. "Is that it? We climbed a wall in the dark and sat on Duponts for a woman telling us the time?"

"Wait," said Inès.

Eight. Nine.

"Gong oznámí devět hodin, patnáct minut, čtyřicet sekund." The gong.

Then, where the next announcement should have been, a little silence. And in it the same voice, but lower, closer, as if the woman had leaned in to the microphone and put a hand round her mouth. French. Careful French, the vowels a little too round.

"Rue Vieille-du-Temple. Il faut venir."

Rue Vieille-du-Temple. You must come.

Then, without a pause, warm and grand again: "Gong oznámí devět hodin, patnáct minut, padesát sekund." And the gong.

Neither of them moved. Below the skylight a pigeon walked across the glass, click, click, click.

"That's us," said Dec. "That's this street. She's saying our street."

"She's saying it after the ninth one," said Inès.

They listened through another eight, and the ninth, and the little silence came again and the voice said it again, Rue Vieille-du-Temple. Il faut venir, exactly as before, to the breath. A recording, then. Somebody had taken a speaking clock and hidden a sentence inside it, after every ninth gong, like a note slipped between the pages of a timetable.

Dec rang off. The atelier was very quiet without her.

"Czech speaking clock," said Inès, and pulled her laptop toward her.

It took her ten minutes. He timed it, because he was a man who timed things, and because it gave him something to do with his hands other than pick up the token again. She read Czech the way she read anything she did not know, which was by refusing to accept that she did not know it: the translation in one window, the original in another, her finger on the screen moving along the accents.

"The Czech speaking clock," she said, "was called Přesný čas. Exact time. Until 1998 you rang one-one-two. Then they needed one-one-two for emergencies, so it moved to one-four-one-one-two, and now you pay for it." She scrolled. "That is not the number on the card. The number on the card is a private line in Prague. Somebody has a copy of the national speaking clock, with an extra sentence in it, on their own telephone." She stopped scrolling.

"What?"

She turned the laptop round.

A Czech news site, an article from a few years ago, with a large photograph. A woman of sixty-something sat in front of an old silver microphone on a stand, the kind with a cage round it like a birdcage for a very small bird. She was small and upright, with silver hair swept up into a high French twist, bright red lipstick and a great many rings, and she was looking at the camera as though she had been asked to forgive it.

"Hlas, který vám třicet let říkal čas," Inès read. "The voice that told you the time for thirty years. Dagmar Vaňková." She ran her finger down the translated text. "For thirty years the recorded voice of the Czech speaking clock, and of the station announcements. Retired. A little famous; they ask her to open things. Once married," and her finger stopped, "to an engineer called Jan Vaněk."

Dec looked at the woman in the photograph. Vox temporis non mentitur. A small woman with rings on every finger who had argued with the masons for an hour, Bastide said, about a centimetre.

"The voice of time," he said.

"Does not lie," said Inès.

They went down to the café on the corner at eleven because Dec said he could think better with a coffee in him and Inès said nobody could think in the atelier with him pacing.

A small zinc-and-mirror café with a red awning and a row of geraniums in a window box along the terrace, past their best, the last few flowers hanging on out of stubbornness. Inside, it was busy. A young woman stood at the far end of the counter in a cream trench coat, with a large pair of sunglasses on in October and a phone held up to her ear, and as Dec came in she turned her head toward him, and smiled, and turned it back. He thought nothing of it. Women in the Marais smiled at you sometimes in a way that meant they had just remembered something funny about somebody else.

He ordered two coffees at the bar and was told to sit down and they would be brought, and he sat on the terrace with Inès under the awning, and the waiter brought them, and Dec, who had been talking about speaking clocks the whole time, picked his up without stopping talking and drank half of it.

It tasted of cloves.

Not much, and not the way you'd taste a clove in a ham at Christmas. Underneath the coffee, at the back of the throat: a dusty sweetness, and under that something flat and cold, like licking a slate roof in the rain.

He knew that taste. He had last met it in a cup of tea at an inn in the Jura, and he had not forgotten it.

He turned his head and spat the whole mouthful into the window box. A geranium took it full in the face.

"Dec!"

"Don't drink that." He knocked her cup out of her hand. It went across the terrace and smashed, and a man at the next table stood up with coffee on his trousers. "Don't drink anything. Cloves. Inès, it's cloves."

She did not argue. She went white, and then she went very still, and then she looked past him into the café.

Inside, the young woman in the cream trench coat was walking out. Not quickly. She went past the end of the bar and out of the side door onto the rue des Rosiers with her phone at her ear, and Dec saw her only from behind, a low knot of chestnut hair, the belt of the cream coat swinging, and heard her as she went, in polished English, in the bright, smiling, faintly bored voice of a girl who books your flights for you:

"Lovely. Yes. All done. Thanks ever so much. Bye-bye now."

The waiter shouted after her. She had not paid.

Dec knew the voice. He had heard it on the telephone more times than he could count, in March, telling him that the Fondation Vaulmont would need the clockmaker's cell finished by the thirty-first, and how lovely it was, and how they had heard he had been arrested.

"Ferrand," he said. "Vaulmont's secretary. Mademoiselle Ferrand."

He was up and running before he had finished saying it, round the corner into the rue des Rosiers, into a crowd of tourists eating falafel and a delivery van backing up with its beeper going, and there was no cream trench coat anywhere. Nine hundred people in the street and none of them was her.

When he got back Inès was standing on the terrace holding a glass of water the waiter had brought her, not drinking it, and looking at the geranium. The geranium looked fine. Geraniums, Dec supposed, had very little to lose.

"You have never seen her," said Inès.

"I've never seen her. I've heard her forty times. That's her. That's the girl who rings you up and says lovely." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. His tongue felt thick, and a little numb at the tip, the way it did after the dentist. "She was stood right there. She smiled at me."

"She smiled at you," said Inès, "because she had just put the Grey Communion in your coffee."

After that, he tested everything.

He tested the bottle of water Inès bought him at the épicerie, by holding it up to the skylight and turning it slowly round, looking for he did not know what. He tested the tap in the atelier's little sink by running it for a full minute and then sniffing it. He tested a cup of tea Inès made him with her own hands while he watched, and she stood there with her arms folded while he sniffed it, and then dipped the tip of his little finger in it and touched it to his tongue, and then held it to the light.

"It's tea," said Inès.

"It's tea now."

"It was tea when I made it. I made it. You watched me make it."

"I can taste cloves at forty paces, Inès. I've a gift. It's a curse and a gift. I'm a sort of a bloodhound for cloves."

"You are a sort of a fool," said Inès. "Drink the tea."

He drank the tea. It tasted of tea. He decided not to say so, because she was watching him with her eyebrows in that particular straight line, and he had learned in six months that there were two kinds of silence with Inès Varga and that this was the dangerous kind.

At 16:10 her phone rang.

She looked at the screen and frowned at an unknown Paris number, and answered it, and said, "Oui?" in the voice she used on people who sold double glazing. Then her face changed.

"Yes," she said. "Yes, speaking." A pause. "Maître Carrère." She looked across the bench at Dec and mouthed something at him that he could not read. "No. No, I understand. He has said nothing at all? To anybody? Since April?" Another pause, longer. "Yes. I see."

She held the phone away from her ear and put it on the speaker, and laid it on the bench between them, beside the token and the white card, so that he could hear it. A woman's voice spoke from it, tired, precise, a lawyer's voice at the end of a long day.

"...nothing to the police, nothing to the magistrate, nothing to me, and I am his lawyer, Madame Varga. Six months. Not one word. He eats, he reads, he feeds the pigeons in the exercise yard, which is forbidden, and he says nothing. Then this morning he asked the guard for paper and a pen." She sighed. "He has asked to see you. Only you. He wrote it down for me, in capitals. He wants to see, and I quote, the woman with the pigeon."
