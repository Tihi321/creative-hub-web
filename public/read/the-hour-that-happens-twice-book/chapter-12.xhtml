Each Alone

The drawing office had been built for a foreman who liked to watch other men sweat.

It ran along the top of one long wall of the casting hall, under the roof: a narrow room with a slanting skylight and two windows. The yard window looked four storeys down to the cobbles. The other was a long window of small dusty panes that looked straight down into the hall, so that whoever sat at the desk could watch a pour without being cooked by it.

Renata Hollerová had been right. It had a lovely view of the pit.

Inès stood at the inner window with her hands flat on the sill and looked down at the cope. It sat on its iron ring at the bottom of the pit, a great grey clay bell in a cage of iron, bolted shut. The man in the ear defenders went on feeding the furnace, and every time he opened its door the whole hall went orange and the cope glowed like a lantern with its candle out.

She knocked on the glass. She knocked harder. The stoker did not turn round.

The door behind her was oak, with a lock that Dec would have called a good one. She had tried it twice. She tried it a third time, because it was there.

The rest of the room was plan chests and dust, drawings of half-bells sliced down the middle like a woman's profile, a dead fern, and a calendar from a firm that sold sand.

From the yard window, at 14:55, she watched a long black car pull up at the gate. Renata Hollerová came out of the little brick house beside it, putting on a grey coat. Behind her came the twins, one from each side of the doorway. The three of them got into the car. Before she got in, Hollerová looked up, straight at the top window, and lifted one gloved hand, the way a woman waves to a child at a school gate.

The car drove away.

At 15:00 the stoker banked the furnace, shut its iron door, hung his apron and his ear defenders on a peg by the side door, and went off to the pub on the corner with another man in a cap, in no hurry at all: they had been told the pour was at six, and they had seen the boss leave.

The hall was empty. The furnace muttered to itself. The cope sat in the pit.

"Bon," said Inès aloud, to nobody. "Bon. Très bien."

She went back to the inner window, and looked at what was outside it, which she had been not looking at for twenty minutes.

The crane ran on two iron girders, one along each long wall of the hall, high under the roof. The near one passed just below the sill of the window. A single steel beam about a hand and a half wide, with a rail along its top for the crane's wheels, and it ran the whole length of the hall, past the furnace, over the pit, to the far wall, where an iron ladder went down the bricks to the floor.

Halfway along, over the pit, sat the crane itself, its yellow bridge stretched across the hall to the other girder, the empty hook hanging down.

Perhaps fifteen metres to the floor. Inès did not measure it. Her stomach had measured it already and given her the figure.

The window was painted shut. She took the scalpel out of her trench pocket, the one that had cut Dec's bootlace a hundred years ago on Thursday, and ran it round the frame. The paint came away in long grey curls. She pushed. The window shrieked and opened, and the heat of the hall came up into her face, smelling of hot brick and wax and something metallic, like blood.

Her father had been afraid of nothing in the world except ladders. He had told her once, at the top of one, painting the shutters in Lyon, that the trick was to look at your hands. Never the floor. The floor would still be there when you wanted it.

She took off her grey ankle boots and tied them together by the laces and hung them round her neck with the mustard scarf. Then she climbed out over the sill and lowered herself onto the girder, and knelt on it, and looked at her hands.

"A kutya mindenit," she said to them.

She went on hands and knees. The steel was warm and gritty under her palms, the rail smooth and cold where the wheels had polished it. One hand on the rail, one on the edge of the beam, then her knees after them, and she said the worst things she knew in Hungarian, steadily, in order, as her father had said them to the car on winter mornings. Somewhere about the furnace she ran out and started over.

Under her the floor went by without being looked at.

The crane's end carriage blocked the girder above the pit: a yellow steel box on wheels, waist high, greasy, with a sign on it in Czech that she was sure said DO NOT CLIMB ON THIS. She climbed on it. For a moment, kneeling on top of it with nothing either side of her but air, she looked down, because the pit was there and Dec was in it.

The cope sat in its cage directly below her, grey and round and blind.

"Dec," she said, not loudly. Then she looked at her hands again, and climbed down the other side of the carriage, and went on.

The ladder was iron and cold and had thirty-one rungs. She counted every one, and when her stockinged foot touched the floor of the hall she stood with both hands on the rungs and her forehead against the iron for a while, until her knees decided to belong to her again.

Then she put on her boots and went to the pit.

He was not in it.

She knew it before she reached the edge. Footprints crossed the floor of the hall, long and wet and clay-grey, slapped down by somebody large in a hurry, coming up from the pit's ladder and going away towards the side door.

She looked down into the pit anyway. The cope still sat on its ring, bolted shut. But at its foot, where the brick of the core went down into the sand, a little iron door no bigger than an oven's stood open in the brickwork, and clay had been smeared all round it and dragged out of it, as if something had been born.

She followed the footprints out into the yard.

He was sitting on the cracked bell with the weed growing out of it, in the thin October sun, eating a rohlík.

He was grey from head to foot. His hair was clay. His leather jacket was clay. His face was clay except for two clean green eyes and a mouth full of bread. His belt hung open at his waist, and he looked at her as if she had come back from buying stamps.

"There's a coat on a peg by the door," he said, with his mouth full. "I found this in it. I left the man a Czech coin. I don't know what anything costs here." He swallowed. "I was coming up for you. I was just having this first." He looked past her at the hall. "Did you come down the crane?"

"Yes."

"You hate heights."

"Yes."

He looked at her boots, laced anyhow, and at the curls of grey paint on her coat, and he stopped eating.

"How did you get out?" said Inès, before he could say anything else.

"Every core's got a chimney." He waved the rohlík at the hall. "You light a fire in it to dry the clay. A fire needs a door, and a door needs a lock. Where there's a lock," he said, "there's me. I dug down through the top with my hands. It was soft. It was mostly horse. There was a padlock on the fire door outside, on a hasp, and I could get my arm through the vent." He dug two fingers into the tiny pocket of his jeans that nobody in the world ever uses, and held something out to her. "I forgot I had it. I told you I'd give it back."

Her hairpin, bent into an L, crusted with clay.

"And the belt," he said. "The tongue of the buckle. It's steel. Makes a grand tension wrench, if you don't mind your trousers."

Inès took the hairpin. She looked at it for a moment. Then she pushed it back into her hair, clay and all, above her ear, and Dec watched her do it and said nothing at all, which for him was a speech.

"You were brave in the stupid way," she said.

"I told you there was no other."

The little brick house by the gate said HOLLER on a brass plate, and its door had a lock that took Dec, with a hairpin and a belt, almost six minutes. He swore at it the whole time in a low, loving voice.

Renata Hollerová's office was panelled in dark wood and hung with portraits of men with beards standing beside bells. Her desk was clean and bare. In its top drawer, laid side by side like evidence, were two phones, a canvas satchel and a soft roll of brown canvas.

Dec picked up the cutlery and held it against his clay chest with both hands and closed his eyes.

"Not now," said Inès.

"Give me one second."

She gave him one second, and opened the grey folder that lay on the blotter.

It had a bronze bell embossed on the cover and a Roman V written in the corner in steel-grey ink. Inside were twelve pages, printed in English. The first said COLLECTIO HORAE DUPLICIS. For the Nine. Not to be copied.

Inès photographed every page before she read any of it. Then she read it, and Dec read it over her shoulder, dripping.

PragClear settles in local time. That was the first line of the second page, and under it, in a neat table: 03:00:00 summer time. System clock set back to 02:00:00.

"Go on," said Dec.

02:00, first. Batch P-1 released: state and private pensions, savings transfers. 46,212 payments.

02:00, second. Scheduler keyed to local time. P-1 released again. Duplicate payments held in suspense, pending time confirmation.

"Oh, that's lovely," said Dec, very quietly. "That's horrible and lovely. You key a thing to the clock on the wall, and the clock on the wall goes back an hour, and the thing does it all again." He wiped his nose with the back of his hand and left a grey stripe. "I built a room in Cork once with the dungeon on a timer. The night the clocks went back, a hen party had to go through the dungeon twice."

Time confirmation, read Inès, on the next page. J. Vaněk's voice check, 1996. The system accepts a correction of its clock only in the official voice of time, in speaking-clock form. And in brackets, in Czech, a line she could almost read: (Gong oznámí __ hodin, __ minut, __ sekund.) Confirmation releases the held batch to the accounts in Annex C.

Annex C was four pages of account numbers in very small print. She photographed them twice.

"Jan built a lock," said Dec slowly, "that only opens for her voice. To keep people out. And they're going to use it as the key."

Voice: recording of 15. 10. (VIII reading the list, with clock form), on the cabinet machine in the Orloj. To be played when the Orloj strikes two. Live presence of VIII not required.

J. Vaněk's so-called stop phrase: not found in the code. VIII questioned 15. 10., after the reading: no result. Considered a legend.

The last page had one line on it.

VIII: paid in full. To be remitted after the Collection.

"Remittere," said Inès. Her voice did a strange thing on it, and she waited for it to stop. "To send back. To forgive a debt." She closed the folder. "They will send her back. Wherever they think she came from."

Dec did not make a joke.

Inès put the folder back on the blotter exactly square, as she had found it. Then she took the photographs she had made of the line round the bell's lip and printed them, eight sheets, on Renata Hollerová's own printer, because it was there and it was hers. A restorer always checked her copy against the original.

The clock on the panelled wall said 16:20. The pour was at six.

The bell shed was as they had left it: the rows of bells on their black frames, the tin labels, the smell of coins. At the end of the last row, on its short frame, Jan's bell hung where it had hung for thirty years, brown as a conker, the clapper still tied up in its twist of wire. Nobody had touched it. Nobody had thought it worth touching.

Dec patted it on the crown, the way you pat a horse that has not kicked you.

"She doesn't know what it is," he said. "She walks past it every week. It's just the bell PragClear never wanted."

"Then it stays," said Inès. "If it is gone tomorrow, she will ask herself why."

She knelt on the boards under the frame and laid the eight printed sheets down end to end, one for each piece of the lip, the way she would lay out the fragments of a broken panel, so that the line that ran round the bell above her head ran straight across the floor in front of her: from the little raised star, through the smooth bald strip, back to the star. Then she checked every sheet against the bronze, letter by letter, with her fingertip on the metal and her eyes on the paper, because that was the rule.

DNUKES ALUN TUNIM ALUN NIDOH ▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒ ÍMÁNZO GNOG

Beside it she laid her phone, open at the photograph of the page with the brackets. Gong oznámí.

She stood up with her hands in her trench pockets and did not move. Dec had the sense to stand still too.

The last word did it. GNOG. She had looked at it at the bell and seen a noise. Now she looked at the bracket, and then back, and her eye did what it did with a signature under three centuries of varnish: it stopped reading the letters and listened to the tune.

"Read them like the clock in Josefov," she said.

She began at the right-hand end of the line, and walked slowly to the left, and read.

"GONG." She moved along one sheet. "OZNÁMÍ." Then the strip of nothing, and she stepped past it. "HODIN. NULA. MINUT. NULA. SEKUND."

Dec made a small noise.

"Nula," he said. "Nothing. I had that on an invoice in Paris. Gong oznámí, so many hours, nothing minutes, nothing seconds." He stared at the floor. "It's the speaking clock."

"It isn't nonsense," said Inès. "It never was. Jan wrote it backwards, like the clock in Josefov, and left it in Renata's own house, where nobody would ever think to read it."

Dec grinned at the floor, and then at her, and then the grin went slowly out of his face, because she was not smiling.

"Say it again," she said. "Without the hole."

"Gong oznámí." He frowned. "Hodin, nula minut, nula sekund."

"That is the speaking clock," said Inès. "That is what it says every ten seconds of every day. Kroupová rang Dagmar's number and heard it. If that were a stop phrase, the machine would have stopped in 1996 and never started again." She put one finger on the smooth grey strip in the photograph, between OZNÁMÍ and HODIN. "Everything that matters is here. The words are only the envelope. The hour is the letter."

She reached up to the bell, turned on her phone's torch and held it flat against the real bell, so that the light skimmed along the lip. It was how she found a lost signature under varnish: light from the side, and every scratch throws a shadow.

The file marks threw shadows. Nothing else did. Whoever had cut the number away had gone down past the roots of the letters, carefully, until there was nothing left to find.

"Try them all," said Dec. "There's only twenty-four hours. It's a combination lock with twenty-four numbers. I've opened worse with a cold."

"And if the system hears the wrong one?"

He opened his mouth and shut it again. He had built enough rooms to know. Three wrong answers and the lights went red. In some rooms, one.

"Jan made it an emergency brake," said Inès. "A man who builds an emergency brake does not let you pull it twenty-four times to see which works. And they questioned Dagmar. It is in the plan. No result. She says hours all day long. She would have said any hour they asked for." She turned off the torch. "So it is not an hour they could think of."

From the hall came the clang of the furnace door, and a man's voice singing something cheerful and flat in Czech. The stoker was back from the pub, getting the fire up for six.

Dec gathered the sheets off the floor without being told. "So," he said quietly. "We can read the whole thing."

"Every word," said Inès. She looked up at the bell, at the smooth place in its brown skin where a man's hand had taken something away, and the singing went on through the iron door. "Except the only one we need."
