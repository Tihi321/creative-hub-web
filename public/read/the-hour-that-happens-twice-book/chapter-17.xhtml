Twenty-Five O'Clock

The tape was already talking.

Dec heard it twice at once: small and close, from a speaker the size of a biscuit inside the cabinet, and a heartbeat later huge and far away, from every lamp post on the Old Town Square, coming back in through the open shutters above his head like weather.

"Vaněček, Josef," said Dagmar's voice, slow and thick, the voice from the drip room. "Devatenáct set třicet osm..."

He was on his knees before he had decided to be. The bronze door hung open over his shoulder, and inside it everything was laid out as neat as a surgeon's tray: the grey machine, the green clock reading 02:00:03, the brown tape running from one spindle across the heads to the other, and the black cable climbing into the back of it all like a root. Clipped inside the door at the height of a standing man's chest, on a short chrome stalk, was a microphone, for the engineers to test the line. Lower down, at the height of his own nose as he knelt, was a black switch with two words under it, PÁSKA and MIKROFON, and a red light over PÁSKA.

He did not think about the switch. He thought about the tape. He put his whole hand round the turning reel, the way you grab a cat that is going under a car, and tore it off its spindle.

It came away with a noise like a zip. The tape snapped, and leapt, and spilled from the machine in long brown loops that tangled round his wrists and his knees and the hem of the stupid robe. Out on the square the huge slow voice said "Vaněč..." and stopped, mid-name, as if somebody had put a hand over its mouth.

"Mr Hartley," said Hollerová.

He looked up. She had come down the gangway while the door swung, quick and quiet, and now she stood over him with the pistol pointing at the middle of his face. She was not angry. That was the worst of it. She looked like a woman whose taxi had come to the wrong door.

Then Dagmar was there.

He never saw her cross the room. One moment she was by the iron door in her grey felt slippers, and the next she had both her hands round Hollerová's right wrist, all ten rings biting at once, and was hanging on it with the whole of her small upright weight, the way she must have hung on to microphones for thirty years.

The pistol went off.

In that iron room it was not a bang. It was a blow, to the ears and the chest and the teeth. And straight after it, worse, came the sound of the clock being hurt: a shriek of metal on metal from the alcove where the procession drum turned, a grinding that went up and up, and a great iron clunk that Dec felt through the boards in his knees. Every wheel in the frames shuddered. Something above the dial cracked like a stick. And then a part of the noise of the room that had been going on all week, so steady he had stopped hearing it, simply was not there.

The drum had stopped. A shining smear of lead was jammed between two of its iron teeth. Up through the stone, half in and half out of his little window, an apostle stood frozen with his hand raised in a blessing he could not finish. The wire to Death's arm hung tight as a fiddle string and did not move.

"Ježíšmarjá," said Dagmar, very quietly, and let go.

Hollerová looked at the drum. For the first time all week, her face had something in it she had not put there on purpose.

Inès did not look at the drum at all. She went past the twins and past Hollerová and past Dec on his knees in his nest of tape, straight to the open cabinet, and pulled the microphone off its clip. Its cable came after it, coiled, still plugged in at the back. She held it out.

"Madame," she said. "Your line."

Dec did not get up. Still on his knees in the tape, he reached in under her arm to the switch at the foot of the door and pushed it from PÁSKA to MIKROFON. The red light jumped across. Somewhere in the cabinet a relay clicked, and from the speaker the size of a biscuit, and from every lamp post on the square, came the soft hiss of an open line, waiting.

Dagmar took the microphone.

She held it the way he had seen her hold the old caged silver one in the newspaper photograph: a little below her chin, not too close, with her rings round it and her little finger lifted. She did not look at Hollerová. She did not look at anyone. She drew one breath, a professional's breath, all the way down, and when she let it out it was the voice.

"Gong oznámí dvacet pět hodin, nula minut, nula sekund."

It went out over the square like a warm coat dropped over a cold child. It filled the tower. It filled the room. It was low and round and absolutely certain, and it told forty-six thousand payments and a hundred grey hoods and forty sleepy policemen that it was twenty-five o'clock, exactly, as if that were a fact like rain.

Every speaking clock needs a gong. Dec reached up and flicked the little bronze bell of the lock with his fingernail, beside the microphone, and it rang once, small and clear.

For a moment nothing happened at all.

Then, on the bench behind him, the PragClear engineer's laptop woke up by itself.

Its screen went red from edge to edge. A list began to scroll up it, faster than he could read, line after line of white capitals, and out of its little speaker came a noise like a smoke alarm having a nightmare. A second alarm joined it, higher. A third. Down on the square, somewhere in the crowd, a phone began to ring, and then another, and then a pocketful of them, the way phones ring at a funeral when the news has got out.

"What does it say?" said Dec. "Somebody. Please."

Dagmar lowered the microphone a little and looked over at the screen with her chin up, the way she would have looked at a menu in a bad restaurant.

"Zúčtování zastaveno," she read. "Settlement stopped." Her eyes went down the lines. "Time source rejected. Twenty-five hours, nought minutes, nought seconds. All batches suspended. All suspended payments cancelled. Emergency stop, Vaněk." She stopped, and swallowed. "It says his name, Jan's. On the screen. Nouzové zastavení, Vaněk."

Her mouth shook once, and was a red line again.

Then she lifted the microphone, and turned to the open shutters, and spoke to the square below in a voice that was not the speaking clock at all, but her own, plain, a little hoarse, a woman of sixty-eight at two in the morning.

"Nikdo nic nedluží."

She held the microphone a moment longer, and said it in French, for Inès, softly, so that only the room heard it. "Personne ne doit rien."

Nobody owes anything.

Dec got up off his knees, shedding tape, and went to the greenish window.

The square was very still. Under the lamps the grey hoods stood among the coats, and they were looking at one another, as people look at one another in a church when the priest has said something that was not in the book. Near the foot of the tower an old man with a walking stick had pushed back his hood. He was looking at his wrist. As Dec watched, he undid the strap of his watch with fingers that were clumsy from the cup, and held it up to the light, a plain silver disc with no hands, and looked at it as if he had never seen it before. Then he put it in his pocket. Beside him a woman in a good scarf took hers off and simply let it fall on the cobbles. Further off, another. Not all of them. Enough.

At the corner of the tower, by the NO TOURIST door, he saw a grey parka go in at a run.

They heard the feet long before they saw them, because there were a great many, and none of them were trying to be quiet.

Hollerová had not moved. She stood in the gangway with her back to the stopped drum, and the pistol hung at her side as if she had forgotten it, and she was looking at Dagmar.

"Twenty-five o'clock," she said at last. "Jan never could resist a joke."

"He liked me," said Dagmar. "That was his joke."

Major Kroupová came in from the main stair with her parka open and her cracked phone in her hand, and behind her two men in plain dark coats, and behind them two officers in uniform, breathing hard, who stopped dead in the doorway at the sight of the twins tied together by the boots and had to be pushed in by the officers behind them.

Kroupová looked at the room. She looked at the tape round Dec's ankles, at the red laptop, at the old woman with the microphone, at the pistol. Then she held up her phone, so that Hollerová could see the screen. It showed a page of numbers in small print, the third page of Annex C.

"Paní Hollerová," she said. "On Friday I was taking note. On Saturday I was a tourist." She put the phone away. "Tonight, nobody told me to come up these stairs. I decided it myself. I expect to be in a great deal of trouble on Monday." She held out her hand. "The gun, please."

Hollerová looked at her for a long moment, with real interest, as if the major were a bell whose note she had not expected. Then she walked to the cabinet, past Dec, close enough that he smelt her scent, something cold like a church in winter, and laid the pistol on the bronze roof of it, beside the little bell, gently, the way you lay down a programme at the end of an opera.

"Your watch also," said Kroupová.

Hollerová smiled, undid it, and dropped it into the major's palm. "It doesn't tell the time, Major."

Kroupová turned it over. Dec leant to look. On the silver back, in the same small fine engraving he had last seen on a watch in Paris, was written: V de IX.

"What does it mean?"

"Five of nine," said Inès.

"And who are the other eight?"

Hollerová only looked at the stopped drum, and at the apostle in his window with his hand up, and said nothing at all, which, Dec thought, was the first thing she had ever learned from the twins.

The twins themselves were exactly where he had left them, sitting knee to knee on the boards, each with a gloved hand to his forehead, in exactly the same way. One of the uniformed officers crouched and looked at the knot in their laces, and looked up at Dec.

"Galway," said Dec. "We do hay."

Inès came and stood beside him at the window, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Then he put his hand in the sleeve of his robe and found the hairpin, which he had pulled out of the lock without noticing, bent now into a shape no hairpin had ever been, and held it out.

She took it, looked at it, and pushed it into her hair above her ear, clay and all.

"You will find it in the washing machine in a year," he said.

"I will find it in my hair," said Inès. "It lives there."

The feet on the stair had changed. Some of them were going down now, with the twins between them, hopping, because nobody had yet found anybody willing to untie a knot tied by a man from Galway. And some of them were coming up: two pairs, slow, one heavy and patient and breathing like a man who has eaten well, and one that struck the stone every step like a hammer.

"Pomalu," said a voice on the stair, in pain and in fury. "Pomalu, I am not a piano. I can walk. Who shot my clock?"

Šťastný came into the clockwork room on Pichon's arm, with the tartan thermos clutched to his chest and a tongue still faintly grey and his white moustache standing out like a cat's. Pichon had a napkin round his neck and a paper bag of trdelník in his other hand, and he looked round the room as if deciding whether it served lunch.

"I was taking forty policemen to see a clock," he said, "and I found this one on the stairs, swearing in German. I thought he was yours."

Šťastný did not hear him. He had seen the drum.

He let go of Pichon. He walked down the gangway between the wheels with his hands held out in front of him, as if he were wading into a cold sea, and stopped in front of the alcove, and looked at the smear of lead between the teeth, and at the apostle stuck in his window, and at the wire to Death's arm, tight and shaking. He said nothing. His face did something under the moustache that Dec did not want to look at.

Inès went to him. From inside her trench, from against her ribs where it had been all night, she took his flat cap, and held it out.

He looked at it. He took it, and put it on, and pulled the peak down, the way a surgeon puts on gloves.

"Light," he said.

Dec held his penlight. Šťastný put his thick oily fingers into the iron teeth of his clock, where no one else in the city would have dared to put a finger, and felt for the lead the way you feel for a splinter in a child's hand. He found its edge with his thumbnail. He said something to the drum in Czech, very low, the way you talk to a horse that has been frightened. He took the iron night-pin down off its nail, with its little cardboard bell, and put its end against the lead, and leant.

The lead came out with a small tired click, and fell, and rolled across the boards to Dec's boot.

For a second nothing moved. Then the drum gave a long iron sigh, and turned.

Above them, through the stone, the apostle in his window lowered his hand, finished his blessing, and moved on, and the next came after him, and the next. The wire to Death's arm jerked and went slack and jerked again, and out on the front of the tower the little bone figure rang his bell. The cockerel crowed, a creak of old bellows, from somewhere high up in the dark. The shutters banged.

And then, high in the tower, the great bell of the Orloj struck. Once. And again.

Two.

On the square, a hundred people who no longer knew what to do with their wrists looked up at it. Forty policemen took photographs. Kroupová, in the doorway, looked at her own watch, and then at Šťastný.

The old man took his pocket watch out of his overalls, the one that ran four minutes fast on purpose, and looked at it for a long time.

"Eleven minutes," he said. "Six hundred years." He shut the watch with a snap and put it away, and turned to Dec, and his eyes were wet and terrible under the peak of the cap.

"Late," said Vilém Šťastný. "My clock is late. For the first time in its life."
