The Voice

The twin at the end of Inès's row still knelt, hood pushed back, and looked at her for three seconds or so. For a man who never spoke, that passed for a long conversation.

The stone cup in front of him was full to the brim. Inès had watched him carry it back from the urn with everybody else and lift it to his hood. Now he had set it down again, untouched. In a room of people giving their hour, he had kept his. As she had kept hers, and Dec his.

Then something buzzed inside his robe. Out came a phone. He read it, put it away, and studied Dagmar's red mouth. Inès's hand closed round Dagmar's elbow. She watched him weigh them: one woman of sixty-eight in felt slippers, one younger, a cellar full of drugged believers, and no way out but a stair his brother had just climbed. He rose, stepped over the cup without spilling a drop, and went after the faithful, quick and grey among the slow grey backs, and was gone.

"He has decided we are furniture," said Inès.

"Good," said Dagmar. "I have been furniture all night. Very restful. Where do we go?"

"Up."

They went against the hymn. The faithful shuffled towards the way they had come, singing with their eyes half shut, and nobody stopped two more hoods heading the wrong way round the end of the trestle table. In the corner the grey heap still rose and fell, as it had at two o'clock when she first dared to look. Šťastný was not dead, only Stopped, like Dagmar, like Roux in Paris, the Grey Communion in him and his night taken away. Inès knelt, lifted the robe off the white moustache, and tipped the last of the tarry coffee between his lips.

He coughed. Coughed again, horribly, and said a word in German that she had heard from her father once, about a carburettor.

"Touristen," said Šťastný, quite clearly, eyes still shut.

"Yes," said Inès. She pressed the thermos into his hand and closed his oily fingers round it. "Drink it all. Then come up and shout at us."

His fingers tightened. That would have to do. His cap was still inside her trench, against her ribs, and she left it there.

The arch led to a stair so narrow that Inès's shoulders brushed both walls at once, and so steep that she climbed it with her hands. Every door Dec had opened stood open behind him. The twin had not bothered to shut them either.

Dagmar went first, in the grey felt slippers somebody had put on her feet, one hand on the wall and the other holding up the robe like a ball gown.

"Two hours, forty-four minutes and twenty seconds," she said, on the first landing.

"Madame, you don't have to."

"I do. It is how I breathe on stairs." She went on up. "Thirty years, darling. Be grateful I leave out the gong."

On the landing of the old cells the council's reindeer watched them with its sorrowful glass eyes. Dagmar stopped in front of it and inclined her head.

"Dobrý večer," she said to it, and to Inès, in French: "One must be polite. One never knows who is in the Brotherhood."

Above the cells the stair turned against the outer wall, and there was a window in it: a slit, a hand wide, no glass, and the cold came through it smelling of wet stone and frying sugar. Inès's father had been afraid of nothing but ladders, and he had taught her to look at her hands and never at the floor. She looked at the square.

A long way down.

The faithful were coming out from under the arcade at the corner of the Town Hall in a slow grey stream. The crowd of coats parted for them without being asked, as it would for a hearse. Their hymn came up thin and steady over the cobbles. On the lamp posts round the square, at the height of a man's head, Inès saw grey loudspeaker horns that had not been there on Thursday, cable-tied tight, each with a small bronze bell stencilled on its side, all of them turned towards the tower.

And out of the mouth of a street at the far side of the square came a second procession that nobody had planned but one man: forty-odd people in raincoats and anoraks with conference lanyards round their necks, in a loose, happy crocodile, one of them singing in Dutch. At its head walked Commissaire Pichon, holding up a paper bag of trdelník like a lantern, cinnamon sugar down the front of his beige raincoat.

By the statue stood Kroupová in her grey parka, cracked phone up, as she had stood all night. Inès watched the major lower it. Kroupová looked at the grey hoods, and at the forty policemen, and at the wrists in the crowd. Then she turned her head and looked straight up the tower, and for a moment Inès could have sworn the major was looking at the slit.

Kroupová put the phone to her ear. Her other hand pulled the zip of her parka down to the middle of her chest, so that she could run in it.

"Now," said Inès, "she is watching."

"Two hours, forty-seven minutes," said Dagmar from above. "Come away from the window, darling. You are green."

Near the top, where the stair squeezed between two walls, a gap opened in the stone on the left, and in the light of her phone Inès saw wooden feet in painted sandals under the hem of a gilded robe. A ring of carved saints stood on a wheel in the dark with their backs to her, shoulder to shoulder, waiting behind their shut shutters like men in a queue for a train that was late. One of them held a key. She did not stop to see which.

The low iron door at the top stood ajar, a red dot of paint on its lock, and through it came the breathing of the wheels, and a woman's voice, low and warm, reading to children.

"No, I'd rather you sat up, Mr Hartley. Hands behind you, like a good boy at school. I like to see a man's face while he's thinking."

Inès put out her hand to stop Dagmar. Dagmar was already pushing the door.

"I go first," she said. "I am the Eighth. She will not shoot the Eighth before the hour. It would be very bad liturgy."

The clockwork room was long and full of turning iron, and in the middle of its one gangway, knee to knee on the boards, sat the twins, each with a gloved hand to his forehead, their boots tied together in a large and cheerful knot. They looked up at Inès with exactly the same face.

"Ah," said Inès.

By the main stair stood Renata Hollerová in her fitted grey robe, the bronze bell at her throat, a small black pistol held low in her right hand. At the foot of the bronze cabinet sat Dec, back against it, long legs out in front of him, in a grey robe that stopped at his shins, his belt hanging open, his hands behind him. The little bronze bell of the lock hung just above his shoulder. Šťastný's key ring lay on the boards by his hip.

His eyes found her and stayed.

"You're late," he said.

"We were held up by a reindeer."

"Dagi," said Hollerová, with real pleasure, as if Dagmar had arrived at a dinner. "You came up. I was going to send for you." She spoke English, for Inès's benefit, and smiled at her too. "Madame Varga. You've worked so hard this week. I want you to follow everything."

Dagmar stood very straight in her slippers. With the lipstick back on, she looked like herself.

"You put my hair up," she said. "Badly."

"I'm sorry. I had other things to do." Hollerová lifted her left wrist, glanced at the watch with no hands, and smiled at it.

"It does not tell you anything, Renata," said Dagmar. "It is two hours, fifty-three minutes and ten seconds. I tell you. I always told you."

"You did. Thirty-two years." Hollerová's voice went lower and kinder. "The Fifth calls the hour and the Eighth reads it. A bell and a mouth. You never missed one, Dagi. Not one Collection. Not when Jan left, not when your mother died. And now you've come all this way up the prisoners' stair, with a French girl who thinks I'm a witch, to stand in front of a clock and do the one thing you've never done. Break your word."

Dagmar said nothing. Her rings were very still.

"Everybody owes," said Hollerová. "You know that better than anyone. You've read the names. A pensioner more than most. Think how long they've had." She tipped her head a little. "Božena has had seventy years and more."

Inès felt Dagmar's arm go hard under the grey sleeve.

"Page three hundred and twelve," said Hollerová gently. "Vaňková, Božena. 1953. You read her yourself on Thursday. Beautifully. I don't suppose you remember." From inside her robe, with her left hand, she drew a fountain pen and held it up between two fingers. "I have the list on the system, darling, and I have this. One line. I can take one line out. For you. Her pension stays where it is. Her room is paid. She never knows there was a Saturday." The pen turned in the floodlight from the window. "Nobody else on earth could give you that tonight."

Dagmar took a step towards her.

A small step, in felt slippers, and the most frightening thing Inès had seen all week. She watched the old woman's face go soft with longing, and understood exactly what was being bought, and with what. Forty-six thousand names, and one of them her sister's.

"And me?" said Dagmar. Her velvet had gone thin. "What happens to the Eighth, Renata, afterwards?"

"You rest. You've paid in full." Hollerová smiled. "You'll be remitted."

"Madame," said Inès.

She did not raise her voice. Dagmar stopped.

"In Paris," said Inès, "last spring, under the city, we found a ledger. There were names in it. Next to one of them, a man called Lassalle, someone had written remis. Remitted." She kept her eyes on Hollerová. "By then Monsieur Lassalle had been murdered."

The pen stopped turning.

Dagmar looked at it. Then she stepped back, carefully, as if from the edge of a platform, and put her hand back on Inès's arm.

"Renata," she said. "You always lied very badly. You do it too well."

Hollerová put the pen away without hurry. Nothing in her face had changed, but her right hand had come up an inch, and the small black pistol was now looking at Dagmar.

And in the quiet that came after, under the hundred ticks and the breathing of the wheels, Inès heard it. Very faint, behind Dec's back, from the bronze: a note. One clear note, high and pure, that rose out of the iron noise and hung there, and did not fade.

She had spent her life listening to the tune of things, a brushstroke, a signature, a voice on a tape run backwards. She knew a new voice joining a choir when she heard one. Dec's elbows had not moved. His face was turned up to Hollerová, mild and interested, a man sitting on a floor. But behind him, the lock had one more voice than it had a minute ago.

Hollerová's head began to turn towards the cabinet.

"Your grandfather tuned that," said Inès quickly. "Dec says it sings."

Hollerová's head stopped. Her eyes came back to Inès, and something in them warmed, very slightly, the way a cold stove warms when you open the door.

"Every lever," she said. "Twenty-four. He did it the winter my father was born. He said a lock should sound like a peal when it opens, so that you'd know it from the next room."

"I don't understand bells," said Inès. "A bell is one note. You hit it, it rings."

"Oh, no." Hollerová almost laughed. "That's what everybody thinks. A bell is five notes at least, pretending to be one. There's the hum, at the bottom, which goes on after everything else has stopped. There's the strike, which is the one you think you hear. And in the middle there's the tierce." She hummed it, softly, a falling sound, and it was beautiful. "A minor third. That's why bells sound sad, even at weddings. People think it's the occasion. It's the tierce."

Behind Dec, under her voice, a second note slid into the chord. Then a third.

"How do you make them agree?" said Inès.

"You take metal away. From the inside, on a lathe, a shaving at a time, and you strike it, and you listen, and you take a little more." Hollerová's free hand turned in the air, shaving bronze. "You can only ever take away. If you take too much, there's nothing to do but break it up and pour it again. My grandfather stood inside every bell he cast, with his head up in the waist, listening. He was deaf in one ear by fifty. He said it was the ear God didn't need."

"Two hours, fifty-seven minutes and forty seconds," said Dagmar, telling the time to nobody, in the voice of a station.

Inès glanced at the greenish window. Down below, the grey hoods had reached the foot of the tower and stopped, a hundred faces turned up to the dial. The parka was no longer by the statue. She found it after a moment, much nearer, at the corner where the NO TOURIST door was, with two men in plain dark coats beside it who were not looking at the clock at all.

"And the lock," said Inès. "If you tune it to twenty-four notes, you could open it by singing."

"You could, if you were God. A key is quicker." Hollerová smiled at her, almost fondly. "You're very good, Madame Varga. You'd have liked my grandfather. He used to keep people talking too."

The pistol had come back round to Dec.

"Mr Hartley," she said, without looking down, "I can hear you. You've done twenty-two. That's very nearly a miracle, and in a minute and a half it won't matter in the least, because at two o'clock that reel plays whether the door is open or shut, and nothing in this room can stop it. So do go on, if it makes you feel better." Her voice was perfectly kind. "But if you stand up, I'll have to shoot you, and I'd hate to do it in front of Dagi."

Dec looked up at her. Then, very slowly, he went on.

Twenty-three. Inès heard it go in, low, almost a hum. One left: the twenty-fourth, the last.

"Two hours, fifty-nine minutes," said Dagmar, "and thirty seconds." The time, as always, going one way only.

Nobody said anything after that. The wheels breathed. Down on the square, a hundred people stopped singing at the same moment, as if a hand had been laid over their mouths, and in the silence Inès could hear the hymn's echo still going round the tall houses, looking for a way out. Somewhere above her head, through the stone, something wooden shifted on its wheel.

She looked at her phone.

02:59:57.

02:59:58.

02:59:59.

The next number was not three. The screen blinked, as if it had changed its mind, and said 02:00:00, and the hour that had already happened began to happen again.

Above them the shutters banged open, both at once, with a clatter of old wood. Cold air poured down through the stone. A wire on the wall beside the drum, which Inès had not even noticed, drew suddenly tight and sang, and out on the front of the tower, she knew without seeing it, the little bone figure of Death was lifting his bell rope.

Hollerová smiled.

And behind Dec's back the twenty-fourth lever sang, the smallest and the highest, a note like a fingernail on a wine glass, and Dec, sitting on the floor with his hands behind him, said quite quietly, "Got it."

There was a sound like a whole peal of bells struck at once, very small, as if from inside a church a mile away. The bronze door of the cabinet swung open over his shoulder on its heavy hinges, and inside it, threaded across the heads, the reel of brown tape was already turning.
