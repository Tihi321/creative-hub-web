The Hour Again

The prison stair went up inside the Town Hall wall the way a worm goes up inside an apple, and it had been built for men who were not expected to come down again.

Dec climbed it in the grey robe, knees bent, Šťastný's keys pressed to his chest with both hands so they would not ring. Steep steps, short steps, always turning right. Every thirty steps a door: iron, oak, iron. Every door locked, and every lock on the ring, and the ring was the size of a saucer and carried perhaps sixty keys, not one of them labelled, because Šťastný had known which was which for forty years and could see no reason why anybody else should.

He found the first on the ninth try.

"You could have written on them, Vilém," he whispered. "A dab of paint. A wee number."

He tried not to look at his watch. He looked. 02:11.

Forty-nine minutes. Out on the square, through the old stone, the Orloj was saying eleven minutes past one, and it would go on saying the right thing, minute after minute, at its own unhurried pace, until its hand came round to two. And the moment it got there, every telephone and laptop and bank in Prague would fall back from three to two to meet it, the hour would begin again, and the box in the cabinet would play Dagmar to the bank.

He could not stop that clock. He would not have dared. The man who kept it lay on a cellar floor under a grey robe with a tongue the colour of pencil lead, and Dec had put the backs of his fingers to the old moustache on his way past and felt the breath go in and out, slow and stubborn, like a bellows in a shed. They had not killed him. They had only taken his night off him, the way the council had wanted.

The second door took eleven tries. The third took four. Beyond it lay a landing of old cells, each with a barred grille and a slot for the bread, and the council had filled them with Christmas: coils of lights, a plywood stable, three kings without their gifts, and a life-size reindeer whose glass eyes caught the light of his phone and followed him up the next flight with great sorrow.

The last door was low and iron, and on its lock sat a little red dot of paint that Dec could have kissed. He put his ear to it instead.

Through the iron came the breathing of the wheels, the hundred ticks, and over them a man's voice, bored, talking Czech into a telephone.

Dec eased the door open a finger's width.

A young man in a fleece with PRAGCLEAR across the back sat on Šťastný's crate eating a sandwich out of foil. A laptop lay open on the bench beside him, and a toolbag, and a tin of the wrong kind of oil, which Šťastný would have had a great deal to say about. The engineer watched the procession drum and nodded, the way a man nods at a machine that is doing what it was paid for.

The drum was turning. Not fast; it never moved fast. But on the beam above it Dec could see the iron night-pin that Šťastný slid through the frame every evening, so the apostles could sleep. It had been drawn out and hung on a nail. From it dangled a cardboard tag on a string, printed with a little bronze bell.

The engineer finished his sandwich and made another call, a long one. Dec stood with his cheek against the cold iron and listened to his own watch. Then the phone rang again. The young man listened, said "Ano, ano, jasně," three times in rising tones of misery, screwed up his foil, and went out by the other door and down the main stair, the one that came up from the NO TOURIST door on the side of the tower. His feet went round and round, getting fainter.

Dec counted twenty, and went in.

The clockwork room stood as he had left it on Friday, except that it was night, and he was alone with it, and he was frightened of it in a way he had not been in daylight.

The great wheels turned in their frames, black and yellow. Behind the dial the gold hand's arbor crept round. Through the small square window of greenish glass, the square was full.

He went to look. He could not help it.

Not with grey; not yet. The hooded ones were still under his feet, giving their hour. Coats, that was all: people standing in the cold among the cafés' stacked chairs, hands in pockets, faces turned up to the dial, as if they had been told a famous singer would appear at a window. Some of them wore watches with no hands. He looked for a grey parka by the statue and found it, quite still, a phone held up at nothing.

Above the dial, just over his head through the stone, the two little apostles' windows were shut. In forty-one minutes they would open, at night, which they never did.

Dec turned his back on the window and went to the cabinet.

It stood against the far wall, bronze going green, eight steel bolts, the black cable dropping through its raw hole into the floor. He looked at the cable. He had no tool in the world that would cut it, and it was the only road a voice had to the bank.

He knelt, lay down on his back on the boards, and shone his penlight up into the little bronze bell that hung low on the cabinet's face where the keyhole should be.

Through the vent slots in the side, if he turned his head and squinted, he could see a strip of the inside. Two spindles. A reel of brown tape loaded on one of them and threaded across the heads, ready. Under it, on a little panel, a green digital clock.

01:19:44.

An hour behind his watch. Like the Orloj. Of course it was. The tape was keyed to the clock that does not lie.

"Right," said Dec. "Right. Hello again."

He tried the cheap way first, because only a fool doesn't. He got up, went to the grey plastic box on the beam by the drum, the one with the red light and the little steel arm, and followed its wire with his finger to where it went into the cabinet, and pulled it out by the plug.

The red light went off. The drum went on turning. In the vent slot the green clock went on counting, 01:20:31, 01:20:32, without the slightest interest in what he had done. The box was only a pair of braces. The belt was inside the bronze.

He put the plug back, so the engineer would not come up and wonder, and lay down again.

He took the hairpin out of his robe and unbent the L a little, so the tip was a thin bright hook no wider than a grass seed. He undid his belt, took the steel tongue of the buckle between finger and thumb, slid it into the bottom of the keyway as a tension wrench, and leant on it, very gently, the way you lean on a sleeping child to see if it is really asleep.

Then he touched the first lever.

It rang.

It still went through him: a tiny clear note, high and pure, hanging in the bronze. He lifted the lever a hair. The note went sour. It rubbed on something and turned to a dull buzz, like a fly in a jar. He lifted it another hair.

The note came back, clearer than before, rounder, and stayed.

He lay very still with his heart going.

"Oh, you clever, horrible family," he whispered.

Every lever was a little bell. That was the whole of it. A bell founder had built this, a man who had spent his life listening to bronze, and he had cut every lever to ring its own note. At the wrong height a lever leant on its neighbour and choked. At the right height it hung free in its gate and sang. And the bolt would only move when every one of them hung free at once, all singing together, like a peal.

There was no picking it by feel. Every lever felt the same. You had to tune it.

He counted them by touch, front to back. Twenty-four. Of course there were twenty-four. One for every hour of an ordinary day.

He started at the back, where the levers were longest and the notes lowest, and worked forward. Each one he lifted until the sour buzz went out of it and the clear note came, then he leant on the buckle a hair more to hold it there, and moved on. It was slow. He had to learn every note before he could find it. Four. Five. The ones he had set sang quietly behind the ones he was setting, so the cabinet began to hum to itself, very low, a chord with notes missing.

Seven. Eight.

On the ninth his hand shook, the pin slipped, and every lever behind it dropped with a single soft clang like a dinner gong in another house. The hum stopped.

Dec lay on the floor and looked at the dark underside of the cabinet and said a long, quiet sentence in Irish that his grandmother would have taken a spoon to him for.

02:31. The green clock in the slot said 01:31:02.

He started again from the back.

It went faster the second time, because now he knew the first eight notes the way you know the first bars of a song, and his fingers went to them without asking. One. Two. The hum came up under his hands again. Six. Eight. Nine sang and held, and he breathed out through his nose, very carefully, as if the lock could hear him. Ten. Eleven.

He heard them before he saw them, because he had been listening for them all week.

Feet on the main stair, coming up round and round, neat and unhurried. And at the very same moment, behind the low iron door he had come in by, feet on the prison stair: the same tread, the same weight, the same pace, as if one man were climbing two staircases.

02:44.

If he took the pin out, eleven levers would fall, and there would not be time to lift them again. So he did not take it out. He held the buckle tongue steady with one hand, and with the other he reached for Šťastný's key ring, sixty keys, heavy as a bag of sugar, and hung it by its ring on the end of the tongue where it stuck out of the bottom of the keyway. It swung once and settled. Its weight leant on the bolt for him, steady as a hand. Above it the eleven levers went on singing, very low, to nobody.

He had once held a jeweller's safe in Salthill that way while he went out to the van for a sandwich.

He got up and left the hairpin in the keyway like a flag on a hill.

The clockwork room was long and narrow, and the frames of wheels ran down its middle, so that between them and the wall there was one gangway, the width of one man, from one door to the other. Halfway down it, by the window, sat the alcove where the procession drum turned in its iron cradle under the beam.

Dec stood in the gangway in front of the alcove, in his short grey robe, hands at his sides.

"Come on, so," he said.

Both doors opened at once.

In the doorway at the main stair stood a shaven head in a grey robe with the hood thrown back, the watch on his right wrist. In the iron doorway behind Dec, the same, the watch on his left. They looked at him, then past him, at each other, along the gangway, the way he had seen them look along the deck of a paddle steamer, like the two weights of a scale.

For once they did not walk.

Something had told them there was no time; a telephone, a watch, a woman's voice. They came at him from both ends at once, fast, heads down, black gloves out, the same long stride, the same breath, like a man running at a mirror.

Dec had spent a week waiting for this. He knew they would arrive together, because they always did. He let them come. He let them come until he could see the stitching on the gloves.

Then he stepped back into the alcove, beside the drum, the way you step into a doorway to let a tram go by.

They met in the middle.

There was a sound Dec would remember for the rest of his life. Not loud. A deep, round, two-note sound, like two church bells being introduced to each other at a party. The left-wristed twin sat down on the floor. The right-wristed twin sat down facing him. Knee to knee they sat, with exactly the same expression, and each of them put one gloved hand to the front of his own shaven head, in exactly the same way.

"Sorry, lads," said Dec. "Physics."

He knelt between them, quick and polite, and untied the left boot of the one and the right boot of the other, and tied the two sets of laces together, tight, twice, the way his uncle in Spiddal tied hay on a trailer he did not mean to lose on the road. It was only fair. The last time laces came into this business, it had been him in the hold of a coach.

The twins watched him do it, holding their heads. Neither of them said anything. Neither of them, Dec thought, could have said for certain what day it was.

"That'll hold you a while," he said. "Mind you don't both get up at once. You've a habit of it."

He went back to the cabinet on his knees. 02:47. The green clock said 01:47:20. Thirteen minutes, and thirteen levers still to find.

The keys hung where he had left them, quite still. In the bronze, very faintly, eleven levers were still singing.

He lay down, took the buckle tongue between finger and thumb, and lifted the ring off it as gently as a man lifting a sleeping cat off a newspaper. The chord wavered and held. He laid the keys on the boards by his ear and took up the pin.

This time he talked while he did it, because a nervous man who explains locks is a man whose hands are steady, and because the twins were the only audience he had.

"It's a bell, you see," he told them, lying on his back with the pin in the keyway. "Your boss's family. They think in bells. A bell's not one note, it's a lot of notes that agree with each other. So every lever's a little bell, and you have to set them, one at a time, so they agree." The twelfth lever sang. "There. Don't ask me what that one's called. I only know the hum note, at the back, the deepest, and the strike." The thirteenth. "Your grandfather, or whoever, he tuned all these. Twenty-four of them. That's a year's work, that is. That's love. Horrible love, but love."

Behind him the twins breathed together.

The chord grew under his hands, low and warm, full of beats and shimmer, like the inside of a bell just after it has been struck. Through the greenish window, faintly, from the square, came the sound of a hundred people singing a slow grey hymn. The hooded ones had come up. For one mad moment the lock seemed to be humming along.

Fourteen. Fifteen.

Then, in the gap between one lever and the next, he heard feet on the main stair.

Not the twins. The twins were sitting knee to knee on the floor, tied together by the boots. These feet were lighter, one pair, quick and sure, in good shoes, coming up the worn stone round and round as if they had all the time in the world, because their owner always had.

Dec did not take the pin out. He held the buckle tongue where it was, with fifteen levers singing in the dark above his face, and turned his head on the boards.

Renata Hollerová stood in the doorway in her fitted grey robe, with the bronze bell at her throat and a little colour in her cheeks from the climb. In her right hand, held low and easy, the way other women hold a programme at the opera, was a small black pistol.

She looked at the twins on the floor, and at their boots, and her eyebrows went up a quarter of an inch. Then she looked at Dec, on his back under her family's lock, with his belt undone.

"Mr Hartley," she said warmly. "Do keep still. My grandfather took two years to tune that, and I'd hate you to hear it go flat."
