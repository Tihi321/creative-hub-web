Romanesque

Major Kroupová kept a cactus on her desk. Nothing else in that room had ever been fed.

Eight o'clock Saturday morning, a police station in the Old Town, at the end of a corridor painted the green of hospital soup. The major still wore her grey parka, zipped to the chin, as though her own office were a place she was only passing through. A plastic cup of water stood on the desk. Beside it, a typed sheet.

She read it out in the flat voice of a form. "'Reported by Mr Šťastný, twenty-two fourteen hours. A door was opened. A chain was cut. Nothing was taken.'"

"Dagmar was taken," said Šťastný.

He had come in his cap and his blue overalls. The cap had stayed on his head, and he had not sat down. He stood beside Inès's chair with his fists in his pockets, and his white moustache bristled like a dog's back.

"Mrs Vaňková is an adult woman," said Kroupová. "Adult women leave flats."

"Without the coat?"

"Also without coats." She turned the sheet over. "Your neighbour saw two men on the stair at half past seven. Your neighbour is ninety-one. Last year she reported that the Pope was living in the flat below her." Her eyes came to Dec. "The two men you describe were on the boat from eight in the evening until midnight. I watched them all evening, Mr Hartley, because you watched them all evening."

"I have also rung Mrs Vaňková's private number," Kroupová went on, as if granting them something. "It answered. It told me the time." She paused. "It was correct."

"So you'll search PragClear," said Inès.

No expression at all crossed the major's face. She let the silence sit there.

"PragClear pays the salaries of half the country, Madame Varga. It pays mine." She folded the sheet in two. "I will not search the largest clearing house in Central Europe on the word of a French restorer, an Irish locksmith and a speaking clock."

"And the keeper of the Orloj," said Šťastný, swelling.

"And the keeper of the Orloj," Kroupová agreed, "whose neighbour reports the Pope."

Behind them the door opened, and Commissaire Pichon came in sideways, a paper bag in one hand and a telephone in the other, icing sugar down the front of his raincoat. He set the bag beside the cactus and held up one finger for silence.

"Yes. Pichon. Commissaire Pichon, Police Judiciaire de Paris." His public voice, the one he kept for prefects and the dead. "I wish to request, in the name of European cooperation in matters of heritage, an immediate..."

The phone said something small and pleasant. Pichon listened. His moustache moved once.

"I see. Yes. Thank you." He lowered the phone and stared at it. "He tells me to enjoy the conference."

"Who does?" said Dec.

"A deputy director of something. Very kind. Today the delegation visits a brewery in Smíchov, with lunch." He put the phone away with great care, as if it might go off. "I shall enjoy it. I shall enjoy it so much that they will be sorry."

Kroupová opened the paper bag, looked at six small round pastries with plum jam in the middle, and closed it again without taking one.

Outside on the steps, in a thin rain that had nearly made up its mind to stop, Pichon took Inès gently by the elbow.

"They will be watching me," he said quietly, in French. "A policeman at a brewery is a policeman who is not anywhere else. That is the point of the brewery." His little glasses came round to her, and his eyes went sharp the way they did. "Find me something I can put on a plate, Madame Varga. Something printed, or signed. Then I will eat it in front of her."

Šťastný did not go back to his clock. He crossed the Old Town Square through the tourists and the umbrellas without seeing any of them, at a short furious stride, as if the form were still in his fist.

"Forty years I keep the clock," he told the cobbles. "They give me a formulář. I keep it. I wipe the oil with it."

"Where are we going?" said Inès.

"Down."

He turned into the narrow passage that ran beside the Town Hall and stopped halfway along at a door Inès had walked past twice that week without seeing. Low and black it was, banded with iron, at the bottom of three worn steps below the level of the street. Šťastný took out a ring of keys the size of a saucer.

"In eighty-seven the river comes up. The council gives me the keys, for the water. Nobody asks for them back." The lock turned with a sound like a cough. "The wire from my clock goes down through the floor. I think: a wire goes somewhere. Where a wire goes, a man can go."

"You never went," said Dec.

Šťastný glared up at him from under the cap. "I am seventy-four. I go now."

The steps went on down past the place where steps should stop. Šťastný found a switch, and a chain of bare council bulbs came on one after another along a vault, as if somebody were walking ahead of them lighting candles.

Inès had seen cellars. Paris was built on them. This was no cellar.

It was a room. A vaulted ceiling of rough pale stone sprang from one fat round column in the middle like the branches of a stone tree. In the far wall stood a doorway with a round arch and a stone threshold worn into a dip by feet. High up on one side, very carefully made, a small round-headed window looked out at solid earth.

"A fenébe," said Inès softly.

"Eight hundred years ago this is the street," said Šťastný, and slapped the column as if it were a horse. "This door, you walk in from the street. This window, you look at the street. Then the river comes in, every spring, and the people are tired, and they put earth on everything, three metres, and build on top. Prague is a town standing on a town. Up there they sell tourists little wooden Deaths with batteries in. Down here is the real one."

He ducked under the next arch. "Short people," he said, with approval. "Romanesque people are short. Better people. Mind the head."

Dec minded it, and hit it anyway on the next arch, and said a word in Irish that Inès suspected was very old indeed.

They went on through room after room. One still smelt of potatoes. In a low passage someone had stuck chewing gum on a twelfth-century pillar, and Šťastný stopped and looked at it and said nothing at all, which was worse than shouting. All the way along, at the foot of the wall, clipped to the stone every metre with neat new plastic clips, ran a black cable as thick as Inès's wrist.

The chapel was the last room and the largest, and somebody had swept it.

Every other room wore a skin of grey dust on the floor. This one was clean to the stone. Fat church candles stood in iron holders round the walls, burnt down an inch, and the wax still soft at the rim when Inès touched it.

On the end wall a clock had been painted on the old plaster, gold laid over a red ground the way the old masters did it, and she could not help seeing that it was good work. Roman numerals and a single hand, and at the top, where twelve should be, stood IX. Below it stood nine black high-backed chairs in a shallow curve, each with a numeral cut into its top rail, I to IX.

"The parish council," said Dec quietly.

Chair V had a grey velvet cushion. On chair VII a small white card was propped against the back, one Czech word on it in steel-grey ink, in a beautiful upright hand.

"Omluven," read Šťastný. "Excused."

"He sent a note," said Dec. "Dear Brotherhood, Auguste can't come to the end of the world, he has a magistrate."

Inès did not laugh. She was looking at the table.

It was a long trestle table under a white cloth, against the side wall, and on it stood a stone urn the size of a child, with a stone lid, and beside the urn, in rows as neat as a cemetery's, a great many small rough grey cups, each the size of an egg cup. She began to count them and stopped at a hundred.

One cup stood on its own at the end of the table, as if someone had set it down and been led away. A half-moon of red lipstick marked its rim.

Dec picked it up and held it to his nose. Then he put it down exactly where it had been and wiped his fingers on his jeans, slowly, one at a time. Inès had seen him make that face once before, over a cup of tea at an inn in the Jura.

"Thursday," she said, lower than she meant. "Or last night."

"Doesn't matter which," said Dec.

It did, and they both knew it, and neither of them said so.

In the corner furthest from the chairs stood the machine: a tape recorder the size of a chest of drawers on wheels, grey steel, with two great spindles and a row of square black keys. A microphone on a heavy chrome stand was angled down in front of it to the height of a seated woman. The black cable ran into the back of the machine, came out again, and climbed the corner of the room in its clips to a raw round hole drilled in the vault. Above that, Inès thought, was the Town Hall, and above that the clockwork room, and a bronze cabinet the size of a fridge.

"That's my cable," said Dec. "The one that goes down through his floor."

The spindles were empty. The lid over the heads was shut with a brass padlock no bigger than a stamp.

Dec dug in his satchel for the cutlery and swore, because it was at the bottom under a rohlík he had taken from the pension breakfast and forgotten. Inès had pinned her fringe back with two black hairpins before they came down, the ones she kept in her trench pocket for leaning over varnish. She took one out and held it out to him without a word.

He bent it into an L with his thumb. The padlock opened in about the time it took her to push her hair back out of her eyes.

Under the lid there was clean steel and no reel at all.

"They've taken her up already," said Dec. He clicked the padlock shut again and slid the hairpin into the pocket of his jeans.

"That is mine," said Inès.

"I'll give it back."

"You will not. You will find it in a year in the washing machine."

Then she heard it.

Very small: a single clear note from behind the machine, like a fingernail on a glass. Then a pause, long enough to forget it, and then the note again.

She went round. There was a crack in the vault there, dark with wet. A drop gathered at the end of it and swelled and let go, and fell into a little stone channel in the floor with that same clear note.

Plink. Silence. Plink.

She had heard it before, in a basement on Vinohradská, behind two minutes of hiss and a slow grey voice reading names.

"This is the room," she said. "The second recording, with the names. That is the drip." She looked at the chrome stand, angled down to the height of a seated woman. "She sat there. They made her read it there. Again, from the beginning, Mrs Vaňková."

Šťastný had taken his cap off. He held it against his chest in both oily hands and looked at the microphone, and for the first time since Inès had met him he looked his age.

Under the table, behind the cloth, was a printer's carton full of folded cards, cream and heavy, each with a small bell embossed on the front in bronze. There were hundreds. Nobody would miss one.

She took one, pushed her glasses up with her knuckle, and read it under the painted clock.

The heading was in Latin, and that at least was hers. "Collectio horae duplicis. The Collection of the Doubled Hour." The rest was Czech, set out like the order of service for a wedding. She gave it to Šťastný, and he read it aloud, translating badly in three languages while she wrote it down.

"Saturday, twenty-four October. Six in the evening. At the Holler Gießerei, the foundry, in Holešovice, the bell of the Collection is poured. The faithful are welcome." He sniffed. "Like a christening. Then the night. At the first two o'clock the faithful come together below." He tapped the floor with his boot. "Here. The cup. The first hour is given. When the hour comes again, the faithful go up to the square. The apostles walk. Death rings." His voice had gone harsh. "When the clock that does not lie strikes two, the Voice speaks from the clock. The names are read. The Collection is made." He turned the card over. "And on the back: the hour is not lost. It is paid."

"The clock that does not lie," said Dec.

"Mine," said Šťastný.

He said it the way a man says my wife, and jabbed a thick finger at the painted IX on the wall as if it had insulted her.

"Tonight every clock in Prague goes two, three, and then two again. Telephones. Computers. Your watch, Schlosser. They jump back like frogs." He made a frog of his hand and jumped it. "My clock does not jump. She keeps winter time all the year, since 1912. At the first two o'clock she says one. She is not wrong. She is waiting. When everybody comes back, she is there already, and she strikes two. One time. The only true two in Prague." He hit his chest. "And they make her ring for these."

"Vox temporis non mentitur," said Inès.

The two men looked at her.

"On Jan's grave. The voice of time does not lie." She looked down at the card. "Either they took it from him, or he took it from them and turned it round."

They went out another way, because Šťastný said nobody with sense left a cellar by the door he came in, and he led them up a stair so narrow that Dec climbed it sideways, to an iron door under an archway in a lane north of the square.

The rain had stopped. The cobbles were drying in pale patches.

Inès came up the last step still turning over the day that was longer than the others. She had turned it all the way through the dark, under the sound of the drip, and it would not open.

"The longest day of the year is in June," she said. "The solstice. That was four months ago."

"Maybe she meant a long one," said Dec.

"She did not write a long day. She wrote the longer day. As if there were only one, and everybody knew it." Inès frowned. "A leap year is longer. But that is a year, not a day."

"Mondays," said Dec.

"Be quiet."

Šťastný had stopped in front of them, very still, looking down.

On the cobbles at the foot of the iron door, so close that all three of them must have stepped over it, someone had drawn a clock in white chalk, the size of a dinner plate. Twelve ticks and no hands, and at the top a careful IX. From its rim a long straight arrow ran away across the stones, up the lane.

Inès crouched and touched the tip of the arrow. The chalk came off on her finger, dry and clean, though the stone round it was still dark with wet.

"After the rain," she said. "It stopped at ten. This was drawn while we were down there."

"At this door," said Dec. "Not the one we went in by. This one."

Nobody said what that meant. Šťastný looked up the lane, the way the arrow pointed, and spat neatly to one side.

"North," he said. "The river. Then Holešovice. Then Holler."

"It's a trap," said Dec.

"It is an invitation."

"Same thing," said Dec, "on better paper."

"We went to the boat." Inès wiped her finger on the mustard scarf, which would not show it, and stood up. "They pour the new bell at six. Jan's bell is in that foundry, Dec, with his words on it. If we wait to be invited properly, they will melt it down to make the new one."

Dec looked at his watch, and then over the roofs at the tower, where the Orloj stood an hour behind him and perfectly sure of itself.

"Then we'd better be early," he said.
