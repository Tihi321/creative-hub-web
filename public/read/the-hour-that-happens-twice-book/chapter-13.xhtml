Pichon Eats Knedlíky

They left the foundry by the plank door at the end of the bell shed, the stoker still singing to his furnace, and went out through the gate past the cracked bell as though someone had sent for them and the errand had gone well.

On the tram back across the river, the other passengers gave Dec room. He could not blame them. The clay had dried on him in the wind and fell off in flakes whenever he moved. An old woman in a knitted hat watched him for three stops, then, without a word, handed him a packet of paper tissues and got off.

"She thinks I'm a golem," said Dec.

"She thinks you are a man who fell in a river," said Inès. "She is being polite." Her phone was already at her ear. "Commissaire. It is Varga. Where are you?"

"I am in a brewery in Smíchov," said Pichon's voice, small and round and full of noise, "with forty policemen from twenty-two countries, and a Dutchman is singing. I have been given a hat."

"You asked me for something printed," said Inès. "Or signed. I have both. Twelve pages, in English, with a V on the cover in her own ink. I have photographed every page."

There was a pause, during which the Dutchman reached a high note. When Pichon spoke again, his public voice had gone.

"There is a beer hall on the embankment below this place. U Tří kohoutů. The Three Cockerels. Nine o'clock. I will bring the major. Bring the old man with the clock. He frightens her."

At 18:01 Dec was standing in the bath at the pension in Liliová, with paní Benešová shouting through the door about her towels, when his phone buzzed on the basin.

A number he did not know.

The pour is going beautifully. I'm so sorry you couldn't stay. Do tell Madame Varga that my printer is temperamental and she must have been very patient with it. Nothing you read changes what time it is. R.

He carried it out to Inès with the water still running grey off his feet. She read it twice. Her colour did not change, which with Inès meant that it had.

"She knows we read it," she said.

"She doesn't care that we read it." Dec sat on the end of her bed, and it complained. "That's the bit I don't like. She's just lost her two best prisoners and she writes like somebody thanking you for the flowers."

Inès was quiet for a moment. "Dec. It is Saturday."

"It is. All day."

"Šťastný has her coat."

Šťastný had the coat folded over his arm when he opened the door in Kožná, the way a soldier carries a flag he has been told to hand to somebody's widow.

"The council sends me a letter," he said, instead of hello. "Dear Mr Šťastný. Tonight the sponsors' engineers will be working in the tower. You are thanked for your forty years and are kindly asked to take the night off." Something went through his moustache that was not a word in any language. "Forty years, they thank me. Like a horse." He passed the coat to Inès.

Camel, old and very good. The fox on its collar had been dead longer than Dec had been alive. In its pocket Inès found a lipstick and a green card printed Domov Na Vrchu, Žižkov, with a column of dates down one side, each with a little rubber stamp. Every one was a Saturday, visiting hours five to seven. The column went back two years and did not miss one. The last stamp was the tenth of October.

"Every Saturday," said Šťastný. "Five o'clock. In this coat. She goes to tell her sister she is her mother." He rubbed his nose with an oily thumb. "You will be late."

The home was a long yellow building at the top of a hill in Žižkov so steep that the tram climbed it on its knees. Inside, it smelt of soup and floor polish.

The nurse on the desk was a big calm girl called Pavla, with good English. She looked at the green card, and at Inès, and at the coat over her arm.

"Paní Vaňková is ill?"

"Paní Vaňková is away," said Inès. "She could not come. I did not want her sister to wait."

"Put the coat on," said Pavla. "She doesn't know faces any more. She knows coats."

So Inès put it on. Too short in the sleeves, too wide in the shoulders, and the fox sat under her black bob like a small sleeping animal. A girl at a funeral, Dec thought, who had borrowed her aunt's coat and her aunt's courage along with it.

Božena Vaňková sat by the window playing patience on a tray. Small and upright, like her sister, but her white hair was cut short as a boy's and she wore a man's cardigan buttoned to the throat. She glanced up from her cards at the camel coat coming in at the door, and her face opened.

"Mami," she said, to the coat.

"It was their mother's coat," said Pavla quietly, from the door. "Whoever wears it is Mami. Paní Vaňková lets her."

Then Božena's eyes went on up, past the fox collar to Inès's face, and narrowed, and she said something short in Czech.

"She says you are not her mother," said Pavla. "Her mother is shorter. And has better lipstick."

"Tell her that is true," said Inès.

Božena looked at the coat again, and at Inès, and pointed at the chair across the tray.

"She says, it's the right coat, so you'll do."

Inès sat down. Božena looked Dec up and down, from his still slightly grey hair to his boots, and addressed him in loud, crisp German.

"She thinks you are one of the Germans," said Pavla. "All the staff are Germans. I have told her I am from Olomouc. It doesn't help."

"Ja," said Dec, who had his German from a ferry.

Božena pointed at the window and gave an order. Inès translated without looking up from the cards.

"The window. It sticks. The Germans have locked it so that she cannot escape. She wants it open."

The window had a restrictor on it, a little steel arm with a lock, so that nobody's grandmother goes out of the third floor.

"Tell her I can't take the lock off," said Dec. "It's the law. But it does stick." He eased the swollen frame and soaped the hinge, and the window swung its permitted four inches as sweet as a door in a palace. Cold air came in, smelling of chimneys.

Božena watched him do it, her head tipped to one side. Then she said something that made Pavla laugh out loud.

"She says, for a German, not bad."

They stayed forty minutes. Božena cheated at patience with great skill and no shame, moving a red seven onto a red eight while looking Inès straight in the eye. She asked after Mother's cough, and Inès said it was better. She told them, with Pavla putting it into English a beat behind, that Mother was on the telephone all day long, telling the whole country what time it was.

"She rings the number," said Pavla quietly. "Every morning. On the phone at the desk. She listens to her sister say the time and then she tells me what time it is, very proud. I let her. It costs twelve crowns."

Božena put a black queen on a red king and said something about money.

"She says on the first her pension comes," said Pavla, "and the Germans take all of it for the room. Mother signs." Božena added something and slapped the tray. "And she says if it ever comes twice, she'll buy a coat like that one and not tell Mother."

Dec looked at the window. Somewhere on Inès's phone, in a column of small print on page three of Annex C, there was an account number that did not belong to Božena Vaňková, and tonight, in the hour that happened twice, her pension would leave the bank a second time and go into it. Somebody in an office would spend a year deciding whose loss that was. It would not be Renata Hollerová who explained it to the woman by the window.

When they rose to go, Božena took hold of the coat's sleeve and did not let go.

Inès looked down at the small hand with its swollen knuckles, holding on. Then she took the coat off, carefully, a sleeve at a time, and laid it across Božena's knees with the fox on top.

"Tell her Mother will come for it," she said to Pavla. "Tell her tomorrow."

Pavla told her. Božena stroked the fox and said something to it, not to them.

"She says Mother never comes on Sundays," said Pavla.

"She will this Sunday," said Inès, and went out into the corridor in her navy trench, which was not warm enough, and walked very fast to the stairs. Dec followed her, and kept his mouth shut the whole way down the hill.

The Three Cockerels was a long vaulted cellar on the embankment, as loud as a railway station and as warm as a bakery, with waiters who carried ten mugs at once and looked at you as though you had insulted their mothers by not ordering.

Pichon was already there, the brewery's paper hat folded into his top pocket and his napkin in his collar. In front of him lay a plate of roast pork, sauerkraut and bread dumplings sliced like a loaf, and he was eating them with an expression Dec had only ever seen before in paintings of saints.

"These," he said, "are dumplings that have been to university." He wiped his moustache. "Show me."

Inès showed him. He put on the reading glasses on their cord and went through all twelve pages with one finger, eating with the other hand, and held his tongue until the last line.

VIII: paid in full. To be remitted after the Collection.

He took the glasses off. "Four days," he said, "this woman has paid for my lunch. Nobody pays for four lunches out of love." He rang Paris. He spoke to somebody he called mon petit, and Dec heard the name Ferrand, and the rue des Rosiers, and at dawn, gently, and with a warrant if you can find a judge who is awake.

"On what grounds?" said Dec, when he had put the phone down.

"Coffee," said Pichon. "In Paris, I am still a commissaire." He picked up his fork. "Here I am a man in a paper hat. So. Tonight at two the Europol delegation takes a night walk of heritage sites. I have just invented it. Forty policemen full of PragClear's beer, on the Old Town Square, looking at a clock."

Major Kroupová arrived at 21:20 in the grey parka, sat as far from the pork as she could get and asked for water. The waiter looked at her as if she had asked for petrol. She read the twelve pages and handed the phone back.

"A folder," she said. "In English. With a letter V. Anyone with a printer can make a V."

Dec showed her the message about the printer. Something moved at the corner of her mouth, and was put away.

"You want me to stop forty-six thousand pensions with this?"

"No," said Inès. "We want you to stand on the Old Town Square at two in the morning and look at a clock."

"As a tourist," said Pichon helpfully, through dumpling.

The major looked at each of them in turn. Then she took out her own phone, old, with a crack across one corner.

"Send me the pages," she said. "To this number. My own." She paused. "Tourists take photographs. Nothing more."

That was the first thing she had asked them for all week. Inès sent them, all twelve, and the four pages of Annex C twice.

Šťastný came last, in his cap and overalls with a black Sunday jacket over them, and drank half a beer standing up. Then he sat, took the beer mat out from under the glass, turned it over, and drew two clocks on the back with a carpenter's pencil.

"Listen. I say it once." He tapped the first clock. "Tonight at two, everybody's two, my clock says one, because my clock is right and the city is on holiday since March. The faithful go down. The first money goes out. Then at three your phones," he jabbed a thick finger at Dec's pocket, "jump back to two. Like frogs. My clock does not jump. And at its two," he drew the hand round on the second clock, "which is now everybody's two, the apostles walk, Death rings, and that box plays Dagi to the bank."

"So from the first two until the second," said Dec, "I've one hour to open a lock I couldn't open in a whole day."

"Fifty-six minutes," said Šťastný, and showed him the pocket watch. "I am fast."

The plan came in pieces across the table, between the dumplings. Šťastný would take them in by the low black door in the passage, down through the cellars, past the chapel, to the old prison stair into the Town Hall, for which he had a key nobody had asked for back since 1987. Dec would go up to the cabinet and stop the tape. Inès would find Dagmar in the chapel, because the rite would want the Voice in the room even if it no longer needed her voice, and bring her up to the cabinet, where the cable ran down to PragClear, and put her on the line.

"And she says what?" said Kroupová.

Inès laid her notebook among the beer rings, open at the line, turned the right way round now in her own small hand.

Gong oznámí . . . hodin, nula minut, nula sekund.

"Like the speaking clock," said Pichon.

"Like the speaking clock," said Inès. "Except for the hour. Someone cut it off the bell twenty years ago."

"So you do not have it," said Kroupová.

"Dagmar has it. She hid it from herself. I will find where."

Šťastný studied the dots in the notebook with the face he kept for his wheels.

"Jan," he said at last, and shook his head. "Jan liked jokes that nobody laughed at for twenty years."

The night tram came over the bridge at 01:10, nearly empty and lit like a fish tank, with the black river sliding underneath.

Pichon fell asleep against the window before the first stop, his raincoat buttoned over a parcel of dumplings the waiter had wrapped in a napkin, his hands folded on top of it as if it might try to leave. Kroupová had gone ahead in her own car. Šťastný had left the beer hall at eleven to fetch his keys and to look at his clock from the square, where nobody could stop him.

"Quarter to two," he had said. "At the door. Three steps down. Do not be early. Early is for tourists."

Inès sat by the window, because she always did. After a while she put her hand on the seat between them, flat, the way she had laid it on the armrest of a coach all the way to the Czech border, and left it there.

Dec kept his eyes on the river. He knew where it was.

At 01:45 by the city, the square was not empty.

They were coming into it from every street, in twos and threes, in ordinary coats, quietly: an old man with a walking stick, a couple in good scarves, a young woman in a red anorak. Their faces stayed turned down. Not one of them glanced up at the floodlit towers. Not one of them met another's eye. They watched the cobbles instead, as if they could see through them to what was underneath.

On every wrist Dec could see, under every cuff, a plain silver watch.

High on the tower, the Orloj's gold hand stood at a quarter to one, and was perfectly sure of it.

Kroupová stood by the statue in the middle of the square in her parka, her cracked phone held up at nothing, a tourist who had come out at two in the morning to photograph a clock. She paid them no attention. Her eyes were on the watches.

Dec and Inès went round into the narrow passage beside the Town Hall, out of the lamplight, to the low black door at the bottom of the three worn steps.

Šťastný was not there.

They waited. A minute. Two. Out on the square, feet went by on the cobbles, soft and many, like rain beginning.

"He said don't be early," said Dec. "He didn't say anything about him being late."

Inès turned on her phone's torch and pointed it down the steps.

On the middle step, upside down, lay a flat cap. Inside it, like eggs in a nest, was a ring of iron keys the size of a saucer. Beside it, standing upright and patient, was a tartan thermos flask.

Of the man who had owned them, there was no sign at all.
