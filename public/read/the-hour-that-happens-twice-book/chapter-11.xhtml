The Bell Foundry

The Holler bell foundry began as a brick works, and it had never got over the demotion.

It stood at the end of a cobbled lane in Holešovice where the river bends: one long hall of black-red brick, a sawtooth roof, a chimney stuck up like a factory's finger making a point. HOLLER ran down the chimney in white letters three storeys tall. Over the gate hung the same iron mark Dec had last seen on a bronze cabinet inside the Orloj. An H inside the outline of a bell.

"Same fella," he said.

"Same family," said Inès. "Three hundred years of it."

At 14:15 the gate stood open for a lorry backing in with a load of split beech, and nobody gave a second look to a tall man and a woman in a mustard scarf strolling in behind it as though they had been sent for. Bells stood about the yard like cattle waiting for a train. One had cracked straight down its side, and a weed grew out of the crack.

The hall's side door wore a cheap padlock painted over so many times it had turned into a kind of sculpture. Dec had it open with the cutlery in under a minute, mostly by scraping off the paint.

"You enjoyed that," said Inès.

"It's the last easy thing that'll happen today. I'm letting myself have it."

Inside, the heat came at them like a dog.

The casting hall was the size of a church and darker than any church he had been in, its skylights black with a century of soot. The furnace ran along one side, a brick hump with an iron door, and a man in a leather apron and ear defenders stood with his back to them, feeding it beech logs. Each time he swung the door open the hall went orange and the roar came out like something slipped off its chain.

The pit lay in the middle of the floor.

A square hole edged with iron, deep enough to drown a bus. Dec stopped at the rim and looked down with his whole heart, because it was the best lock he had ever seen and it had no keyhole. The great mould sat buried to its shoulders in rammed sand, only its crown showing: a dome of grey baked clay as wide as a kitchen table, a round hole at the top like a mouth waiting to be fed. At the bottom of the pit, round about, stood the other parts of other bells: a knee-high stump of brick and wet clay, the start of some bell's inside, and against the wall, hanging from the hook of a travelling crane, a great hollow clay shell in an iron cage, the outside of a bell with nothing in it.

"The cope," said Inès, who had read about it on the tram. "The outside. They build the core, then a false bell of clay over it, then the cope over that. Then they lift the cope, break away the false bell, put the cope back, and pour the bronze into the space it left."

"So a bell's made of a ghost of itself."

"Of course you would say that."

On a slate by the pit, in chalk, someone had written SO 24. 10. 18:00, and under it had drawn a little bell with a cross on top, the way a child draws a church.

"The bell of the Collection," said Inès, looking at the buried crown. "Their christening."

A workbench by the wall held shallow wooden trays like a printer's, and in the trays lay hundreds of letters an inch high, brown wax, the accents sorted into their own little compartments. Dec picked up a wax Ř. A man pressed these onto the false bell, Inès said, and the cope took their print, and the bronze came out with the letters raised.

"So whatever's on a bell," said Dec slowly, "somebody stood here and picked it out of a tray. Letter by letter."

"Put it back."

He put the Ř back, in the wrong compartment, out of spite.

The old bells were through a door at the end of the hall, in a long shed that smelt of damp timber and old bronze. To Dec the bronze smelt like a mouthful of coins. They hung in rows on frames of black oak, dozens of them, from things the size of a bucket to one as big as a wardrobe. Every frame had a little tin label nailed to it, and on every label someone had painted a year in white.

Inès walked the rows, reading them out under her breath. "1912. 1938. 1946. 1968." She stopped at that one. A small, plain bell with nothing on it at all. "Nobody wanted bells that year."

The oldest was in the first row, no bigger than a teapot, gone black-green, with the H inside the bell cast into its shoulder and 1741 on its label. Dec flicked it with a fingernail and it gave a small sour note, like a chemist's shop bell.

"Three hundred years," he said. "She wasn't boasting, then."

"She was," said Inès. "It was also true."

The shed had two doors, one at each end, and Dec noted them: the iron one they had come in by, from the hall, and a plank door opposite, onto the yard. He liked to know where the doors were. Professional failing.

Near the end of the last row Inès stopped, and said no year, and Dec came and looked over her shoulder.

A small bell, no bigger than a coal scuttle, hanging alone on a short frame as if it had been put in the corner for misbehaving. It had never been hung in a tower. There was no wear on the crown, and the clapper still tied up with a twist of old wire. Its bronze had gone the soft brown of a horse chestnut. On the tin label, in white: 1996. PragClear.

Round the bell's waist, cast in small raised letters, ran a line that was easy to read. PRAGCLEAR. PRAHA. MCMXCVI. And under it the H inside the bell.

Round the lip, lower down, where the bronze flared out, ran a second line, all on its own.

"Hold on," said Dec, and crouched, because his knees had decided for him.

It began after a small raised star. Plain capitals, a finger's width high, cast sharp and clean, the right way round, every one of them. He put his fingertip on the star and went round the bell on his haunches, reading as he went, and Inès walked round behind him.

"Dnukes," Dec read. "Alun. Tunim. Alun. Nidoh." He stopped. His finger had come to a place where there were no letters. "Then there's a bald patch. Then... ímánzo. Gnog." His finger arrived back at the star. "That's the lot."

He looked up at her. "That's not a language. That's a man trying to start a motorbike."

Inès gave no answer. She crouched beside him and pushed her glasses up with one knuckle. Then out came her phone and her grey scale card, and she laid the card against the bronze and photographed the whole line, a piece at a time, all the way round, twice. Then out came the notebook, and she copied it, capital by capital, with the accents, the way she copied a signature: slowly, saying nothing, getting it right.

When she had finished, she turned the notebook round and showed him.

DNUKES ALUN TUNIM ALUN NIDOH ▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒▒ ÍMÁNZO GNOG

"You've drawn the bald patch," said Dec.

"I've drawn what is there." She tapped the shaded place. "Ten letters wide. Perhaps nine and a space. Look at it."

He looked. Between NIDOH and ÍMÁNZO the bronze of the lip had been cut back flat and smooth, a strip about the length of his hand. Chisel and file, carefully, by somebody who knew bronze; he could see the faint ridges of the file under his thumbnail. But the cut was as brown as the rest of the bell. The same soft horse-chestnut skin had grown over it.

"That's not this week," he said.

"No. That's years. Twenty years, perhaps more. The patina has grown back over the tool marks." Inès sat back on her heels. "Someone cut it away long ago. Someone who wanted the rest to stay."

"Jan?"

"Or someone who caught Jan." She looked at the star where the line began. "Read it again."

"Again, from the beginning," said Dec, before he could stop himself, and wished he hadn't, because her face closed up like a box.

He read it again anyway. Dnukes, alun, tunim, alun, nidoh, nothing, ímánzo, gnog. It sounded worse the second time. It itched at him the way a lock itches when four pins are set and the fifth won't go.

"Alun's in there twice," he said. "Fella called Alun. Welsh, maybe. They'd have had a Welshman at the opening."

"Be quiet a moment."

He was quiet. There was a little crease between her strong dark brows that he had learned meant something was going on behind them that was not ready yet, and that if he spoke to it, it would go back into its hole.

So he looked at the doors instead, from habit.

And both of them opened.

They opened at the same moment, with the same small sound. In each door, against the light, stood a man in a black overcoat and a grey scarf, with a shaven head and black leather gloves.

They did not hurry. They never hurried. Down the long aisle between the hanging bells they came, each from his own end, at the same unbothered pace, towards the middle, where Dec and Inès crouched by a small brown bell with forty feet of floor between them and either door.

And Dec understood Kessler.

It went into him all at once, like a key turning. A big, sad, bald man in a sweatshirt in La Santé, who wrote one word on a yellow pad and then looked at one door of the visiting room, and then at the other. Two what? Inès had asked him. And Dec had spent four days guessing: two o'clock, the second time, two doors, a beer. It was none of them, not a time and not a riddle. It was a warning from a man who had done this work himself. There are two of them. When you see one of them, you are already caught, because the other one is behind you.

ZWEI.

"Oh, Kessler," said Dec softly. "You could have drawn a picture."

He stood up anyway and put himself between Inès and the nearer one, which was no use at all, since the other was coming up behind her with exactly the same face. He thought about pulling a frame of bells down across the aisle, and thought about it so long that the twin arrived, stopped a yard away, and held out one gloved hand, palm up, as patient as a cloakroom attendant.

The watch on that wrist was on the left. The other one, Dec knew without looking, wore his on the right.

"Phone," said Inès quietly. "He wants your phone."

"I know what he wants."

He gave it. The gloved hand closed over it and it went into the black overcoat. Behind him he heard Inès's phone go the same way, and the soft click of the grey scale card falling on the floor, and the twin behind her stooping, picking it up, and politely handing it back to her.

Then the gloved hand came out again, palm up. It did not point at the satchel. It did not need to.

Sooner or later, said Vaulmont's warm-oil voice in his head, somebody always asks a locksmith to hand over his tools.

Dec took the cutlery out of the satchel. A roll of brown canvas, gone soft and grey at the edges, eighteen picks and two tension wrenches inside, and his father had given him the first three of them when he was eleven. He held it a moment. Then he put it in the glove.

The twin unrolled it, looked down the row of steel, nodded once, as if to a colleague, and rolled it up again, and that was the worst thing anybody had done to Dec all year.

Then they searched him, quickly and thoroughly, as if from a manual. They took the satchel off his shoulder. They found the brass token in his jacket, looked at the skeleton on it, and put it back. They went through his jeans and found a receipt and a Czech coin, and left them. They looked at his belt for a while and left that too.

Then one of them took Inès by the arm.

It was not rough. It was worse than rough: the way a man takes the arm of an old lady on an icy step. Inès looked down at the gloved hand on her navy sleeve and then up at the shaven head, and said something in Hungarian that Dec was fairly sure had a dog in it.

"Inès..."

"I'm all right." She was white to the lips and her voice was perfectly level. "Dec. Don't be brave in the stupid way."

"What other way is there?"

She did not answer that. The twin walked her down the aisle, between the bells, out through the iron door into the hall. Dec heard their feet on the iron stair that went up the side of the hall to the top floor: hers quick and light, his steady. A door opened somewhere high up, and shut, and a lock turned. A good lock, by the sound. Four minutes with the cutlery, and the cutlery was in another man's overcoat.

The other twin, the left-wristed one, stood and waited for Dec to work that out. Then he nodded towards the hall.

The pit was hotter than the hall. The air at the bottom of it shook.

They went down an iron ladder bolted to its side, Dec first. At the bottom the sand was soft and black and the walls went up all round like the sides of a well.

The great buried mould of the Collection bell stood in the middle, its clay crown at the height of Dec's head. Past it stood the unfinished core, the knee-high stump of brick and wet clay, waiting for the rest of itself, and above it, hanging on the crane, the empty cope in its iron cage.

The twin pointed at the stump.

Dec looked at it. Then he looked up at the cope hanging over it, a great hollow bell of baked clay, and at the crane's yellow control box on its cable in the twin's other gloved hand, and he understood that too.

"Ah, no," he said. "No, now. You'll want to think about this. I'm a terrible fit. I'm all elbows."

The twin waited.

Dec thought about the ladder. Then he thought about the other twin, upstairs behind a good lock with Inès, and decided he did not want to find out how polite that one was.

He sat down on the stump with his knees up near his ears.

The twin pressed a button. Above him the chain clanked, and the cope came down.

Slowly, swinging a little, it blotted out the skylights, and then the orange glow of the furnace, and then everything. The iron rim of its cage grated on the iron ring round the core. A thump he felt in his teeth. Then the dark was complete, and through the clay he heard the chain going slack, and bolts being driven home round the rim one after another, eight of them, eight blows of a hammer, as steady as a clock.

Dec put his hands out. On every side the clay was hard as a flowerpot and curved in over his head. Warm. Under him the core was softer, crumbly, smelling of straw and wet earth and, faintly, of horse. Above his head, very high, a little round disc of grey light: the hole in the crown of the cope, where the bronze would go in.

He looked up at it for a long time.

Through the clay came the sounds of the hall, thick and far away, as if from another house: men's voices in Czech, the scrape of a shovel, the clang of the furnace door, and then a deep rising roar as somebody fed the fire, and fed it again.

Then, quite close, just above the little grey hole, footsteps on the iron edge of the pit, light and unhurried. The disc of light went dark as somebody leaned over it.

"Mr Hartley." Renata Hollerová's voice came down the hole into the dark, low and warm, in no hurry at all, as if she were telling him where the good cake was. "I'm so sorry about the accommodation. You'll be quite safe there until six. The pour is at six."

The light came back.

"Madame Varga is upstairs," said the voice, a little further off. "She's in the drawing office. She has a lovely view of the pit."
