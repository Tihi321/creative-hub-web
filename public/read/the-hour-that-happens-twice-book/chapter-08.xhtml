The Clock That Runs Backwards

The rules belonged to Inès. She handed them over on the third-floor landing in Kožná at eight o'clock on Friday morning, and Vilém Šťastný listened from his doorway in a string vest, chewing bread, enjoying every word.

"You will wear the headscarf. You will wear the glasses. You will not tell anybody the time." Inès raised a fourth finger. "You will not speak at all."

"Not at all?"

"Not one word. You are the most famous voice in this country. Ask for a tram ticket and the tram will turn round."

Dagmar studied the headscarf. Inès had bought it on Thursday afternoon, along with a grey jumper, flat shoes, a toothbrush and the dark glasses, and none of the eleven items Dagmar had dictated, three of which were lipsticks. Brown cloth. Small yellow horses galloping across it.

"I look like a woman who sells eggs," said Dagmar.

"Good."

"I have never looked like a woman who sells eggs. Not in sixty-eight years."

"Then nobody will look for you in one." Inès tied the scarf under the famous chin herself, firmly, and settled the glasses on the famous nose. Behind the dark lenses the red mouth stayed visible, painted at dawn in Šťastný's bathroom mirror, and it was not coming off for anyone. "The lipstick?"

"The lipstick," said Dagmar, "stays."

Šťastný laughed through his bread. First time Inès had heard him laugh. He went back inside and slammed the door.

Dec had left at seven with a flask of tea and Šťastný's spare keys, to lie on his back in front of the bronze cabinet and listen to its levers ring. Inès had told him not to touch it. He had promised to only look at it very hard.

The rules survived as far as Maiselova street.

They crossed the Old Town in the thin morning, Dagmar's arm through Inès's, and Dagmar kept her mouth shut for twelve minutes with visible effort, like a woman holding in a sneeze. Then they passed a café with its door propped open and coffee coming out of it, and she stopped dead.

"One coffee," she whispered. "Inès. I am sixty-eight years old. I have been abducted once from my own sofa and very nearly again from my own kitchen."

So they went in. A narrow place, zinc counter, a young waiter with a ponytail drying cups. Inès ordered in English. The waiter nodded and turned to the machine, and Dagmar, who could not help herself, leaned across the counter and said softly in Czech that she would like hers with a little hot milk on the side, please.

The cup hit the floor.

The waiter didn't look down at it. He spun round with his mouth open and stared at the headscarf, and an old man by the window lowered his newspaper and announced, loudly enough for the whole room, "Přesný čas!" Then he began to laugh, and a woman at the back stood up to see.

Inès paid for two coffees nobody drank and took Dagmar out by the elbow.

"I said please," said Dagmar on the pavement, with dignity. "It was only a little milk."

"It was the voice of God asking for a little milk."

Dagmar thought about that. Under the yellow horses, she looked pleased.

"Renata's men," Inès said, walking. "The two from yesterday. Are they her brothers?"

"Brothers?" Dagmar was offended enough to forget the whisper. "Renata has no brothers. Renata has employees. Their mother cleaned for her father." Down went the voice again. "They are brothers to each other. That is enough for anybody."

The Jewish Town Hall stood on its corner in Josefov, pink and white like a cake left out in the rain, the steep brick gable of the old synagogue alongside it and a little wooden clock tower on the roof. Two clocks. The upper one, in the tower, had Roman numerals and kept summer time like every other clock in the city except the Orloj: it said twenty to nine. The lower one sat in the gable beneath, and it had been stopping tourists on this corner for three hundred years.

That dial had no numbers. It had letters instead, small black Hebrew characters set round the rim where the numbers should be. Inès looked at them, and then at the hands.

Her eye did what eyes do with a thing that is wrong. It told her the clock was broken. The small hand sat in the wrong place for twenty to nine, and the large one, while she watched, crept the wrong way. Not broken. She made herself stand still and let the clock be what it was. The letters ran round the rim from right to left, and the hands followed them: anticlockwise, the way a Hebrew reader's eye travels along a line.

"It isn't backwards," she said, half to herself. "It's only backwards to us."

Dagmar, forbidden to speak, made a small impatient noise in her throat that plainly meant and so?

Inès was thinking about signatures.

Two hundred of them, perhaps, she had cleaned under varnish and soot and the thumbs of three centuries of owners, and she had learned early that you do not read a damaged signature with your eyes. You read it with the hand that made it. Upside down, in a mirror, it made no difference: a man's signature had a tune: the push and drag of the pen, the place where he always lifted, the flourish he always overdid. You knew whose it was before you could read one letter.

On Thursday morning in the flat she had heard a tune in the tape. Sounds had swelled up out of nothing and stopped dead. There had been breaths in the wrong places, and words sucked back into the mouth.

Behind them, over the roofs of the Old Town, a church bell struck once. One deep note, hard strike first, then the long fading tail.

In her head she heard it the other way round. Tail first, swelling out of nothing, then the strike, then silence cut off as if with scissors.

"A fenébe," said Inès softly.

Dagmar took off her dark glasses to look at her.

"Your tape," said Inès. "It isn't a language. It's you, in Czech, backwards. You recorded yourself and turned it round, so anybody who found it would hear a mad old woman talking in her sleep." She pointed up at the gable, at the hands creeping the wrong way past the letters. "Jak židovské hodiny. Like the Jewish clock. You told yourself how to read it. It has to run the other way."

Dagmar stood on the corner of Maiselova and Červená in a headscarf printed with horses and stared up at the clock. Then she put her glasses back on, very carefully, and spoke for the first time in ten minutes.

"Bohouš," she said.

Bohumil Kratochvíl was eighty-one and had spent fifty years in the basement of the Czech Radio building on Vinohradská, first as a sound technician, then as a man nobody had the heart to retire. He met them at the staff door at half past nine in a brown cardigan with leather elbows, took one look at the egg-seller's headscarf, and kissed both of Dagmar's hands, all ten rings, one after the other.

"Dagi," he said. "Dagi, Dagi."

His archive was a long, low room with a hum in it. Shelves of grey tape boxes went back into the dark, and there were three tape machines the size of dishwashers, a desk with a kettle, and a calendar from 1989 that nobody had taken down. He made tea before he would look at anything, and Inès caught herself sniffing hers before she drank, like Dec. It tasted of nothing but tea.

Bohouš spoke good, old-fashioned English, learned, he said, from the BBC World Service under a blanket in 1968.

"I recorded her," he told Inès, threading the reel onto the biggest machine with fingers that shook until they touched the tape, and then did not shake at all. "The speaking clock. 1986, the new version. Every number, one by one. Nula to padesát devět for the minutes and seconds, hours to twenty-three, because a speaking clock never needs a twenty-four. Two weeks in Studio 4. She was perfect. Every take. Never once said a number wrong." He looked at Dagmar over his glasses. "She was a terrible person to have lunch with. Always complaining about the soup."

"The soup was a crime," said Dagmar.

He played the reel forward first, a few seconds, and the room filled with gibberish: Dagmar's velvet voice swelling and gulping and stopping dead. Bohouš listened with his head on one side and began to smile.

"Two machines and a cable," he said. "I taught her that. 1979, a radio play about a ghost in a castle. Record forward on one machine, play it backward into the other." He shook his head with deep pleasure. "Forty-six years, and she remembers."

"I don't remember," said Dagmar. "That is the whole trouble."

Bohouš wound the reel back to the start, lifted it off, turned it over like a pancake, threaded it again the other way up, and pressed the key.

A hiss. Then, clear and crisp and close to the microphone, the way she must have spoken in Studio 4 in 1986, came Dagmar's voice, speaking Czech, quick and fierce and entirely sane.

Dagmar sat down in Bohouš's desk chair without being asked, one hand at her throat.

It was not a long message. Inès understood none of it but two names and a word she had learned that week, hodiny, clock. The voice stopped. The room was quiet except for the hum. Bohouš studied the floor, embarrassed, the way a man does who has overheard a woman talking to herself.

"Again," said Dagmar.

He played it again. This time, under her breath, half a second behind her own voice, Dagmar put it into English for Inès, one phrase at a time.

"Dagmar, you goose. You fool. Jan's words are on his bell. At Holler's." She stopped, swallowed, went on. "Read them like the clock in Josefov." And then, slower, as the voice on the tape slowed: "And remember the day that is longer than the others."

The hiss ran on.

"His bell," said Inès. "Jan had a bell?"

"I don't know." Dagmar stared at the turning spools. "Holler's is the foundry in Holešovice. Renata's family. They have cast bells in this country for three hundred years." Her rings had begun to click together in her lap. "If Jan put his words on a bell at Holler's, he put them in the one place Renata would never look. Her own house."

"Read them like the clock," said Inès. "Backwards, then. Whatever is on the bell is written backwards." She wrote the whole message in her notebook, in Dagmar's English, word for word, and underlined the day that is longer. It sat on the page beside the diary's line, ten delší den, and meant nothing to her yet, which she disliked.

Bohouš was frowning at the machine. "There is more," he said. "A good archivist listens to the whole reel."

He turned the reel over again, right way up, and let it run forward past the place where the gibberish ended. The hiss ran on for a minute, then two, and Inès was about to tell him it was blank.

Then the sound changed. The hiss grew deeper and harder, as if the walls of the room on the tape had turned to stone, and a faint echo lay on everything, and somewhere far off a drip of water. Bohouš sat up.

"Different machine," he said quietly. "Different room. A good machine. Stone walls. A cellar." He looked at Dagmar. "Not your flat, Dagi. Your flat has curtains."

A voice began to speak, and it was Dagmar's, and it was not.

It was slow and thick, as if her tongue were too big for her mouth. It dragged each word out and let it fall, with long breaths between, and what it read was a list. A name, a year, a string of numbers. Another name, another year, another string of numbers. The velvet was still underneath, but it had worn thin and grey.

Inès did not need Czech for this. She had seen the sheaf with the black clip on the low table in the flat, as thick as a telephone directory.

"Vaněček, Josef. Devatenáct set třicet osm..." said the slow voice on the tape. "Vaňková, Božena. Devatenáct set padesát tři..."

Dagmar made no sound at all. She took off her dark glasses and laid them on the desk, and sat with her hands flat on her knees and her eyes on the turning spools.

On the tape the slow voice faltered and stopped. A little silence full of stone. Then another woman spoke, close to the microphone, not loud: calm, low, pleasant, with all the time in the world.

"Znovu, od začátku, paní Vaňková."

The reel ran out of sound into hiss, and Bohouš, after a moment, reached out and stopped it.

"Again," said Dagmar, to nobody. "From the beginning, Mrs Vaňková." Her voice was very steady and very low. "That was Renata."

Inès looked at the reel and thought with the cold, clear part of her mind, the part she used for forgeries. It gave her the answer whether she wanted it or not.

Perhaps on Thursday night they had asked for the phrase and got nothing, because Dagmar had hidden it too well. That was not what they had come for. They had come for the Voice. They had taken her somewhere with stone walls and made her read their list, slowly, the way you make a drunk read a breathalyser card, and from the beginning again when she stumbled, until they had every name in her own voice on a good machine. Somebody had tested the levels on the nearest reel to hand, heard gibberish on it, and thought it rubbish. And somebody, in the small hours, had carried it home in a coat pocket and hidden it behind a pendulum, and could not now remember doing it.

They had what they needed. On Saturday night the Voice would read the names whether Dagmar Vaňková stood in front of the Orloj or not.

They did not need her any more. They did not need her alive.

Inès said nothing. She looked at Dagmar, and saw that she did not need to.

Dagmar picked up her glasses and put them back on. She retied the headscarf with the yellow horses, tight, under her chin. Then she took a gold lipstick out of her coat pocket, uncapped it, and did her mouth in the reflection of the tape machine's steel lid, two strokes to each lip.

"Bohouš," she said. "Make me a copy. Make me three."

They left Dagmar in Kožná at one o'clock, with Šťastný, three copies of the tape and the chain on the door, and Inès walked back alone to the pension in Liliová, a narrow yellow house where the landlady, paní Benešová, had written their names in a ledger with a fountain pen and told them that breakfast was at seven and not at five past.

Paní Benešová was waiting in the hall, with an envelope in her hand and the expression of a woman who did not approve of it.

"For you," she said. "And for the Irish."

A thick, square envelope of cream laid paper, heavy as a tile. On the front, in steel-grey ink in a beautiful upright hand: Mr Declan Hartley & Madame Inès Varga, the name of the pension, the street. Nobody in Prague but Šťastný and Dagmar knew where they were staying. Inès had not told even her mother.

"Who brought it?"

"A man." Paní Benešová folded her arms. "He did not say anything. He held it out. When I took it, he went."

Inès opened it with her thumbnail on the stairs, because she could not wait for a knife.

Inside was a single engraved card, with a small bronze-coloured bell embossed at the top.

Renata Hollerová requests the pleasure of the company of Mr Declan Hartley and Madame Inès Varga at the PragClear Foundation charity evening, aboard the paddle steamer Vyšehrad, Rašínovo nábřeží, this evening, Friday 23 October, at eight o'clock.

Underneath, in the same steel-grey ink and the same beautiful hand, someone had added a line.

Black tie. Mr Hartley may come as he is.
