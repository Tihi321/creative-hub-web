Eight of Everything

Madame Ardouin counted keys the way other people said grace.

"Un," she said, and lifted the first new key off Dec's palm with two fingers, as if it were still warm from a stranger's pocket. "Deux. Trois."

Eighty-four years old, widow of a judge, second floor of the building in the rue Vieille-du-Temple, directly under the atelier. Last Tuesday she had climbed the four flights in a hat to ask whether the words et serrures on the sign by the street door were a joke. Dec had painted them himself, one Sunday in August, under Varga & Hartley, restauration, with no talent at all. Very small letters, she had said, for something that was not a joke.

Now she stood in her doorway in pearls and a grey cardigan, and Dec knelt at her feet on a sheet of yesterday's Le Parisien, her old lock in pieces and the screws laid out in the order they had come out, the way his father had taught him on the floor of the shop in Dominick Street.

"Quatre. Cinq. Six. Sept." She paused, and took the last one. "Huit."

Dec dropped a screw.

"Eight," he said.

"Eight," agreed Madame Ardouin. "One for each day of the week, and one for emergencies."

"There are seven days in a week, madame."

"I was married to a judge for fifty-one years, Monsieur Hartley. Not to an imbecile." Seven of the keys went into her cardigan pocket. The eighth stayed in her fist. "My husband had eleven keys cut to this door in his lifetime. I know where ten of them are."

"And the eleventh?"

"The eleventh," said Madame Ardouin, "is why you are on my carpet."

The new cylinder was Swiss, six pins and a sidebar, the kind of lock that made other locksmiths go quiet at parties, and it slid home with a soft, expensive click he felt in his back teeth. He would have liked to explain the sidebar to her. She could see that, and was not going to allow it.

She tried the key five times, out, in, turn, back, with her head on one side, and found nothing to hold against it.

Then she looked past him, down the stairwell, at nothing, and said in a different voice: "My husband sat on the bench for forty years. He used to say there were men in this city who wore watches with no hands. He said a judge should never look at their wrists in court, because then they knew that you knew." She turned the eighth key over in her fist. "I read the newspapers, Monsieur Hartley. I know about the clockmaker in the rue des Gravilliers. And the Panthéon."

For a moment the stairwell smelt of floor polish and of nothing else at all.

"Next Sunday the clocks go back," said Madame Ardouin briskly. "An extra hour. I shall spend it listening for the eleventh key." The door shut. Through it he heard the bolt go home, and then her voice, very low, counting to eight again.

Inès was waiting on the half-landing above with two coffees and the expression of a woman who had heard all of it through the floor.

"Eight," said Dec.

"I heard."

"She wanted eight keys. Out of nowhere."

"She is eighty-four and she is frightened of a key," said Inès. "It is not a sign. Drink your coffee."

He sniffed it before he drank, a six-month-old habit, and she watched him do it and said nothing, which was how he knew she still did it too.

"In June," he said, climbing, "you told me everything was a sign."

"In June I was wrong about the Métro."

They had been wrong about a great deal since April. The parcel had come by a courier who would not say who sent him: an enamel clock face out of a broken carriage clock, the size of a saucer, twelve black marks, no hands, a careful IX where the twelve should be, and under it, in grey ink, HUIT. Eight. No letter. No name. Pichon's laboratory found no fingerprints and an ink you could buy in any stationer's in France.

So in June they had ridden line 8 of the Métro from Balard to Pointe du Lac and back, twice, looking for anything with a nine on top. In July, in a heatwave, they had walked every street of the eighth arrondissement, where the only clocks were in jewellers' windows on the Faubourg Saint-Honoré and each cost more than Dec's father had earned in a year. In August they had eaten at a restaurant near the Bourse called Huit, eight courses, each about the size of a postage stamp, two hundred and forty euros, and the waiter had never heard of a brotherhood of anything, or, as far as Dec could tell, of portions. In September eight clockmakers from a list in Anselme Roux's spidery hand had turned it over, turned it back, and said it was worth about twelve euros.

And there had been the stamp dealer.

"You bought a stamp," said Inès, on the fourth-floor landing, as if he had spoken aloud. He supposed he had.

"It was a very good stamp. Monaco, nineteen fifty-something. It's got an eight on it."

"It is a stamp, Dec."

"It's an eight on a stamp."

The atelier smelt as it always smelt, of linseed and solvent and the rabbit-skin glue Inès warmed in a little pot every morning like a witch making soup. The Allegory of the Sundial stood on its easel under its police tape, two-thirds clean now. And on the shelf above Inès's bench, between the glue pot and the photograph of her father in his good Hungarian hat, in its nest of grey tissue, lay the clock face. Some mornings Dec said hello to it.

"Maybe it's a threat," he said. "Did you think of that? Vaulmont, saying, there's eight more of us and we know where you live."

"Vaulmont walked back into his house in the Sologne in May as if he had been out for cigarettes," said Inès, tying on her apron. "He has no passport and a magistrate who telephones every Tuesday to make sure he's still in France. He is the Seventh. He would sign it seven. And the Brotherhood doesn't send threats, Dec. You know what they send. They send you to sleep, and you wake a day later with every clock in the room stopped at nine and your tongue the colour of a pencil, and everything tastes of cloves and slate for a week, and you don't know what you said or who you said it to." She said it flatly, the way she listed pigments. "That's what they did to Roux. And to Morel. They don't post you a clock face from a jumble sale."

"So it's the Eighth."

"We spent a week on the Eighth."

They had. There was the page out of Brother Vey's ledger, from the frozen priory in the Jura, with nine little seals down its margin, one for each of the Nine: the key, the wheel, the closed eye, the scales, the bell, the ladder, the open hand for the Seventh, a crown for the Ninth. And for the Eighth, a mouth. Just a mouth, in brown ink, slightly open.

"A mouth could be anybody," Inès said. "A singer. A priest. A dentist." She pushed her glasses up her nose with one knuckle. "You, God help us."

The answering machine was the last thing in the atelier Dec had not been allowed to fix.

Morel's, to begin with. Beige, older than the wiring, sitting on a filing cabinet by the door with a red 23 blinking on its little grey screen, as it had blinked since about July. Inès used her mobile for everything and would not throw the machine away because it had been Morel's and it worked, and those, she said, were two reasons, and he had none.

At half past twelve, with a jambon-beurre in one hand, Dec pressed play to clear it.

A man selling double glazing, three times, sadder each time. A gallery in Lyon. Inès's mother, Hélène, in a long, cheerful French Dec could follow one word in four of, asking whether Inès was eating and whether l'Irlandais was eating, and whether it was true he had painted the sign himself, because in the photograph it looked very much as though he had. Inès did not look round. The tips of her ears went pink.

The machine beeped. A flat robot voice said: "Mercredi, quatorze octobre. Vingt et une heures."

Then a woman began to speak, and Dec stopped chewing.

Not young. Her voice was low and warm and unhurried, with a plush to it, like a good coat with the nap brushed the right way, and she spoke in a language he had not one word of, every syllable set down in its place. A voice that could have read out a bus timetable and made you want to get on every bus.

And she was, unmistakably, telling him the time.

The shape of it gave her away. One word, then a long number, then a short word, then another short word twice over with a different word after each, falling at the end, the way the lady on the speaking clock at home used to fall at the end when he rang her from the hall phone as a boy, just to hear her, until his mother found the bill. And after a pause as long as a held breath, a gong, soft and deep, like someone striking a brass bowl in another room.

Then a breath, close to the mouthpiece. The small catch of someone about to say something else.

Then a click.

Inès had turned round on her stool with her scalpel in her hand, forgotten.

"Again," she said.

He played it again, and a third time, with a pencil and the back of an invoice, writing down what he heard the only way he could:

GONG OZ-NAH-MEE DVAT-SET YED-NA HO-DIN, NOO-LA MI-NOOT, NOO-LA SE-KOOND.

"It's Hungarian," said Dec.

"It is not Hungarian."

"It sounds Hungarian."

"To you, Danish sounds Hungarian. My father was Hungarian, Dec. If it were Hungarian I would understand it." She came and took the pencil. "It's Czech. My father's cousins in Komárno spoke Slovak to the neighbours, and this is near that and not it." She put a neat line under one word. "Hodin. Hours. That's all I have."

"Well, I have minoot and sekoond," said Dec, "and I'm only a poor Irish locksmith. And noola. Nil. Nothing minutes, nothing seconds. So the dvat-set yedna is the hour. And she says gong at the start, and then there's a gong. She tells you it's coming and then it comes." He felt the little cold climb his spine, the one he got in the last room of a good escape game. "She's saying, at the gong it will be such-and-such hours, nothing minutes, nothing seconds."

"A woman rang a restorer's atelier in Paris," said Inès slowly, "to tell it the time."

"Not just the time." Morel had been proud of this machine in its day and had set it to show everything. Dec pressed its button until the little screen gave him what he wanted, in square grey digits: ME 14/10 21:00:00.

"She rang on the stroke," he said. "To the second. The machine picked up at nine o'clock and nought seconds, and she told it nine o'clock and nought seconds, and then the gong. That number's twenty-one. Has to be."

They both looked, without meaning to, at the shelf. At the white enamel face with its careful IX where the twelve should be.

"Nine o'clock," said Inès.

Down in the street a moped went by, and a woman shouted at it, and the glue pot ticked as it cooled.

"Or," said Inès, "she said the time and Morel's machine agreed with her. Machines are agreeable."

"That machine has never agreed with anybody in its life."

She did not argue, and that frightened him more than anything the voice had said.

Pichon rang at ten past one. Inès put him on the speaker and laid the phone on the bench beside the apple she was cutting into precise eighths, which Dec decided not to mention. Pichon was eating too: the scrape of a knife, a glass set down, the hush of a good restaurant at its busiest.

"Madame Varga. Monsieur Hartley." He said Monsieur Hartley the way a doctor says shingles. "I'm at lunch. I'll be brief, because there's a sole meunière in front of me that has done nothing to deserve my neglect."

"We've had a phone call," began Dec. "A woman, in Czech, we think, telling us the..."

"Monsieur Hartley. This summer you telephoned me about a Métro train. You telephoned me about a restaurant. You telephoned me at eleven at night about a postage stamp."

"It was a very good..."

"So I'll speak, and you'll listen." The knife scraped. "This morning a keeper at Père-Lachaise found a drawing in chalk on a grave in the eighty-ninth division. A circle. Twelve marks. And at the top, where twelve should be, a nine." A pause, for chewing. "He'd read the newspapers in April, like everybody. The twentieth arrondissement rang me, because I am now, apparently, the commissaire for clocks." He swallowed. "The name on the grave is Czech. And I thought, who do I know who has a clock face they will not stop talking about?"

Dec looked at Inès. Inès looked at the answering machine.

"Czech," said Dec.

"Czech. I have the name written on a napkin, which I'm wearing. I'll be at the main gate on the boulevard de Ménilmontant at three, after my cheese. If you happen to be walking past at three, I won't have invited you." Another pause. "Bring the clock face. And, Monsieur Hartley?"

"Yes?"

"Smell your coffee."

The fog came up out of the ground at Père-Lachaise as if the dead were breathing.

Out on the boulevard it had been a plain grey afternoon with chestnuts roasting at the gate. Two hundred yards up the cobbled lanes it was another country. Fog lay between the tombs in soft white rolls, and the chapels came at them out of it one at a time, little stone houses for the dead with rusted doors and stone women weeping on their roofs, and went back into it behind them. Somewhere ahead a man's voice, patient and invisible, was calling, "Mimi? Mimi. Titus. Where's Titus?" and something with a bell on its collar answered him.

Pichon went in front in his beige raincoat, with a black umbrella he did not open and the napkin still tucked into his collar. Neither of them told him. Inès had her mustard scarf wound twice round her neck and the clock face held flat under her arm, the way she held anything old.

"The keeper's gone to look for his cats," said Pichon, turning onto a muddy path between two rows of granite. "He says they're upset. He says they knew."

"Knew what?" said Dec.

"He didn't say. He's a keeper in a cemetery, Monsieur Hartley. They're entitled to say things like that."

The tombs here were newer and plainer, polished slabs with photographs in ceramic ovals and plastic flowers faded to the colour of sweets. Pichon stopped, pointed with the furled umbrella, and stepped aside.

Black granite, polished so smooth the fog lay on it in beads. Plain and narrow, with a slab before it, and a stone vase lying on its side in the wet gravel with its dead chrysanthemums spilled out.

On the slab, someone had drawn in chalk. The damp had softened the lines, but there was no mistaking it. A circle the size of a dinner plate. Twelve short strokes round the rim. No hands. And at the top, where twelve should be, a careful IX.

Dec had seen that circle on the floor of a clockmaker's shop in March, and on a good many things since. One or two mornings this summer he had let himself hope he would never see it again. His stomach went slowly down a stair.

He crouched in front of the stone, the knees of his jeans soaking through at once, wiped the beads of fog off the lettering with the sleeve of his leather jacket, and read it aloud, because somebody had to.

"Jan Vaněk," he said. He got the little hook on the e wrong and knew it. "Nineteen fifty-one to twenty twenty-five. Ingénieur." An engineer. A year in the ground, near enough to the day.

Another line ran under the dates, cut smaller, in the same clean capitals. He wiped it with his cuff, and the fog came back on it at once, and he wiped it again.

2 H 30, LA SECONDE FOIS.

Dec read it twice. Then he looked up at Inès, who had gone very still above him, with the clock face held against her chest.

"Half past two," he said. "The second time."
