The Ladder

The pumping house was not the hospital district. Between the market footbridge and the hospital's basement transformer lay a quarter mile of water that had once been a street, and the water was moving, because the second high tide had come in and the district sat low behind the market with the sea on three sides of it.

They went along the top of the wall.

The footbridge's rail ended at a buttress, and the buttress became a parapet, and the parapet ran along the embankment past the backs of the market sheds with the water on their left running fast and brown and full. Sol went first. Ife came behind with the line coiled over her shoulder and a loop of it paid out in her hand so it could not snag.

The old man from the vending machine had come with them as far as the footbridge's far footing. There he stopped.

"Go back to Mrs. Okafor's," Sol said. "Stand in her window with your lamp and watch the reach and tell the air what you see. The wrench is in the water and the looter's in the dark. Somebody has to be the market's eyes tonight. It isn't climbing. It's watching."

The old man studied her. Rain ran off his chin. "I am an old fool, and I'll stand in a window if it will do any good," he said, and went back along the reach with his coat over his head.

The parapet was a foot and a half wide and wet with rain coming in flat off the reach. Sol kept her eyes on the coping, and Ife kept a hand where Sol could see it.

"You'll tell me if the wall's moving," Ife said.

"The wall isn't moving."

"The wall will move when it wants to."

They came off the parapet above the district wall, and Sol stopped, because the hospital's floodlights were on, and they were on because the emergency line held, and the water beneath them was full of reflections, and it was also up.

The courtyard was old paving and old steps and a service entrance with a hood over it, and the canal came into it from the side where the district met the reach. Under the surface, where Sol was looking, there was a vault lid.

"Listen," she said.

She crouched where the parapet met the service stair and pressed her knuckles to the stone. The transformer under the lid was alive. Not running the wards, that was the emergency line's job, the battery and the generator and the switch-gear up on the upper floors, all of it well above where the water had got. The basement transformer was sitting there waiting to be needed, and the water was on its casing.

A transformer that is well is a quiet thing. It hums, and the hum is even, and through a casing you can feel it living the way a sleeping animal lives. This one was not even. There was a flutter in it, a tick that came and went, and a note over the top of the hum that Sol did not like at all: the thin high note of something tracking across a wet surface, looking for the path that would let it go where it wanted to go.

"How long?" Ife said.

"When it finds a path it'll go up the casing and take the vault and everything on that wall. The emergency line's on the other side of a breaker, so the wards won't go dark all at once. That's what the breaker's for. But the breaker is a man-made thing and it'll hold so long and then it won't, and when it doesn't the battery carries the wards and the battery runs out." She took her hand off the stone. The note stayed with her, the way a note will. "It's not hours. It's this tide."

"How long of this tide?"

"A transformer doesn't promise you. It just gets worse."

"Then where's the switch?"

"The fourth is the pumping house," Sol said, mostly to have it straight in her own mouth. "That's the one I went up the ladder for, and it's off and bound and back, and the market's feed is cut off the hospital's fault the way Dell designed it. But the fourth only takes the market off. The vault's the hospital's own feed, and the water's over the vault now. There's a link on this wall that separates the vault from the courtyard water. Dell told me on the grille at the pumping house, while I was still on his ladder with my hands full. Same man's work, same kind of box, and it's the last switch this side of the reach."

"And this one's in his notebook?"

"No. This one's the one I'm going to make him put in the book."

The link sat on the canal face under the district wall's service stair, in a squat iron box with a grille over it, and the box was bolted high, above the waterline, the way it should be. The trouble was the way to it. The ladder went down from the top of the wall to a landing over the vault, and the landing had gone under when the tide came in and the ladder had gone under with it, so that the lowest part of the climb would be done with the flood running over the rung where her foot would want to be.

Dell had said it was above the flood line, and it was, and above the flood line was not the same as out of reach of the water.

"Half of it's under," Sol said. "The box is above the water, and I can see the box, and the way to the box is wet to the fourth rung."

She counted the rungs that she could see. She counted them twice, because the first count had not been a count, it had been a wish.

Eight dry. Then the water.

"Eight dry rungs and then you're in it to the waist," Ife said.

"Then I'm in it to the waist." Sol got the bag round in front of her and opened the flap and looked in at it: the good torch, the spare battery, the coil of the boy's wire, the roll of tape, the screwdriver set, Dell's notebook wrapped in its oilcloth. "The torch goes in my coat with the lens down. The notebook goes in the bag in the oilcloth and the bag goes on my back with the strap choked up short so it doesn't swing. If I slip, I don't want my hands full."

"If you slip you'll have the line and me."

"You'll have the line and the stone and a barge's worth of water on your boots."

"I've held worse." Ife said it without heat, the way she said most things that were true.

But the water was not what made the climb wrong.

A boat came round the corner of the service entrance.

It came slowly, an electric works boat with a canopy and a searchlight, and the light swept the wall and found them on the parapet and held on them a moment and then swung off and swept the courtyard, taking in the floodlights and the roof and the water over the vault and the ladder. The motor made a low clean sound, the sound of something municipal and well kept, and the sound went out across the courtyard and up the hospital's wall and came back off it.

"Ah," said Ife. "There it is."

The boat came in to the service stair and a man stood up in the bow. He had a high-visibility coat over good rain gear and a clipboard in a clear folder and a helmet with the city works flash on it, and he had the look of a man who had been awake all night and had decided to be correct about it.

Sol watched him stand, and something in her chest that had been holding all night went cold and tight. She had been in the trade long enough, three years of it, to know every flavour of works coat by the silhouette, and this one had rank on it.

"I'm the municipal engineer of works," he called up. "Who's in charge here?"

Plenty of people heard him. The handheld on Sol's hip was live, and Ozzy was on it, and when Sol looked down into the courtyard she saw heads at the hospital's upper windows and a figure at the roof door, and she saw the light of a small lamp in the window across the courtyard.

"I am," Sol said.

Her voice went up small and came back off the hospital wall larger, and she heard it travelling and let it.

The engineer looked at her. He saw the bag on her shoulder and the notebook in her coat and the wet line in Ife's hands, and he took all of it in, and then he unclipped the folder and opened it.

"Name."

"Sol Vieira. Apprentice, third year. I'm Dell Marchetti's—"

"I know whose you are. It's been on every channel since the pumping house." He turned a page. "Miss Vieira, the water over the transformer vault is a live flood hazard, the vault is a municipal asset under a standing no-entry provision, and the installation you intend to access is unpermitted, unrecorded and outside the surveyed plant. I'm required under section fourteen to order you to stand down and to withdraw all persons from the hazard."

A woman spoke from the window across the courtyard before Sol could answer.

She had her child on her hip and her raincoat on over a borrowed dressing gown, and she stood in the light with her lamp held high, and she was the woman from the roof; Sol knew the voice before she knew the face.

"I was on a roof in the drowned reach with a crate that was all my boy's food and my medicines," the woman said. "A bargeman threw me a line and it pulled me straight, and my boy drew breath tonight because of it. The girl's name was on that line. I heard it, and I told the doctor on the ridge that name. And I have been in this courtyard two hours and the water has come up eight inches and the woman who let me in has a husband on the third floor under a heart monitor. You order that girl down and you order the darkness up." She shifted the child, and it was not a gentle shift. "I'm in the window with a light. I'll be here."

The engineer opened his mouth, and the doctor got there first.

He had come out of the hospital's side door with his coat half on and his bag in his hand, and he stood at the top of the service stair in the rain, and he was a tired man being precise.

"My name is on this district's wards," he said. "Thirteen beds. A theatre that's cold and stays cold. A blood fridge that has to hold its temperature or the blood in it is water with iron in it. The vault under this courtyard is the transformer that keeps all of it honest if the emergency line falters, and the emergency line has been carrying the whole district since the first surge. If the transformer goes, the blood fridge goes with it. I've said the rest of it to the board in a letter they'll read on Monday." He looked down at the engineer. "I'm not a man of your trade. I'm telling you what I have under my hands."

The engineer looked at his folder. He did not look comforted by it.

"I'm required to order it," he said. "Stand down. Withdraw all persons from the hazard."

The word went round the courtyard. Sol heard it come back off the hospital wall a beat late and smaller, the way a shout comes back.

She stood on the parapet with the bag in front of her and her hand still in the flap. She did not move. The cold tight thing in her chest had grown a voice, and the voice was saying, he is right, in the ordinary way of it he is exactly right, and if you say the wrong thing now you will be an apprentice who argued with an officer of the works in a flood and there will never be a hall in this city that will have you.

And under that, quieter, the other voice, the one that had been with her since the control room: but the vault is ticking.

She got her hand out of the bag and straightened up.

"You can order me down," she said, "and I can't obey you and keep the wards lit. Both of those are true and I can't make them fit together. The fourth switch is the pumping house and it's done, and the market's feed is off the hospital's fault the same as Dell designed it. The link on this wall is the last one, and it's the one that keeps the vault from the courtyard water. If it stays where it is and the fault finds its path, the vault goes and the district goes dark and the battery on the upper floors runs out before dawn. If I go down, there's a live vault and an unrecorded link and a man's license on the line, and I know it. I'm not asking you to say it's legal. I'm asking you to say what's the same either way."

The engineer looked at her while the rain came down on his helmet and off the brim of it in a line. He was a tired man with a wet sheet of paper and a rule written on it, and Sol could see him working through what he had and finding that he had less than he had thought.

"Miss Vieira—"

Another light came on in the courtyard, and it was not the hospital's. It was a small lamp, a caged work lamp on a long cord held above the heads of three or four people coming along the parapet from the market side, and the person carrying it was a woman in a waterproof cape and rubber boots with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder.

"Who on this wall remembers the culvert at the depot yard?" the old woman said. "Because I do. I remember a bargeman in the yard with her engine under water and a girl up to her chest in the flood with her hands in a busbar, and they bailed another woman's gear with no talk of permits. And I remember my sister's kettle and a boy with his tools. So you'll hear this from me and from everyone at the culvert. There's no section of any code that runs that ladder. The girl runs the ladder. You tell her no and we'll stand here and be the news."

Behind her, on the parapet, there were more of them than Sol had expected. The sister's boy with his canvas tool roll. A man Sol did not know at all, carrying a coil of rope as if it were the only thing he had saved. Two people from the market, lamp-lit and soaked. And at the back, with his coat over his head and a lamp in his hand, the old man from the vending machine, who had come along the wall behind them after all.

They stood along the parapet in the rain, and none of them had a permit to stand there, and all of them stood there.

"Who's in charge of that lamp," the engineer said.

"I am," said the old woman.

"And who's in charge of the rest of you?"

"Nobody," said the sister's boy, and somebody laughed, and the laugh went round the courtyard like a stone skipping.

Ozzy's voice had been climbing under the handheld for a while.

Sol had stopped hearing him properly when the boat came in. The handheld was on her hip and she had been talking over it and it had been talking into her coat, and now she heard it come up into the open air and find a gap in the rain where it could be heard.

"Ahem," Ozzy said, and then, with the broadcaster's rhythm back and the flatness gone, "yes. I have this. I have a canal wall in the hospital district and I have an engineer of works and I have a ladder and I have a window with a lamp in it and a woman in a rain cape at the culvert, and I have most of the district in the handheld, so let's do this properly. Let me ask the open channel one question, and I want it short: if you are listening and you are inside the hospital district's dark zone, say something."

The handheld filled up.

"A night porter at the south gate. There's nine of us here and two patients on the ground floor and the corridor's lit and we want it left that way."

"The old folk at the charity almshouses. There are nine of us and we have candle. We have listened to every word since the waterworks and we have prayed for the girl on the wall and we are not praying for a section number."

"Mrs. Okafor. I'm the one the old fellow shouted to from the hall door with his lamp, and I've got him in my front room now with a blanket and a cup of tea and he won't sit down. He's stood at my window and watched the wall since you came off the parapet, and he says to tell you the looter's skiff went up the far reach at the turn of the tide and it's not coming back tonight. The market's dark but the market's people are up. And I want the girl on the wall left alone to work."

"Tomas Ilyich. Alive. They got a doctor off the ridge to me this evening and the doctor's had a word with me about my ribs and another word with the roofer's union, and if it's the engineer's order that's holding up a switch, then I'm the roofer's union and I'll be at the review."

"Mrs. Okafor again. That's my neighbour, and he's a good man when he's not being a roofer."

The courtyard was quiet. The engineer stood in the bow with his folder in his hands, and Sol watched his face change the way a face changes when a man hears the city itself speak through a radio and finds, all in one moment, that he is inside the story instead of outside it.

"Do you hear it?" Sol said, and it was not a challenge. Her voice had gone quiet. "That's the district. Thirteen beds and nine at the south gate and nine old people with a candle, and the roofer's union, and the woman in the window with her lamp. That's the thing the section's about. I know the section. I know what it's for and I know what it's cost to live without it. But the section doesn't know about the blood fridge." She looked at him. "You have a boat and a light and a folder. The vault doesn't care about the folder. It cares about the water."

The engineer closed his eyes a moment.

"I'd have the doctor's word again," he said at last. "Not for the file. For me."

"If the transformer goes," the doctor said, "the blood fridge goes with it, and a man on my third floor dies of a thing he was going to survive, and it will be written up as the flood. That's all I'm going to say to a man on a boat."

"That's the first sensible thing said on this wall," said the old woman.

"Miss Vieira," the engineer said. He opened his folder and closed it again. "I can't authorize the installation. The link is unrecorded and the record will reflect that it is unrecorded and that I found it so, and that the pumping house switch was unrecorded too and that I heard the whole of it on the radio before I ever put out. But the record will also reflect that I was on the water when I found it and that I heard the district on the radio, and it will reflect that I hold the following view, which is mine to hold: that when the water is over the vault and the code says stop, the code has not understood the situation. I'm not giving you an order to climb. I'm not giving you an order not to." He closed the folder. "The rest is on your conscience and your mentor's. The boat's light's on. I'll hold it steady on the ladder."

"On the ladder," Sol said. "Not on me. I want to see the rungs and I don't want to be lit up like a poster while I do it."

"I'll hold it on the ladder."

The searchlight swung, and the beam came down the wall and steadied, and the rust on the rungs stood out of the dark like the veins of a leaf.

"Ife," Sol said.

She got her coat off and put it in a bundle on the coping and got the torch out of the bag and dropped it into her inside pocket with the lens turned in. Her hands were not quite steady. She looked at them and did not waste any time being sorry about it. The bag went on her back with the strap choked up short.

"Say it," Ife said.

"You'll brace the base and I'll go down with the bag. It's the section link under the stair and it's the last one. Same sequence as the pumping house and I'm not going to hurry it: off first, wait, bind, put it back. The wait's the part that matters. The vault's got a fault cooking in it and a switch turned off and turned straight on is a switch that finds the fault again. It has to sit off while the water settles." Sol crouched at the top of the ladder. "And I'll call it down to you and you'll call it up to Ozzy. Not the other way. If I go quiet, you tell the city I've gone quiet."

Ife stepped forward and put both hands on the ladder's stiles and set her boots against the stone and said, "Go down. I'm the base, and I don't drop people."

The line came round Sol's waist once, a bowline, snug, and she checked it with two fingers and tightened it with a twist and said, "That's right. That's not going anywhere."

"It was never going anywhere," Ife said. "It's my line."

The old man from the market came up the service stair and put his hand on the coping and looked down into the floodlit dark. He said nothing for a moment. Then he said, "The wrench. If I ask you to find a wrench down there you'll laugh at me."

"Not tonight," Sol said. "I'll keep my eyes open for the switch."

"Then keep your eyes on the switch," the old man said. "I'll hold your coat."

He took the coat and folded it over his good arm, and stood where the light would find him, and did not go away.

The water was at the second rung from the top when Sol put her boot on the third and began to climb down into the district's dark, with the bag on her shoulder and the floodlight behind her and the note of the vault coming up through her hands.
