The Second Breaker

The market reach narrowed as they came in. Under the surface the water went shallow and busy with things, so Sol stood in the bow with the torch and called the way a foot at a time.

"Left. There's a car under us on the right. Take it left."

Ife took it left. The barge nosed past the drowned car into the square, where the water lay flat and black and full of the tops of stalls, laid over like a fence that had given up.

The lamp was up in the second window over the fishmonger's, burning steady. The only light in the square.

"Somebody left that burning for us," Sol said. "A landmark."

"Or it's somebody letting the oil down slow in a cup." Ife brought the throttle to a knock. "The hub's in the middle. Where?"

Sol had the notebook open under the torch. The market page had the wheel with its spokes and the little square at the hub, and beside it Dell's old shorthand: the market hub, second on, lever's a hard pull, the whole square won't hold under the winch, mind the — and then a brown blur where something had sat on the page years back, a wet cup or a thumb in oil. She got the first letter and the last, a c and a t, and said a word under her breath.

"What."

"A word I can't read, and it's going to be a word that would have saved me twenty minutes." She closed the book. "Take me to the middle."

The hub was a cast-iron cap on a pillar, a hand's width out of the water. The vault hatch was under the cap and the vault under the water, and the housing she needed was down in the black under all of it. Sol lay on her belly on the bow and put her ear to the iron and listened down the pillar.

"It's ringing," she said. "Like a bell with a crack in it. That cap's not bolted, it's been got at. The market's a place you can walk to from three directions. Somebody had a boat and a wrench and they came before us."

The hatch gave a smell of old oil and live water. The cap came up, and the housing came up with it, because the cap was the housing's head and the housing had been loosened and was riding up. Whoever had been at it had got it half out, and that was no triumph, because it came up wrong.

The cast body had a gouged hollow where a bolt and its iron had been. Where the bolt had been, a copper lug hung out like a tongue, burnt black at the tip.

"Tell me what I'm looking at," Ife said.

"Somebody's had it half out with a wrench, and had that lug off and back on wrong, and it heated and burnt, and the water got it and the fault tripped it." Sol drew her finger above the gouge without touching it. "And they took the copper. A storm doesn't cut copper. That's a hacksaw, and it went out of this square this evening with somebody in a boat."

"Looters."

"People," Sol said, because she had been about to say thieves and had heard Dell go quiet in her head. "There's a whole dark city out there and copper buys things. Where you get copper at this hour is where somebody put it there for a packet of nuts."

The four vending machines stood in a row under the fishmonger's awning. Sol took them the way the waterworks had taught her, an inch at a time. The first lock came open in a minute, because locks built to keep out a boy with a coin are not locks built to keep out an electrician with a bar. The coin box held wet vouchers. But the coin return's little copper chute came out in her hand, with three feet of harness to it, and she had it to her ear before she could stop herself. Dead as a spoon.

The second machine had been got at and its board was bare. The third gave up a chute and two brackets and a strip of busbar long as her forearm. The fourth machine had a man in it. His hands came up slow and easy, and he was old and wet through, holding a stub of a wrench.

"Don't put that light in my eyes," he said. "Same as you, by the look. The market's been stripped since it went dark. I'm not stripping it. I've a lamp up in a window and a line to a woman across the way and a daughter in the market hall up to her knees. I'm looking for a length to make a hook and pull a shutter open."

Sol lowered the bar. "Take what you need. Leave me the coin chutes."

The man looked at the copper in her hand and the wet housing down on the deck with the gouge in its side, and put it together in one breath.

"Market hub. You're the ones putting the hub back. Then hear this. There's a wire under the fishmonger's front, down the gutter, come off the awning when the feeder let go. You can't see it. The water's black there and it's got a slick so it lies still. I've been telling every boat to keep off the gutter."

Sol's whole body went still and small. She got down into the shallows with the tester and put the lead in the water a yard from the wall, and the needle kicked. She stepped back and put it in again, and the needle held high and steady.

"Live," she said, clipped. "It's live in the gutter and it's been live a while. Gutter runs the whole front of the square." She looked down at the man. "You've been pulling that shutter with a hook. Have you been in that gutter to do it?"

"Only my arm."

"Then your arm got lucky and I'm glad. Keep it out now, and the hook too. Tell the woman across the way, tell the hall. Tell them the gutter bites and the hub's being put back and to keep to the high stone."

The man nodded once, straight down, like a man agreeing to a thing he means to hold to. "Give me your line and I'll get my daughter and shout it from the hall door." He held out his hand.

Sol had no line for him. Ife had a line and a winch and a temper about both. She gave him the old stiff coil out of the bag, and it was not much.

"That's a poor line."

"It's the line we've got. Tie it right."

"Tie it right, she says." He went into the shallows with the copper and the wrench held up over the water, and stopped without turning. "The lamp in my window. When you've got the hub back, put your boat in the light and I'll know it's done and I'll shout it where it'll be heard. That's what a lamp is for. It's for the next one coming."

He went into the shadow with his arm up out of the gutter. Sol watched him go, then went back to the machines with her hands going a pint a minute, because the gutter was live and the man was in the water and the repair was not even begun.

The copper came down onto the deck and the deck became a shop, a good shop, with a dead housing on it and two of them kneeling either side with the torch between their knees.

"The bolt's gone and there's no thread," Sol said. "So I'm not putting a bolt in it. I'm going to strap it. Lay the busbar across the burnt iron and down over the tongue and bind it off, clean and tight, because if it's loose it heats and if it heats it goes, and we lose the square and the road up to the hospital with it."

"How do we make it tight?"

"Not the winch. That'd have the strap off and the barge over." Sol was already up for the boat hook and the turnbuckle off the third machine. "The turnbuckle and the pole for a lever and me."

You could not put a busbar on a live thing and switch it on, so she made the housing safe the only way there was. The fault had tripped it and the lug was dead on this side, but dead on this side was not dead on the other, and Sol could hear it, the small burr of amps in the cables in the drowned square, wanting to come up. She set her palm on the iron and left it there until she was sure of the cold.

"Power's off this side. Now the bind."

The turnbuckle went round the busbar and round the tongue. She got the pole in and pulled, and the busbar laid flat against the burnt iron and the tongue came down under it. She turned it another half and the pole bowed and she felt it in her wrists, and she stopped when her wrists said stop. That was the whole of her testing: a wrist.

She put the tester on the busbar and the tongue, and the needle went over and sat firm and did not shiver.

"Read it out," Ife said.

"Continuity." Sol let out a breath. "It's rough work, and Dell would have done it neater. A strap and a turnbuckle, and it'll run warm. But it's tight." She sat back on her heels. "That's the housing. The lever's down in the black, and the cap's been got at and the pillar's loosened."

She went over the side.

The market's water was a moving square with a river in it, pushing at her from three sides, nothing like the still roofed water of the works. She went down the pillar with her eyes shut, her right hand finding the twist in the iron and following it down like a stair rail. The cap rocked under her hand, and the rocking made a knocking she felt against her ear. The lever was up, because the fault had tripped it, and that was a small good thing.

She pulled. It came down an inch and jammed, and the square rocked the pillar and the pillar rocked the lever.

Her chest was going. She set her feet against the gouged body and got both hands to the lever and hung her whole weight off it, and the lever went down and did not go home.

The stutter came up all around her, high and wrong. The strap. She had switched it on inside the vault and the strap was running hot where she had bound it, and the heat was coming through the iron into the water, and she had her hands on the thing that was heating it.

She got her hand off and found the turnbuckle, and it was warm, and she turned it. A quarter and it turned easy. A quarter more and it fought her, and she turned it anyway, one last notch, and the stutter in the water changed and went low and even and quiet, and the thing in her chest let go.

She came up under the barge's stern and got her hand to the boat hook, and Ife hauled her over the gunwale and she came aboard in a heap of wet with the water running off her.

The lamp over the fishmonger's was still burning. And down the row the fishmonger's little lamps came on, one, two, and then the market hall's own high windows stood up out of the dark, lit and yellow and full of people.

Then the shout came across the square, from the hall door on the high stone, not one voice now but several, and they were shouting it up and down.

"Hub's in! Lamps are on in the hall! Keep off the gutter!"

Ife had Sol by the shoulder and was looking past her at the hall, full of yellow light and people moving, and one of the shapes at the door could have been the man with his arm round a woman in the doorway.

"Will you look at that," Ife said, and her voice had a crack in it. "I said it was not my business, saving other people's crates of an evening. Will you look at that."

"Market hub," Sol said, sitting with her back against the dead housing. "Second one's done."

They did not get the time back. Sol got the notebook out to put the hub down, and turned again to the market page, and looked a while at the smudge with a c at the front and a t at the back.

She had gone down on a guess and a wrist, and the guess had held, and the word was still there and she still could not read it. Dell would have known it by looking and not by guessing, and Dell was on the roof of the grid, out of earshot since the loading arch. She took her finger off the smudge.

"Write it down yourself, Sol Vieira," she said to the air, "and write what you did when you couldn't read it, and both of us can read it in the morning." She took up the pencil, and her hands were shaking now that it was allowed, and made the letters anyway.

Then Ozzy's voice came up off the gunwale, and Ozzy was not doing the broadcaster's rhythm.

"—write it on your stairs," he said. "The surge has not begun to fall. It is rising faster than the tide table said, and the hundred-year mark the engineers painted on the low walls is not the mark any more, the water is over it. The barrier holds its line, but the river above it is still coming down on us. The second high water will be worse than the first. If you are in the low streets and you are dry, get up two floors and take your neighbor's old people up with you." A paper sound, a breath. "And I will say the thing I have been saving. There are four breakers in this city that must be put back by hand. One is in the waterworks, which is lit and pumping this hour. The next is in the market, and I can see the market hall's windows from where I sit. Two are done and two are left. Whoever is out there doing this, hear the water line. The hospital district's line has held through the first high water. It will not hold through the second at the rate we are losing time. So finish it before the second high tide. I do not know if you can hear me. I am told you can. Finish it."

The voice went to the small news, the streets and the names, and Sol sat with the pencil in her hand and wrote nothing for a while.

"The second high tide," Ife said at the wheel.

"Two breakers left, a barge that runs hot, a line gone to a roof, and the whole city rising." Sol closed the notebook and got up. "Quick won't do now. We've got to be quick and right, and the right one's the one that scares me."

"The one off the map."

"Dell said it was off his map and he was the reason, and he made me promise to ask him on the air so Ozzy heard." Sol looked at the little wet radio on the gunwale. "A man who draws like that doesn't leave a breaker out because he forgot it. He leaves it out because he couldn't draw it where anyone could read it."

Ife took the notebook and looked at the smudge and touched it with her thumb. "There's one page in this book looks like this. He wrote it, and then he put a wet cup down on the word he was most sorry for." She gave it back. "Ask your question from the depot. I want to hear the answer with the terms in it."

Sol took the throttle and Ife the pole, and the barge went out of the market under the little lamps coming on down the row, and the man at the hall door waved with his arm up, and the lamp over the fishmonger's burned behind them and was still burning when they turned the corner into the dark.

The water ahead was higher on the walls than it had been an hour before. Sol braced her knee against the gunwale and watched it go by the hull, faster than it had any right to, coming down from the river.
