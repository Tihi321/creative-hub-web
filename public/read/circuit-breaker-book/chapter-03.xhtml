The First Breaker

The lock keeper's lane ran between two rows of houses, and the water in it lay deeper and colder and quieter than the street had been. Sol rode in the bow with her knee braced against the gunwale and let the barge feel its way in. The engine had dropped to a slow knock. Ife stood at the wheel with her chin up, reading the dark the way you read a face.

"Houses on your left are number nine and eleven," Sol said. "Left side has a stoop at eleven with iron railings. Mind the railings."

"Mind them yourself," Ife said, but the bow swung a hand's width and the railings went by on the left with a scrape of sound, iron on wood, a complaint and then nothing.

The lane narrowed until the houses leaned out over the water and the rain came down in a thin drip off the eaves instead of a fall out of the sky. Sol heard the change and knew what it meant. The lane was roofed by the houses almost meeting, and beneath that roof the dark was total. She shut her eyes, because open eyes were no use, and gave the dark to her hands and her ears.

"Pipe on the right," she said. "A hundred-year-old service run. Feel the hum? It's not a hum. It's a leak. On nothing. There's no current on it and it's still cold down its whole length, so leave it alone, it can't hurt us." She said it to hear the words, to keep her own nerve in order. "But there's a cable in the wall on the right, three feet up. Dead. Cut at the junction where the lane bends. Somebody did that right, at least."

"Somebody did that sometime," Ife said. "Kick the stern off that stoop."

Sol did, with her wading boot, and the barge turned.

The bridge came out of the dark the way things did in a blacked-out basement. First a difference in the sound, then a shape, then a wet stone arch with weeds hanging off it and a chain hung down beneath the keystone, and the chain was dragging in the current and ringing. It rang low and irregular, a struck note that came and went with the water's push, and it was the best sound Sol had heard all night, because it was a sound she could trust.

"Hear it?"

"I hear it," Ife said, and brought the barge in toward it with a patient quarter turn, and the chain went by on the left, ringing off its true channel. The water opened out on the other side of the arch into a long straight reach, and out across that reach, low and huddled and dark, was the waterworks.

Sol knew it first by the smell. Wet stone and old iron, and the breath of a place built to hold water, flat and mineral, and rising out of it, faint and sour, the smell of a burnt thing, a smell like a solder iron left on. That was the vault, and the fault lived in the vault.

"Dock us anywhere," she said. "The water's over the loading steps but there's a platform inside the arch. Ram it slow."

Ife rammed it slow. The barge's nose came up against the drowned steps with a soft grind of grit and brick, and Sol stepped off into water that came to her chest and kept the toolbag lifted clear. The bag was not heavy. The bag was a shop of dead things: a smashed kettle, a hair dryer from a skip, a length of flex coiled like a rope. None of it was heavy, and all of it was going to matter before dawn.

The loading arch took her in and the rain stopped falling on her head. She stood in the dark of the waterworks with the water at her chest and listened.

The vault lay below her with the water over it, and in the water something still made the small high stutter she had heard from the lane, the mosquito at the window. She laid her palm on the nearest wall. The wall was cold, and the cold had a shape to it: a gradient that went colder as it went down, and out in the middle of the flooded floor a warmer patch, a wrong bathwater warmth, and the warmth was where the fault was. The water was cooking. Not much. Not yet. But a fault that found water and stayed in it would keep finding water, and the water would keep carrying it around the way a rumor went around a stairwell.

"The fault's on the vault floor," she said. "East side, I think, under the second bay. It's live in the water and the water's live around it, but only near it. Where we're standing is quiet." She said that, and then she made herself check it, because quiet was a thing that killed people. She took the little tester out of the bag and put one lead against the arch wall and the other into the water, and she watched the needle with her chin an inch from it. The needle sat at nothing. She moved the lead a yard. Nothing. A yard more, toward the warm place. The needle twitched, a nervous little kick, and she pulled it back and let out a breath.

"All right. Two yards from here it bites. Past that it's the grave. I'm going to swim the bay under that noise and come up under the vault hatch and do it by touch."

"And if you don't come up?"

"Then don't come in after me. Keep the engine running and keep the radio on." Sol put the tester away and looked at Ife, and Ife's face in the lamp glow was doing something complicated that she seemed to want no part of. "Ife. If I don't come up, you take the barge to Dell at the grid and you tell him the first one's done, which it will be, and you get the hospital the rest of the way, and you take the tram's salvage and you don't think about it twice."

"If you don't come up, I'm not going to the tram depot for salvage. I'm going to Barrow Row for my mother." Ife said it flat. "So come up."

Sol went down.

The water took her under, cold at the top and colder at the bottom, and she went down a foot at a time with her hands out in front of her, feeling for the vault lip the way you feel for a step in the dark. She found it: a metal edge, square, with rivets. She pulled herself along it. Her ears filled and the world went to a low roar and then to beats of pressure, and she counted the beats to keep herself slow. One. Two. The heat came before she expected it, a warm cloud against her cheek, and she went still and let the current carry her around it instead of through it. The warm cloud had a taste. It tasted like the mouth of a battery. She went around it, wide, and the water pulled at her coat and she let it, and then her fingers found the hatch handle and the handle was hot and she did not let it stop her, she got her hand across the housing and found the breaker body with her thumb.

It was a big old manual job, cast and worn, with a lever in the up position, and the lever was up because it had tripped, and all she had to do was pull it down. She pulled it down. It fought her. The water took her weight off the pull, so she braced her shoulder against the housing and pulled again, and the mechanism was gritted up with silt, and she hung there in the dark with her lungs going and the warm cloud of the fault a foot behind her ear, and she thought, clean the contacts, always clean the contacts, and she dug her thumb into the housing slot as far as it would go and dragged the lever down an inch and another inch and it went home with a clack she felt through her whole jaw rather than heard through the water.

The current in the water changed. The warm cloud went cool. The stutter in the water above her stopped as if someone had opened a door and let the rumor out.

She got her feet under the lip and pushed up, and her head broke the surface, and she took a great whooping breath that made her cough, and she got a hand over the arch ledge and hung there with her face in her own wet sleeve.

"Done," she said, to the dark. "First one's done."

Then she heard it, and she laughed, and the laugh was a cough too. Not from the vault. From everywhere. The waterworks had come back up. The old pumps were turning somewhere in the dark, and the pipes in the wall had gone from dead to alive with a sound like a long held note taken up by a choir, and the little maintenance lamp over the arch was glowing, a sickly orange, and it had been dead when she went under and now it was on.

Ife's face appeared at the arch. She looked at Sol and then up at the lamp and then back at Sol.

"You're alive."

"Terms of the job." Sol hauled herself up onto the arch ledge and sat in the wet with her legs hanging, and she got the handheld out of her pocket and it was full of water and she said a word she was glad Dell had not heard, and she shook it out and it came back on with the burr still in it, and Ozzy Kwan's voice came up through the burr, and he was talking fast.

"—repeat, the hospital district is holding. Your backup generators have come in at the hospital and I am told the wards are steady, the lifts are running to the wards, and the blood fridge is holding cold. I want you to hear that cold. Cold blood is a good sign, Port Alaric. It means the line is not just holding, it has bought time. Time is what we are buying tonight." A pause, a paper sound. "I want to thank whoever in the waterworks district just got the pumps back up. The lamps are coming on in the low streets. If that was you, and you can hear me, thank you. And I want to remind everybody in the low streets that a lit lamp is not a dry street. Stay up."

Sol sat on the ledge with her boots in the water and her hand full of a wet radio, and the small thing in her chest settled down and went quiet for the first time since midnight.

They had to wait for the engine to warm the barge past the cold it had taken in coming through the lane, so Ife let her drift out of the waterworks arch into the reach with a painter tied off to the loading ring, and they sat there while the pumps sang in the wall and the little orange lamp kept them company. Sol dug the torch out of the bag and put it on, and the reach came out of the dark, and the reach was a street of water with the tops of things in it: a signpost, a mailbox with its hat under water, a lamp green with weed, and off to the right, some way down, a roof.

On the roof there was a person.

Sol put the torch on the roof and the person stood up, and the person was a woman in a raincoat too big for her, with something wriggling inside it, a child, and she had a crate beside her on the roof, a supply crate, and the crate was in the gutter of the roof and the water was lapping at its corner and she was trying to pull it up the slope and the slope was wet slate and she was not winning.

Ife saw it the moment Sol did. She was out of the wheelhouse and had the torch off Sol and put on the roof before Sol could say anything.

"Don't," Ife said. "I know that voice. I've been that voice. That's a woman doing sums." She swung the torch. "Is there rope on the roof? Is there something to tie to?"

"Roof's got a chimney," Sol said, shading her eye and squinting against the light. "Left of her. And there's a chimney pot, and a chimney pot'll hold. Ife, if we put a line across—"

"Then she's got a line and we've got a crate. Throw it and she ties it and we haul and she has dry blankets and we keep the crate from going down the gutter and taking her child into the water with it." Ife was already going for the coil under the gunwale. "You're learning, promising."

"Don't."

"Too late. Right. Can you throw?"

"I'm an electrician. I throw to people who can't hear me."

"Then throw."

Sol got to the bow and got her feet under her, and the gap to the roof was twenty yards of black water if it was one, and the coil was old and stiff and would not want to go, so she took half of it out of the coil and laid it in the bottom of the bow in loops and made the rest a coil in her left hand and put her right hand through it, and she leaned out and let the coil's own bulk carry it. The line went up and out and the woman on the roof had her hands up, and the line came down across the gutter and the crate and went into the water a yard short.

"Again!" the woman shouted. Her voice came thin over the water. "Again, the chimney's behind me—"

Sol hauled the line in hand over fist and let it fall in loops again and threw again, and this time the coil went high and wide and the woman got a hand to it and a second hand and wrapped it twice and pulled, and pulled the slack down off the gutter and over the corner of the roof and held it.

"Tie it to the chimney!" Ife shouted. "Not the pot, the stack itself, two rounds and a knot you can't argue with!"

The woman did it, working with her whole body, and Sol took the burning end of the line and took two turns round the barge's bollard and let Ife have the engine and pay it out slow, and the line came up out of the water and stood straight and singing, and the crate slid up the wet slate out of the gutter with a shriek of nails and came over the ridge and stopped against the chimney.

The woman sat down hard on the roof with the child in her lap. She sat there and rocked twice, and then she shouted something across the water that Sol could not make out, and then she stood up with the child on her hip and shouted it again, and this time Sol got it.

"What's your name!"

Ife turned in the wheelhouse with her hand on the throttle and her jaw working, and she did not answer.

"Ife Aduba!" Sol shouted. "Barge Aduba, out of Canal Street! Tell your block a roofer fell in the market and the market needs a doctor!"

"Aduba!" the woman shouted, and pointed up at the sky, and then at the dark where Barrow Row was, and the pointing seemed to mean something, and then she sat down again with her child and her dry crate and her line, and the torch went off her, and she was a woman on a roof being warm, which was all anybody could do tonight.

Ife put the engine in gear. The barge came round off the loading ring and the reach opened ahead of it, and Ife stood at the wheel with her voice perfectly even.

"That's forty feet of my good line gone," she said. "I want it back before the night's out."

"You will get it back," Sol said. She sat down in the bow with the bag against her hip and the wet radio beside her, and she looked down the reach toward the market and the depot and the dark, and she heard Ozzy Kwan say the hospital district was still lit, and she settled her palm flat on the cool iron of the gunwale beside her, because it was there and it was steady. "She said something else. She pointed. Over toward the market."

"I saw."

"I think she's got a doctor."

"Then let's go and get the market's breaker and leave the doctor to it." Ife brought the throttle up and the engine knocked steady under them, a bit rougher than before, a bit hotter, but steady. "Two down and two to go, and the next one's in the market where a roofer fell, and you heard the man say the market's got a live feeder in the water. So you'll want your eyes up and your hands dry, promising. Tell me the way."

Sol told her the way. The reach ran straight to the market, and the market was a wide square with streets off it like the spokes of a wheel, and the wheel's hub was where the second breaker lived, and they would need the winch for the housing and the bolt set would not want to turn. She said all that and kept her hand on the gunwale so Ife would know she was not making it up, and the barge went on down the reach into the dark, and the pumps sang behind them in the wall of the waterworks, and up ahead in a turn of the wind, Ozzy Kwan's signal brought them the city's small lights and the city's small news, and Sol listened with her hands in her lap.

"Two down," Ife said. "You keep saying it like a prayer, you'll get it wrong. Say it like a checklist."

"Waterworks done. Market next. Depot after." Sol closed her eyes and felt the river go under the hull, and put her hand on the bag of dead things, and made herself be calm. "Then the fourth one. The one that's off the map."
