What the City Owes

Dawn in Port Alaric does not arrive as a colour. It arrives as a lightening, the grey above the hospital roof going from iron to tin to a soft rinsed pearl, and the rain easing off into a fine mist that was the whole city breathing out.

The works boat came up the flooded street with the motor just over an idle, because a motor at speed pushes a wake and a wake pushes doors. Sol sat in the bottom of it with her hands in her coat and her bag against her knee. Somewhere between the courtyard and the corner she had stopped shivering, and now she was aware of her body in pieces, the way you are aware of a fuse board after a fault: this shoulder, that rib, two fingers gone white that would not warm.

The loading arch stood where the street met the old viaduct, a high stone mouth with a lip of dry stone inside it, and Dell Marchetti sat in the dry with his bad leg out in front of him and his radio on his knee.

He looked old in the dawn. The storm had been dark and forgiving and the dawn was neither. Beside him on the stone stood the chair from the control room, the one that squeaked, carried up God knew how and set down where he could reach it. Somebody had carried a chair up a flooded street for an old man with a bad leg. Sol looked at it and had to look away.

The engineer nosed the boat in against the arch's step and Ife was up on the stone before the motor stopped turning, the line over her shoulder.

"He's been on the air all night," Ife said. "Ozzy's been handing him the channel every hour to say the district's holding."

"I heard," Sol said.

She got out with the engineer's hand, which she took because her legs had opinions now, and went up the step and across the dry, and Dell looked up at her.

"You're wet," he said.

"I've been in the water a long while." He shifted his bad leg an inch and did not manage more. "Sit down before you fall down. There's a chair. Somebody brought me a chair and I haven't earned it."

Sol sat on the wet step next to him instead and let her head go back against the stone.

"Grille was seized," she said. "Your hidden screw was a clever screw for about a decade and then it was a nuisance. The boy had a stub wrench and the old woman held the tin lid over the fuse so it stayed dry. The bracket had ridden off the wall and it's shimmed now and I drove the nut home and put in a new cartridge and waited out the tick and it came back quiet. It's holding. It wants a survey before the end of the month and I want to be on it."

"All of that," Dell said, "in the dark, on a ladder, in the flood." He said it as a man says a fact he does not like. "You didn't do it by the book."

"I did it by the water. The book was a liar, you told me so on the roof." Sol opened her eyes. "The engineer's been fair and he's going to say what he's going to say, and you'll take it, and then I'll say what I'll say. And Ozzy's got a channel open. I can hear the hiss of it."

"He has. There's a queue at his door of people who want to say their word and he's letting them in one at a time, and he's asked me twice if I want to say mine." Dell looked at her. "Here you are."

Ozzy's voice came out of the handheld at Sol's hip, warm and rapid, with the fine papery hiss under it that the channel had when the whole delta was listening.

"Port Alaric. It's coming up light and the rain's off. On the line now I have the municipal engineer of works, who is going to say in plain words what happened at the hospital, and who I am not going to let speak in section numbers, because the city has had a long night."

"That's fair," the engineer said. He stood on the step of the loading arch with his folder closed and his helmet under his arm. "The hospital district is lit. The wards are lit, the lifts are running, the blood fridge has stayed cold, and Barrow Row is reading its own meters. I have checked it myself from the substation dials. It is not my doing. It was done by an apprentice electrician, four neighbours on a parapet, a woman in a barge, a radio operator, and a retired man with a bad leg. I am recording all of that."

"And the lawbreaking," Ozzy said.

"I'll say that plainly too." The engineer opened the folder. "Both switches, the pumping house island switch and the section link on the district wall, are unrecorded. They were installed by Dell Marchetti, a licensed electrician, off the survey and off the drawings, over and above the breakers that are on the map. The code has something to say about that and it is not kind. His license will be reviewed, and the district may send its word to it, and I have said so on the parapet." He paused. "But the city should hear the other half from me and not scrape it together out of the air. My men have been up since before the surge rebuilding the outfall feeders from a drawing Mr. Marchetti made when he was twenty, and we could do it because he drew carefully, and if he had not drawn carefully we would have no hospital either. That is not in the code and not on the drawings. I'll not be the last man to say a word against him tonight."

Dell did not look at the engineer. He looked at his hands, and said, not loud, "That's a kind thing from a man who doesn't have to say it."

"It's a true thing," the engineer said. "I don't like it any more than you do."

Then the queue at the tower door came to the microphone, and a voice Sol did not know spoke into it, hoarse and close, and it was angry.

"I'll say my word," he said. "I'm from the flats on Kirk Row. I've been up since three with water in my kitchen and my mother in her chair, and I was listening when your man said he hid a switch in a wall so nobody could catch him at it. So let me ask him a question while the city's listening. What else has he hid? How many other switches has he left off the paper that'll come up next storm and get somebody killed? I don't care if he lit the hospital. He lied to the city for thirty years about the city's own wires, and a man who lies about wires can lie about anything. That's my word."

The channel was quiet, and the loading arch was full of the dawn.

Dell's hand rested on the radio on his knee and had gone white at the knuckle and he had not pressed the key.

"I'll answer him," Sol said.

"Sol—"

"I'll answer him." She got up. Her legs were not good but they were enough, and she took the handheld out of her coat and stood in the mouth of the arch where the dawn was, and pressed the key, and heard her own voice go out across the delta thin and clear.

"This is Sol Vieira. I'm the apprentice on the district wall. I'd like to answer the man from Kirk Row, because he's asked a fair question and he isn't wrong and I'm not going to pretend he is."

"Say it," Ozzy said. "The whole of it."

"Dell Marchetti hid two switches. He hid them because he put them in where they were needed and the survey wouldn't pass them, and then he was ashamed, and a man who is ashamed will keep a thing quiet a long while and it gets worse the longer it's quiet. That's a fault in the man and it's a fault in the record and it wants putting right." She took a breath. "But you've got a mother in a chair, and I want you to hear what held her up all night. It was the section link he's scared of. The one off the paper. It's what kept the flood pumps pumping while the survey was stuck in an office in the dark. You've asked what else he's hid. He's hidden two switches off the paper, and I've had my hand in both of them tonight. That's the wall link and the pumping house island switch, and the island switch is the fourth of the four breakers, the one Dell drew wrong when he was twenty and never went back to put right. So it's six boxes in this city with my hand in them since the surge began: the waterworks, the market hub, the depot's third breaker, the culvert bridge shunt, the island switch that was the fourth, and the wall link besides. Six, and every one of them is written in my notebook. If there was a seventh I'd have found it and put it in the book and told the city, because that's what the notebook is for."

"How do you know it's only six?" the man said.

"Because I know the sound of a house," Sol said. "And I've been listening to this one since before I could read."

There was a silence on the channel, and then the man from Kirk Row said, "That's the girl that went down the wall in a flood," and it was not a question, and Ozzy said it was, and the man said, "Well. Then I'll not say the old man lied to the city about the wires. I'll say he lied to the survey. And I'll send my complaint to the review, and I'd like the girl to put a line in it that says what she just said, because my mother's chair runs off a socket and the socket was live all night and I'd like that in the record."

"It'll be in mine," Sol said. "I'll write it tonight. And if your socket's damp, make it safe before you go to bed, and if you can't do it yourself, tell Ozzy and I'll send someone round tonight who can. That's not a promise I can't keep. That's just how it's done."

The channel filled after that, and the filling was the thing that changed it.

The doctor off the ridge came on, tired and exact. "This is the doctor from the ridge. The wards are steady, the blood fridge never went warm, and I want to be plain about why. It is not the pride of one old man keeping my patients alive tonight. It is the power, and the power was there because somebody put it in a wall where it would be wanted. Let the review do its work. My ward is warm."

The old woman from the culvert came on next with her complaint already drafted, and read it out: a list of things the law had not done during the night, a culvert, a kettle, the sister's block on the other side, the yard that filled and the yard that drained. It was not a speech, it was a list, and nobody could argue with a list.

The sister's boy came on and said, in three sentences, that he had held the light and that nobody had been in charge and it had worked. The woman on the roof came on with her child on her hip and said a line thrown from a barge had saved her boy and that she still had Ife's forty feet of good line and would bring it down herself in the morning, and Ife, on the arch step with her arms crossed, said under her breath, "Finally," and did not smile, and meant it entirely.

The man from Kirk Row came back at the end and said, "I've changed my word and I want the city to hear me change it. I've not changed my mind about the paper. A wire off the paper is a wire the next storm can catch a man on. I've changed my mind about the man. There's a difference and it's worth saying."

"There's a difference," Ozzy said, "and it's worth saying. Port Alaric, the hospital district is lit and a man's license is under review and a woman on Kirk Row has a chair that runs off a socket that was live all night, and both those things are true at once, and this station is going to keep saying both of them, because that is what the station is for."

Dell put his hand over the radio on his knee and said nothing for a while.

"I'll say my word at the review," he said at last. "Not on the air. I've done my saying on the air. I'll sit in the chair and take what comes and be civil about it, and I'll not say the law was wrong, because the law is a good law and I knew it when I broke it. But I'll say the one thing the law has no box for, and that's that a city's wires and a city's people are not two different circuits. You can't survey one and leave the other dark." He looked up. "Your father would have said it better."

"He'd have said it shorter," Sol said. "And then he'd have gone and made the socket safe."

"Aye," Dell said. "That's the job."

The engineer's boat went downriver with the old woman and the sister's boy, because a boat is a boat and people wanted home before the water went down further. The child of the woman on the roof stayed where she was, on her hip and out of the weather, because a mother who had lost a crate on a roof was not going to put her boy in a boat for the second time in a night.

The old man came back along the parapet with Sol's coat over his one good arm, and Sol took it and put it on and it was dry now, because he had held it up out of the rain all the way down and all the way back. "The wrench," he said, and she said, "When the market's lit I'll come down for it myself, and that's the last time I say it," and he said, "Good," and went off up the street with the dawn on him.

Dell went with the engineer in the second boat, which he did not like, and said so, and the engineer said the review would be easier if the man could walk to it, and Dell said a great many things about reviews that did not go on the record.

That left the loading arch and the two of them and Ife.

Ife Aduba had not gone with either boat. She had stood on the arch step for the better part of an hour with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and when the second boat was out of earshot she said, "The tram."

"The tram," Sol said.

"The water's going down on the yard. The salvage rights are mine and I have been up all night earning them and I am not going to wait while the whole city sends its love to Dell Marchetti." Ife came down the step and looked at Sol the way she looked at a blocked intake. "The four breakers are back. So it's mine. First claim, and the rest of the night split, and I want it said out loud with a witness."

"Ozzy's got the channel."

"Ozzy's asleep standing up and I'm not waking him for a barge." Ife turned and looked along the arch, where the engineer had left his man with the folder and the boat, and the old man was still in sight at the corner, and the woman on the roof's lamp showed in her window across the reach. "I'll take the engineer's man and the old man at the corner and the woman at her window as witnesses, and the dawn's a witness, and you've got no boat now and no excuse. Say it."

Sol put her hand out first, because a bargain should be a bargain and Ife had earned hers.

"First claim on the tram's salvage at the Drowned Tram Depot. The rest of the night split. And the board grievance settled by me speaking to Dell and the hall, and not the radio." She took the hand. "I've spoken to Dell. I'll speak to the hall. You'll have my word at the grievance and no word before it."

Ife's handshake was the handshake of a woman who has shaken hands on a barge and means it. "Then we're square on that." She let go. "The engine comes out this morning and goes back in this afternoon and it's four hundred pounds of old iron with the yard in it. I'm not pulling it alone."

"I'll pull it with you," Sol said. "The four-breaker bargain was for the breakers. The motor's the motor. I've never pulled a barge engine out of a barge and I'd like to."

"It's a two-day job done in a day," Ife said. "You'll be wet and your hands will be cut and I'll shout at you."

"That's a bargain I've heard before," Sol said, and it was the nearest thing to a smile either of them had done since the storm, and it was enough.

They went down to the depot yard through streets that were no longer a river and not yet a street, and the water was down to Sol's knees by the time they reached the yard and down to her ankles by the time they got the shed open. The barge sat where they had left it and the shed stood and Ife's motors under the shed were dry.

The barge engine was not a thing you could pull with two women and a length of line, and Sol did not try. She knelt by the casing with her ear to the iron and listened the way she listened to a wall, and the casing told her the shape of the water in it: the intake flooded, the sump full, and a thin high note in the gearbox that had no business there.

"It's full of the yard," she said. "The intake took water and the sump's full and there's water standing in the cylinders. We pull the injectors and turn it over slow by hand and let it blow the water out, and we drain the sump and put new oil in, and we don't put a starter to it until the cylinders are dry. That's the sump plug and that's the oil. And the gearbox wants looking at because there's a note in it I don't like."

"There's a note in it I've never liked," Ife said.

"Then I'll listen with you. When we're done with the cylinders I'll want a thin feeler and a good torch and I'll want to hear it running with the cover off before I believe it."

Ife got the wrenches and Sol got the bag and they set to it in the grey dawn, up to their elbows, taking out the injectors one at a time and turning the motor over by hand and counting, and the water came out of the cylinders in a fine spray and hit Ife's arm and she swore at it, and Sol said, "That's the first one," and Ife said, "Say it like a person, not a meter," and Sol said, "That's the first one, and it was wet," and they laughed in a place that had been under water a few hours before and would be again, and they kept working.

The man with the tobacco tin found them at the shed door in the middle of the morning, when the yard had drained enough to show the cobbles and the sun was up.

Sol did not know him, but Ife did, and said, "You're the man with the feeder in the fish gutter."

"It's grounded and safe," he said. "I came to bring the tin back and tell you it's safe, and to ask if there's a bit of work for a man with a wrench who's finished his job and doesn't much want to go home to a dark flat." He held out the tin, empty now, the lid shut on nothing. "The fuses went down the chain to the wall and I'm glad of it. But the tin's a good tin and it comes back where it came from. And I've a coil of wire in my coat that's clean, if the shop wants it."

"The shop'll take the wire," Ife said. "And the tin goes to the girl, because she likes tins. And there's a gearbox wants two men and a torch and half the morning, if you're not particular."

"I'm not particular," the man said, and came in off the step and got his wrench out.

The day got up and the water went down and the streets came out of it one block at a time, and the city began to be a city.

Ife got her salvage rights in writing from the depot's foreman, who came over at noon from the tram office with a clipboard under his arm and a face that suggested he had not slept and did not intend to discuss it. Ms. Aduba's claim was the first on the yard's paper and he had brought the paper with him, and he read it aloud in a flat voice that gave nothing away, and Ife signed it with a signature she had been practising since she was twelve, and Sol watched her do it and felt the thing turn in the world: a woman with a barge and a claim in writing and a motor to rebuild.

The foreman folded the paper and put it in his coat. "The other claimants will be told it was first and signed," he said. "That's the yard's way and it settles most of them."

"Will it settle them all?" Ife said.

"Nothing settles all of them," the foreman said, and went back across the drained cobbles with his clipboard.

The sun came out that afternoon, and the light on the wet city was hard and bright and full of small moving things, and the puddles steamed, and the market was dark still in the upper streets but its lamps were lit in the lower ones, and the hospital was lit above them all. From the depot yard you could hear Ozzy on somebody's radio telling the whole city which streets must stay dark and which were alive again, street by street, like a man reading out a list he had checked.

Sol went up the yard's wall and stood in the sun with her notebook and looked down the reach at the city she had crossed.

It was not all of it. It would not be all of it for a long while. There were streets with no meters and flats with no lamps and a man on Kirk Row with a mother in a chair and a socket of his own to make safe. There was a review to go to and a word to give at it and a grievance at the hall.

But under her boots, through the yard's stone and into the cold iron of the shed foundation, she could hear the city coming back up, note by note, block by block, the way she had heard it since she was nine years old and her father let her help him wire a lamp.

It was a circuit. Somebody had closed it. A great many somebodies, hand to hand, and an old man in a chair, and a woman with a barge, and a radio operator who would not stop telling the truth, and it was enough.

She got down off the wall and went back into the shed, where Ife was bent over the gearbox with the man with the wrench, and she said, "What's it want?"

Ife put her head up. "It wants a person who can read a motor," she said. "That's you, if you've finished looking at the view."

"I've finished looking at the view," Sol said, and got down on her knees in the shed beside her, and listened to the gearbox until she could hear the bearing in it the way you hear a wrong note in a hymn, and said so.
