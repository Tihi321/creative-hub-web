The Barge at Canal Street

The water at the foot of the stairs stood waist-high and moving. Sol felt the current before she saw it, a push against her legs like a patient hand. She planted her feet wide and thought about it the way she thought about a live line: don't grab, don't guess, go sideways.

She went out the service door and Canal Street came at her all at once. The rain had thinned to a steady hiss, but the water had a sound of its own, a constant low churn with rubbish in it, a lid bobbing, a chair going by on its back like a drowned man. The water ran warmer than it should have. Somewhere below the surface a transformer was cooking its own little weather down in the dark, and she could feel the heat of it through her waders, a wrong bathwater warmth against her shins. Three years she had spent learning the grid through her palms and her ears. She had never once thought she would have to read it through her legs.

She kept her hand on the building and moved by touch, the way she moved through a blacked-out basement. Brick, then a downpipe, then a gap where the café's awning had torn loose and swung. She counted doors. The café. The locksmith. The locksmith's had a pane out, and the water was going in through the empty frame with a murmur like a conversation heard through a wall. Then the corner, where the alley opened down toward Canal Street proper, and that was where Ife had her barge.

She could hear it before she could see it. Three notes over the water: a generator muttering, a rope creaking against a cleat, and a radio tuned two hairs off a station so that Ozzy Kwan's voice came through with a burr on it. The barge lay against the drowned kerb like a shoe on a step, flat and dark and longer than she remembered, the wheelhouse an inkblot at one end and a tarp at the other. The deck riding low. Ife Aduba stood in the bow with a boat-hook, and there was a lamp behind her in the wheelhouse, the one warm thing for a block. It threw a small gold circle on the water and the rest of the world fell away into black, and Ife stood in the middle of that circle like she had set it there on purpose.

Sol stopped where the light stopped and lifted her hands a little, so it could be seen she had nothing in them she meant to use.

"That's far enough," Ife said.

"It's me."

"I can see it's you. That's why I said far enough." The boat-hook came down and knocked on the gunwale, a knuckle of sound. "What do you want, Vieira?"

"The hospital's on its emergency line. You can hear it yourself if you—"

"I'm asking what you want. Not what's happening. Everybody's got a what's-happening. I'm asking what you want."

Sol stood in the water with her boots full of it and her hands beginning to shake again, and she made herself go slow. Ife Aduba had shared the back bench of the training hall with her for three years, close enough to smell the solder on her sleeves, and in three years they had never once been in the same room without a table between them. This had started over a dead training board and a tester that had gone missing, and it had never really stopped. Ife held grudges the way some people hold tools, always in reach, and she had arms that could carry a grudge a long way.

"I need a boat," Sol said. "Yours."

"Course you do." Ife hooked the boat-hook over the gunwale and stood with her weight back. "There's a whole city asking for boats tonight. Go ask somebody who likes you."

"Nobody likes me. I'm not here to be liked." The water rose another inch against her ribs. It climbed slow and deliberate, as if it had been listening and wanted to make its point. "It's four breakers. The waterworks, the market, the tram depot, and one I don't know yet. They've all got to go back by hand or the hospital's line doesn't hold past morning. I can't wade the whole spine of this city asleep with a bag of parts. I need a pilot."

The lamp showed Ife's face. She was not smiling. She had a rain hat on that belonged to her mother, blue with a white bit of cord, and the water was running off the brim onto the deck, and she did not seem to notice it. She looked Sol over from boots to hat, and her eyes stopped at the bag.

"That's Dell's bag," she said.

"He gave it to me."

"Says a lot about Dell." She turned the boat-hook a quarter turn, the way you'd turn a screw. "You had a board in the training hall last spring," she said. "The one with the burnt neutral. You told the assessor I'd left the tester in it and let it go live. You remember that?"

"I remember there was a tester in it and there was a burn."

"I was there all night fixing the thing you broke. Somebody had to. Two in the morning, and I'm in the hall with a torch between my teeth, and I got it right, and I went home and slept on a bench at the tram stop because I'd missed the last one. And you stood up in front of the assessor and gave him a story, and I got written up for a fault I never made." She set the boat-hook down against the rail. "You know what they wrote on my sheet? Careless. You know what they wrote on yours? Promising." She said the word the way you'd say a rotten tooth. "Two words, and they stuck you with the good one. So go on. Get in your own boat, promising."

Sol stood in the current. A little of the warmth left her then, and something colder came up in its place, though not the cold of the river. She wanted to say what she had thought, that night, which was nothing; she had come in at dawn and found the board hot and had reported what she found, and she had not known the tester was Ife's because nobody had told her, because nobody told anybody anything in that hall, and you learned the trade the way you learned a language, by overhearing it. But a grievance was like a live cable: you didn't argue with it. You worked out where the current was going, and you found another path.

She thought about the assessor, too. She thought about the word promising and how it had sat in her chest like a coin ever since, and how she had never once heard it in the training hall without wanting to look around for the person it belonged to.

"The assessor's write-up doesn't hold a hospital," she said. "It doesn't hold lights on. I can't undo it tonight and neither can you. If we get through this, I'll stand up in front of the hall and I'll say what I know and what I don't. But that's a thing that needs a morning on it, and you know it does." She lifted one hand off the wall and let it fall. "Your family's on Barrow Row. That's the hospital district."

Ife said nothing.

"Barrow Row runs off the hospital's transformers," Sol said. "Me and Dell have been over those lines a hundred times. The district's fed from three, and the emergency line takes the load off the wards but it doesn't take the flats. When the emergency line goes, the district goes dark, and the district is your block, your mother's flat, everybody on your stair. The lights in the halls. The lifts. Your gran's machine, if it's the one with the battery, it'll run a while and then it won't." She put her palm flat on the wet gunwale between them. "It's not a favor to me. It's your row. So I'll tell you plain, the way you told me. If you don't like me, fine. Don't bring me back. But get me up the spine."

The water pushed. Somewhere down the street a door gave up and let go underwater with a soft wooden cough, and a smell came up out of it, the cellar smell of a whole street of drowned floors. Ife looked away, down toward the dark where Barrow Row was, though you couldn't see it from here and she knew it.

"Her stair's just a number to me," Ife said at last. "Four flights and a landing and a light that's been gone since I was twelve. You think I got any notion what she does in the dark?" She said it half to herself, and then she turned her head and the half went out of her voice. "Don't answer that."

Ife stepped back from the bow and turned and walked into the wheelhouse. Sol heard her move around in there, heard a drawer, heard the radio register with a hiss, heard the lamp turn up until its glow reached the bank. Then Ife came to the front again, and in her hand was a coil of line.

"Here," she said, and threw it.

Sol caught it against her chest and got the loop over the bow bollard and hauled, hand over hand, her feet slipping on the drowned curb until she found the step she remembered, the one outside the locksmith's, and then she was at the gunwale and up on the deck with water running off her in sheets. The barge rocked and settled. The cabin smelled of diesel and old bread and rain on hot metal, and the warmth of it came through the boards into her boots. Ozzy Kwan's voice ran out of the set, faster than before, with a weight under it now.

"—first casualty reports coming in. On the east side, at the Silt Market, a roofer named Tomas Ilyich, seventy-one, fell from an upper window trying to reach a neighbor's roof. Neighbors have him, they say he is alive, and they say he needs a doctor." A pause, a paper sound, a breath taken a little too close to the microphone. "Listeners, if there is a doctor on the water tonight, the market needs him. And listen—the hospital district is still lit, and I want you to hear those words for what they are. Still lit. That is not the same as safe."

Sol stood dripping on the deck and listened to that, and the little cold thing in her chest settled down into its work. Alive, she thought. Held by neighbors. A roofer falling off a roof in a storm was a broken circuit somewhere, and somebody out there in the dark had closed it with their hands, and that was all anybody could do tonight.

Ife turned the radio down but did not turn it off.

"All right," she said. "Here's what it costs, and I'll say it once, so listen like an electrician. The tram's down in the depot with the water over her. My engine's an old diesel that's been on its last summer since the spring, and a tram like that carries the motors to make her new. So here is the deal. You get me to the depot. I get first claim on the tram's salvage. I pilot, you navigate, and when we're there, whatever we're not carrying we cut loose for the neighbors, but the tram's first call is mine. Say yes or climb back in the water."

Sol heard the terms and turned them over once, the way she'd turn a fitting to see the thread. First claim on the salvage was a big ask. It was also a fair one: no boat meant no route, and the boat belonged to Ife. And there was nothing in it about the breakers, nothing that put a hand on the wheel of the night's real work.

"The tram's not one of the four," she said. "It's not on the map."

"I know it's not on the map. That's the whole point of it. My engine's been dying since spring, and we've been tying it up with wire and hope. Tonight it's a barge. Tomorrow it's a shed with a roof. Say yes."

Up above them the rain had begun again, a fine hard spit that raised points all over the black skin of the water. Sol thought about the fourth breaker, the one Dell had said was off the map, and about a promise to ask a question on the air, and then she stopped thinking about it, because none of it was on this deck yet and the deck had a barge under it.

"Yes," she said. "You get first claim on the tram's salvage. You pilot, I navigate."

"Say it with the terms in it."

"You pilot. I navigate. First claim on the tram's salvage is yours." She held out her hand, wet as it was. "And whatever's left of the night, we split."

Ife looked at the hand a moment with an expression Sol could not read, then took it once, hard, brief, a fastener going home. "And after that," she said, "we're even on the board, and I never hear the word promising again."

"If the board means that much to you, take it. I'll say it on the radio."

Ife's jaw moved once. "Not on the radio."

"You said—"

"I said take it, I didn't say broadcast it. I'm not taking charity off a signal." She let go of Sol's hand and turned to the wheel. "Say it to Dell. Say it to the hall. Say it to the wall, I don't care. A thing said on a radio belongs to everybody, and this one's mine."

"Then it's yours. To Dell and the hall."

"Fine." Ife cast off the bow line in two hard pulls and let the loop pay down through her hands, and she went to the wheel and let the engine come up out of its mutter into a heavy knocking note. She worked the barge out into the street with a slow, patient turn that put the bow against the café awning and used it as a fulcrum. The awning swung and rang with a sound like a struck sheet of tin. The boat came round and lay with the current running up her flank, and the lamp showed the water ahead, brown and full of moving shapes, and the dark beyond the lamp where the streets went on being canals.

"Read me the way, then," Ife said. "I can't see a street sign from here and the signs that are left are under the water."

Sol went up to the bow with the bag against her hip and Dell's dry notebook in her coat, and she set herself to it. The rain came off the lamp in a bright cone and out beyond it the dark was total, and she found she did not need to see any of it. She had walked Canal Street four times a day for three years. The city was in her hands already; she only had to let it out.

"The waterworks is downriver and around the bend where the canal turns," she said. "But the straight run through Silt Market is wrong tonight. The market's got a feeder in the water arcing; you can hear it from here if you shut up for a second."

She shut up herself and listened, one ear closed with her thumb, the other turned out to the dark. It came up out of the rain and the engine: the high stutter, faint as a mosquito at a window, but steady, and she could tell by the shape of it that the bad joint was buried deep and the water around it was doing the work of a ground rod, and if a roofer had fallen in the market, if a doctor paddled in the market, the current would not ask. It would take whoever was in the water. She felt that knowledge go through her like a taste, and she swallowed, and went on.

"Dell used to say the market sits on a hundred-year-old pipe run with modern cable pulled through it, and nobody drew where. So we go the other way, round the back of the lock keeper's lane. Water's deeper there but it's cold water. And there's a bridge with a chain, and the chain will tell us where the true channel is."

Ife came out of the wheelhouse to look at her.

"Say all that again but slower," she said. "And put your hand on the gunwale when you say it so I know you're not making it up."

Sol said it again and put her hand on the gunwale. She made herself go slow, saying the bends of it: past the locksmith's, round the back where the lane narrowed and the houses leaned, under the bridge with the chain hanging like a bell-rope in the dark. Ife watched her hand the whole time, as if the hand and the voice had to agree.

"And the bridge?" Ife said.

"The chain's hung under the arch since before I was born. When the water's high it drags in the current and it rings. You'll hear it, or you won't, but when it rings it rings off the true channel, because the chain was hung off the keystone and the keystone was set on the old pilings."

"And how do you know all that?"

"I don't know it. Dell knows it, and now I've got Dell's book." Sol opened her coat and touched the swollen cover once and closed it again before the rain could get in. "He drew the bridge when he was a young man, and he gave me the book, and the book's drier than the both of us put together."

Ife looked at the coat for a moment, and something moved across her face that was not the grudge. Then she went back and took the wheel, and the barge turned its nose toward the lock keeper's lane and the dark behind the market. The engine beat steady under everything, a big patient heart, anyway, for now. The lamp swung. The bow pushed a slow white wrinkle ahead of it and the wrinkle went out into the black and was gone.

Ozzy's signal came behind them, getting no fainter, following them out into the water on the little handheld in Sol's pocket, which she had brought up on deck without meaning to and set down on the gunwale beside her where she could hear it. "Still lit," he said for the second time, as if he wanted the words to be a rope thrown out to every dark block at once. "Write it down. Say it over your stairs. The hospital is still lit."

Sol sat down by the bag in the wet and ran her thumb along the tester in her pocket, cold brass and warm glass, and listened to the city's small lights and the city's small news coming out of a set that had no right to keep working and kept working anyway.

"Keep that on," Ife said from the wheelhouse. "The radio. Keep it up where I can hear it. I want to know what the man says next."

"So do I," Sol said, and reached down and turned the little set a degree, and the burr went out of Ozzy Kwan's voice, and the water opened ahead of them, dark and endless and quiet except for the rain and the knocking heart of the engine.
