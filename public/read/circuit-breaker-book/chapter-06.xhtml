The Fourth Breaker

They left the barge tied at the bridge with the shed lamps burning over her and the sister's kettle steaming somewhere up above, and they went into the Silt Market on foot with what they could carry.

The boy walked with them as far as the corner of the depot wall, his tools rolled in canvas over his shoulder. There he stopped. He would not go farther into the black water, and Sol did not press him. He gave her a coil of wire and a spare torch battery, then stood on the wall and held the light until they had waded past its reach.

The outfall reach ran colder than the yard, and it moved with a purpose. It came down out of the drowned streets, turned at the market's edge and ran away toward the sea, and Sol, wading to her chest with the bag held high, felt the drag on her legs like a hand hauling on a rope. The reach had a current in it now. The second high water was not here yet, but it had sent its runners ahead.

Ife went first, because Ife was taller, and because she had said she would, and because neither of them had argued. She carried the line over her shoulder and sounded the water ahead with the pole, and every ten steps she called back a number. Four. Four and a half. Three, here, there's a step. Sol counted the numbers and listened to the hum.

The reach had gone quiet. That was the thing she noticed first and could not stop noticing. In the afternoon there had been a dozen notes in the water, the market lamps and the fishmonger's icebox and the arc in the gutter all ringing their own pitch up through the planks and the stone. Now one sound remained, low and even and far off, and it came from above the water instead of through it, and she could not find its edges.

"The market's dark again," she said. "You hear that? The hub's holding. I can feel it holding, the same as when I put it back. But the lamps are out."

Ife stopped and turned. "You said the hub reset lit them. We saw them come on. The whole hall."

"It did. And the old man was going to signal with his lamp when the hub was back, and there was a light in the fishmonger's window when we came in. The hub's still holding. Somebody's cut the feed off it again since." Sol listened. "That's not a fault. A fault rings. That's a hand. Somebody's taken the market off at a remote and they know how to use it, and they're not talking."

"Then talk to them."

"I did. I asked on the air, and the air's the one place a looter won't answer."

They went on. The fishmonger's window lamp was out. The lamp the old man had left burning over the fishmonger's front had gone dark, and behind it the vending machines stood in their row with their doors open, all four of them. The fourth one, where the old man had sheltered, was empty. His folded coat still lay on the shelf and his tin cup still hung from the door. But the stub wrench he had carried since the first surge was not on the shelf and not on the floor, and the water had not taken it, because the water in the machines lay still as a bath.

"He's moved," Sol said. "He told me he'd shout the warning from the hall door and signal with his lamp when the hub was back. He didn't signal. He's not there, and he took his wrench."

"Then he's dead or he's hiding."

"He's not dead. A man who strips copper with a stub wrench in a flood doesn't die easy. And he had a line to the woman across the way. He'd have gone to her before he'd have gone into the water." Sol stood still in the water a moment and made herself hear the reach the way she heard a switch-gear vault. "There's someone under the market hall. Listen. The hall's floor is over the arches, and the arches are full, and there's a knocking in the water."

The knocking was small and regular. It stopped when she moved and started again when she was still, and it came from under the hall's stone steps.

Ife had the torch up. "Something's caught."

"Somebody." Sol was already moving. "Leave the pole. This way."

The knocking was a boat hook, an old man's arm working it against a beam, and under the hall's steps, in an air pocket the size of a cupboard, they found the old man from the vending machine half in the water with his shirt caught on a broken paling and his good arm holding the hook up.

It took time to get him out and time to get him up the steps and time to find that his left arm was not broken and his chest was not full of water, and all of that time the water kept coming down the reach and the market stayed dark.

"Hub's still good," the old man said, when he could say anything. "I went to the hall door with my lamp the way I said I would, and the hub was holding and the lamps were bright, and I had my lamp up to signal and I was shouting the news down the street to Mrs. Okafor across the way. Then a man came round the corner in a skiff with a wrench in his hand and a remote in his pocket, and he took the market off at the remote like a man turning off a light in his own house. The hub never moved. He cut the feed above it. I shouted at him and he looked at me and put his hand on the remote again, and I knew he was going to hold her dark till he'd got what he came for. So I went after him. In the water. In the dark. I am an old fool." He coughed. "He went to the hall's arches where the cable runs, and I followed him in with my wrench, and then the water came up and I lost my footing, and the wrench went out of my hand and that's the last of it. That's all I know."

"What did he want?"

"Copper. What do they ever want." The old man got his breath. "The hall's main comes down the arch and it's fat as your arm, and if a man with a wrench and an empty skiff has half an hour and no one to see him, this market's dark for a month."

Sol looked at Ife. Ife looked back, and her face said the thing she was not saying, which was that they had four hundred yards of reach and one breaker left and a hospital line and a high tide, and a looter in a skiff was not on anybody's list of what to fix tonight.

"We can't stop for him," Ife said at last. "Say it, and I'll say it with you."

"We can't stop for him," Sol said, "but we can mark him." She got the coil of wire the boy had given her, went down the hall's steps into the water, and at the place where the fat main came down out of the arch she tied the wire round the cable in a loop with a tag of the boy's canvas on it. She wedged a scrap of the old man's broken paling in the bracket, so a man pulling the cable would feel it catch and know somebody had been there and would be back. "There. He'll see that and he'll know he's not alone in his own dark. It's not fixing it. It's marking it."

"Marking it is not the market lit," the old man said. "And my wrench is in the water somewhere and I'll want it back."

"You'll have it back," Sol said. "I'll come down for it myself when the market's lit. The market can wait an hour and the hospital can't, and the man with the remote will still be here at dawn and I will be too. That's a promise."

The footbridge rose out of the dark at the far end of the reach, a low arch of wet stone with a handrail going over the top and the market's old outfall running under it, and on the arch a ladder.

The ladder was rusted to lace. It went up the face of the arch from the waterline and it had been climbing that face a long while, and the lowest four rungs were under the water and the rest were scabbed with rust and one had been bent out at the middle by something that had hit it and not been asked.

Above the ladder the footing was a black opening with an iron grille across its lower half, and on the grille a brass plate with old paint on it and the paint said PUMPING HOUSE, and under the paint someone had scratched a date that had weathered almost away.

The radio came up clear here, the way it did in low ground, and Ozzy was in the middle of a sentence.

"—say that again," he was saying. "Say the part about the schematic."

Dell's voice arrived flat and papered and old. "There's no schematic. I've said it three times and I'll say it once more and then I'm done saying it. The fourth is not on the grid's drawings and it never was. It's a manual island switch. I put it in myself in the pumping house under the market outfall, years back, because the section link the company drew was on the wrong side of the outfall pumps and there was no way to test the hospital feeder without shutting down the whole east bank every time. I put a switch where a switch needed to be and I didn't put it on paper, and by the time I could have put it on paper it was too late to put it on paper, because a switch that isn't on paper is a switch a man can't be caught for." A breath. "So it isn't on paper, Ozzy. It's on the arch and it's on my hand and it's on the air now. That's the whole of the story."

The radio said nothing for a moment. Then Ozzy said, "Sol Vieira. Are you hearing this."

"I'm hearing it."

"Then answer me straight, because the city's hearing it too." Ozzy's voice had lost the broadcaster's rhythm and found something worse, a flatness that was anger held still. "An unpermitted switch. Unrecorded. Off every drawing the crews carry. Is that what I just put on the air? A man has to climb a wet ladder in a flood to a box that no inspector has ever seen, in a building nobody has surveyed, and turn it, and if he turns the wrong thing or the box has been rewired by a stranger we lose the hospital line. That is what we are asking a seventeen-year-old to do tonight. Am I hearing it right."

"You're hearing it right," Sol said.

"Then hear me back." A rustle of paper. "There are official channels. There's a municipal engineer of works and there's a flood emergency order and there's a rule that says nobody touches an unrecorded installation, because an unrecorded installation is how people die. If I put this on the air as it stands, some boat will put out from the east bank with an inspector in it and an opinion, and I will have made that happen. So tell me why I shouldn't hold this back until morning."

"Because the hospital's on its emergency line and the emergency line is a battery of hearts and it doesn't run forever," Sol said. She was standing in the water with her arm through the ladder and she could feel the current working at her knees. "And because if you hold it back, we climb the ladder anyway, and the city finds out anyway, and it finds out from whoever else heard you, and it finds out as a secret. Tell it now and tell it whole. Tell it with the ladder and the arch and the plate that says pumping house and me on the ladder with a bag and Ife Aduba on the water behind me and the fourth switch off first, bound, then back. Tell them it's a lawbreaker's switch. I'll not argue that. It's a lawbreaker's switch and it's a right switch and the hospital's on the far end of it."

The radio stayed quiet. Then Ozzy said, slowly, "I'll put it on. All of it. The plate and the ladder and off-first-bound-then-back, and the part where Dell Marchetti broke the law." A pause. "And Sol, there will be consequences. I'm telling you now, on the air, so you can't say you weren't told. There will be consequences and they will come before morning."

"Let them."

The water came up the arch while the radio talked, and the city talked back.

Some of it came off the handheld, one voice after another, and most of them wanted to know which reach and how deep and whether the ladder held. One of them said, "The market's dark and it was lit an hour ago and now we hear a lawbreaker's switch is what's holding the hospital? A lawbreaker's switch. Who says it's the right one?" And another said, "I heard Dell Marchetti's voice for thirty years and if Dell says the fourth is under the outfall, it's under the outfall."

Then a voice Sol knew, an old woman's, rough and plain, the old woman from the culvert bridge, saying, "I'm at the depot and I've had my hands in the yard's water since before the second surge. You want to know about Dell Marchetti's switches, ask the block behind this bridge. Ask the market hall. Ask anyone who's had a light on tonight. The law didn't light a lamp in this city tonight. Dell Marchetti did." And somebody laughed, and somebody else said, "That's my mother, and she's right," and the whole thing turned in the water like a boat coming round.

But some of it held its ground.

A man's voice came off the handheld, and it was not angry, which was worse. It was tired and it was careful and it was very reasonable. "I'm on the Silt Market footbridge," the man said, "and I've been on it since the second surge and I have watched the water come up over that ladder. I'm not going to say the old man's wrong. I'm going to say I don't know that he's right, and I'm the one standing in the water with a boat, and I've got my children on the far side of the market and no light on the far side of the market, and a man tells me to put my boat in a current and go to a switch that isn't drawn on any paper because he put it in himself and didn't say. I don't know him. My children don't know him. Why would I trust that? Why would any of us?"

Sol stood in the water with her hand on the ladder and gave him no answer at once, and the current pulled at her and the radio waited.

"Because you don't have to trust him," she said at last. "You have to trust the switch. The switch is a piece of brass on a board and a wire going out of it to the pumps and another going out of it to the hospital, and if it's the wrong switch, the wrong switch shows itself. It'll ring wrong and it'll run hot and it'll come back dark, and I'll know before I put my hands in and I'll tell you on the air and you'll hear it and you'll put your boat where you want it. But if nobody climbs the ladder, the switch stays where it is and the hospital stays on its battery and the battery runs out and then we all get to be right about a dead man's paper." She got her foot on the lowest rung and it held and it was cold. "My name's Sol Vieira and I'm Dell Marchetti's apprentice and I'm going up the ladder. Say what you like about the law. I'll be up top."

The man was quiet. Then he said, "I'll hold the light."

"Then hold it on the ladder and not on me."

Ife had the bag's strap in one hand and the spare line in the other, and she paid the line round the arch's footing and pulled it taut and made it fast to the footbridge's rail, and she stood in the water with her chest against the stone and her hands braced on the line and her feet set wide in the current.

"Go," she said. "And hear me first. I'll say this once. Your Dell put a switch in a wall and never told a soul, and the hospital's riding on it tonight, and my mother's block is riding on it, and half the market's riding on it, and the man who did the not-telling is the man who drew the map wrong when he was twenty and was too ashamed to fix it when he was forty. I don't like riding on a lawbreaker's switch. I don't like it at all. But I'd like my mother's block in the dark a deal less." She tightened her hands on the line. "So go up. And know that every one of us on this water is standing on a man's secret, and if that secret's wrong we all go down together, and I'll be holding this line when you find out which it is."

Sol looked down at her in the water and saw that Ife's jaw was set and her hands were white on the line and she was not going anywhere.

"If it's wrong," Sol said, "the first thing you'll hear is me telling you it's wrong. Not the radio. Me."

"I'll be listening for you and not the radio," Ife said. "Go."

The ladder was wet and the rust came off it on her gloves in flakes like dried blood, and the fourth rung from the bottom gave under her foot and she caught herself on the fifth and went on. The current pushed at her legs and the bag weighed on her shoulder and the water dripped off her boots and hit the water below with a sound she could hear all the way up. The arch's face was cold and alive with rain and river. Behind the grille the pump house was black, and out of the black came a low steady note and a bad harmonic over it, and somewhere under that a small ticking like a clock with a bent hand.

Halfway up she stopped, because her foot had found solid ground and her hand had found a rail, and she hung there a moment and got her breath and listened.

"Sol!" Ife's voice came up sharp from below.

"I'm up," Sol called down. "I'm up and I can hear the switch." She set her boot on the sill above the grille and brought her face to the iron. The pump house's note climbed through the grille into the bones of her wrist, low and long and steady and wrong at the edges. "The switch is in there and it's hot. It's been hot a long while, and there's water on it since the first surge, same as Dell said, and it's ticking like a kettle that's been on too long."

She got her glove off and worked two fingers through the grille, and the hot brass of the link found her knuckles, and the tick came through it and went up her arm.

"Off first," she said to the dark and to the radio and to Ife below. "Off first, then bind it, then back."

The radio at her hip said, "We have her at the ladder. The city has her at the ladder. Off first, Sol. Off first, then bind it, then back."

She hooked her fingers over the lever and set her boot against the iron and pulled, and it went, with a grind and a jump, and the tick stopped and the hum fell away and the pump house went quiet enough to hear the water running beyond the arch.

"Switch is off," she called. "I'm binding it now."

The binding took both hands and her teeth on the strap, and the pump house stayed quiet the whole while, and when it was done she pushed the lever back up and the note climbed again and rang even and stayed even.

"Back on, and she's even," she called. "That's the fourth. Tell the city the fourth is done."

The radio took it from her and gave it back twice over, and down on the water Ife let her breath go, the one she had been holding since the yard.

Then the water came up the arch behind her and the second high tide came in down the reach and the market stayed dark and the footbridge's rail rang in the current, and Sol Vieira climbed back down the rusted ladder into Ife's waiting hands with the switch warm in the clean air and the hospital's line carrying load on the far side of it.
