The Sound of the Grid

The control room smelled of hot dust and rain. Sol Vieira had been breathing it since six o'clock and had stopped noticing, until the storm pressed its shoulder against the building and the smell turned to wet concrete and the flat mineral breath of the delta coming up the pipes.

Five hours in this room. She could have listed its sounds with her eyes shut, and often did. The transformers in the back room sang a low, steady note. The annunciator clicked. Every forty minutes the kettle in the corner gave its pre-boil rattle, and nobody ever made tea. She had grown up in the noise of a city, and the control room was that noise with all the doors shut, concentrated, exact. She liked it better than almost anywhere.

"Read it to me," Dell said.

He sat in the good chair, the one with the wheel that squeaked, his cap pushed back, his eyes closed. He had been closing his eyes more lately. A week to retirement, he'd told everyone. A week, and then a chair that didn't squeak and a boat he meant to finish. He said it the way people say things they've rehearsed.

Sol stood at the annunciator panel with one hand flat against the steel, the way you'd hold a horse's neck. The board was a wall of small windows, each one a line out into the city. Normally she heard the grid as a low chord: transformers humming at fifty hertz, relays clicking busy, the drum-tight silence of things holding their breath correctly. Tonight the chord had a burr in it. Something out there was arcing where nothing should arc.

"Zone four," she said. "The waterworks line. It's not tripping. It's whistling."

"Whistling."

"High, up under the vault. Like a kettle that hasn't decided yet."

Dell opened his eyes. "Then it's decided," he said. "Get the map."

The map hung on the wall under a sheet of plastic, hand-drawn, Dell's, the north arrow crooked and the pipe runs inked in three colors of pen. Some of the labels were his private shorthand, and she had been learning it for three years the way you learn a language from a man who doesn't know he's teaching. Rain hit the windows behind it in long slaps. Through the glass the city lay as a dark low field of roofs, the canals glittering where the streetlights found them, and the canals had climbed out of their beds. Sol had grown up in Port Alaric. She knew the canal edges like the back of her own hand: the low curb along Canal Street, the water at the Silt Market coming up to your ankles at full tide, the slick green line it left on the bricks. The water stood over the curb now. It stood over the curb and kept finding street.

"How high's the mark along there now?" Dell asked, without turning.

"Two steps up the market ramp."

"Two." He said it flat, but his hand found the edge of the desk and stayed there.

"Bottom of the hour," he went on. "Read the whole board."

She did. She went along the annunciator with her palm and then with her ear, pressing the warm metal, shutting her other ear with her thumb. The hum held at fifty cycles everywhere, but the second harmonic had gone ragged, and every so often the whole chord dipped as if a giant had held a breath. That was load. Somewhere a big line was dropping out and something else was picking it up. She felt it in the panel, in her wrist, in the small bones of her hand.

"Who's picking it up?" Dell said.

"The tram depot's absorbing some. The market's sagging and taking more than it should. Feeder's running hot. Listen to it. You can hear the market building breathing." She moved her hand along. "And the hospital's on backup. No. It's drawing through the normal line, but the normal line's about to let go. The breaker's hot."

"How hot?"

She touched the panel over the label and drew her finger away fast. "Ouch hot."

"Then get the big book. The one with the coffee on it. We log it, and we call the substation, and they send a man in a truck." Dell said it slowly, the way he said things he'd said a thousand times. He stayed in the chair. His eyes stayed off the rain.

The phut came through the floor. Not through the air; through the building's bones, up through the slab, into the soles of her boots. Sol knew that sound. Phase to ground. A line had gone down somewhere and found water, and now the water was carrying it around like a rumor.

Her hand left the panel and her feet went apart before she had decided to move.

"Talk to me, child," Dell said.

"Zones three and four just lost a phase. The whistle stopped. That's the vault. The vault's flooded."

"Don't guess."

"I'm not guessing, I'm reading." She heard her own voice go clipped and short and let it. "Copper's dead in the water, so the fault current has gone to ground, and the ground is the whole street. Everything downstream is tripping at once now. Listen. Hear that chatter? That's the hospital feeder trying to reclose into a fault. It'll keep trying. It'll keep trying until it melts." She looked at him. "Somebody's got to stop it trying."

"Nobody's stopping it from this room tonight," Dell said. "Not with the automatic gone."

Dell was up. He crossed the room in three long steps, and the chair squeaked behind him as if it were relieved. On his way past the bench he pulled out two things: a canvas toolbag, stiff with old oil, and a notebook, swollen at the spine, the cover warped like a wet board. He set them on the desk between them and stood looking at them, and Sol noticed his hands. She had watched those hands for three years. They could strip a wire by feel and find the right fuse in a dark bag. Now they were doing a small thing she had never seen them do. They were hesitating.

"Radio," he said. "Ozzy's frequency. Now."

The grid control room kept a bank of radios in a steel cabinet, and the emergency set drew from a battery that drew from nothing else. Sol spun the dial. Static, static, then a voice, warm and rapid, a voice that sounded as if it were smiling at you over a fence.

"Repeat for the west side and the market: water over the barriers at Blackgate. I say again, water over the barriers at Blackgate. This is not the tide. This is the surge, and it is early." A pause, a papery rustle. "For listeners on Canal Street and the Silt Market, move to your upstairs floors. Do not go down. The tide will not help us tonight; it stopped helping an hour ago."

Sol held the dial and let the room go quiet around the voice.

"He's saying the barriers are gone."

"The barriers are a hundred years old, and they were built for a different river," Dell said. "Get your coat. Get the bag."

"Substations have phones to call. We don't."

"The phones are dead. The lines run along the canal, and the canal is in the street." He put the notebook into her hands and closed her fingers around it. His hands were cold and dry and steady. "You know what a breaker does. And you know how to read a line by ear better than I ever did. That's not a kindness, it's a fact. I've been in this trade forty-one years and I have known three people who could hear a bad joint through a wall, and you're the only one still standing." Something came into his voice now that she had never heard in three years, a tone like a door not quite opening. "There's four breakers that matter tonight. When they trip, they've got to be reset by hand, because the automatic's gone. Every one's got a place it lives. Three of them are in there."

"In where?"

"The book. My book." He looked at the water sheeting down the window. "We wrote a map together, you and me, and I never showed you the whole of it. Now you'll have it. Most of it."

She opened the cover. Page after page of Dell's crooked hand, pipe runs and cable pulls and little sketches of vault doors, some of them so old the ink had gone brown. Her own hand appeared in the margins too, in pencil, in her square schoolgirl letters from three years ago: CHECK THIS. ASK ABOUT THIS. She had drawn a small arrow and written, in her first month, bottom line looks wrong. She could not remember writing it. She must have been sixteen, then, and sure she knew nothing.

"Three of them are in the book," Dell said again, and now he was watching her face. "The fourth is not. The fourth is off every map there is, and I'm the reason it's off. So hear me. You'll not find it on paper. When you've done the other three, you ask me where it lives, and you ask me on the air so Ozzy hears it, because I've kept a thing too long and I'm tired of carrying it."

"Then tell me now."

"Not on a roof in a storm. Ask me when the three are back. Ask me on the air." He tapped the notebook once with his knuckle. "This will get you through the first of them, and then the second, and then the third. It gets you nothing past that. The book is honest and the book is incomplete, and you'll have to be the part that isn't written. Understand?"

"I understand the book can't help with the fourth."

"That's the whole of it." He almost smiled.

The phut came again, closer, and this time the lights browned, the long low sag of a big motor being dragged down, and one bank of the annunciator went dark. Then another. The room was lit now only by the little red eyes over the exits and the gray light off the water outside. The water outside had quit being outside. It stood at the foot of the stairs, and it lay across the landing like a sheet of tarnished steel, and it was climbing.

The glass in the windows had begun to flex, in and out, with a sound like a low struck string, and the room's steel cabinet was humming a note she had never heard it hum. A room was a thing that could be afraid. She hadn't known that before.

"Roof," Dell said. "Leave it. All of it, leave it. Just the bag."

"Your coat's on the hook."

"Leave it."

They went up the narrow back stairs with the water lipping at their heels. It came up after them steady as a clock. Three flights, four. Dell took them slowly, one hand on the rail, and Sol went behind him with the bag knocking her hip, and neither of them said it, but both of them could hear the water changing below: the suck and slap of it finding the switchgear cabinets, the busy electrical drowning noises.

Halfway up, Dell stopped. He put his hand out sideways and she took it, and they stood on the landing in the dark with the water just below, and he said, "I'd like to have finished the boat."

"You'll finish it."

"I'd like to have finished it before tonight," he said. "That's not the same thing." Then he started climbing again.

The roof door stuck. Sol shouldered it, and the storm came in with a wallop that took her hat. Rain in the face like gravel. Wind that had opinions. They got out and got the door shut behind them and stood in the rain on the flat tar roof with the whole city below.

Port Alaric was going out.

She saw it the way you'd see a body go to sleep. First the long bright string of the market district, one end of it to the other, a whole street of café lights and shop signs plunging together, the grid holding on, holding on, then letting go with a kind of sigh you could hear in your teeth. Then the waterworks and the low neighborhoods, block by block. Then the tram depot. Then the hospital, which flickered once, twice, like a struck match, and held.

The rain was warm on her face, which was somehow worse than cold.

"Those generators held," Dell said. "Good old boys. That's the emergency line, that is. Runs the wards, the lifts, the blood fridge, the lights in the operating rooms. That's what we're buying time for."

"How long?"

He gave her no answer. That was an answer.

Sol looked down at her city and made herself find Canal Street through the dark, find the Silt Market, and she couldn't, because the streets and the canals had become one black moving thing. A city was a circuit. She'd known that since she was nine years old and her father let her help him wire a lamp; everything that made the city be a city was a current running through copper in the dark, and everywhere the copper went, people could be people. Lights. Lifts. Refrigerators. Radios. Somewhere out there in the dark there were people on upstairs floors listening to a radio, and the radio was what made them a city too, and the radio was Ozzy Kwan.

The emergency set had gone with the stairs. But the bag was in her arms, and she pulled out the little handheld radio Dell kept in it for the substation men, and because the world is sometimes kind, its batteries were good and its antenna was long.

Static, static. Then the warm rapid voice, thinner now, with a hiss on it like rain on a drum, but steady.

"And this is Ozzy Kwan in the tower on Perch Street, and I am going to stay with you." A crackle. A breath. "All right, Port Alaric, here is what I know, and I will tell you the minute I know more. The surge passed the barriers just after midnight. Power is down in the market and the waterworks and the tram depot and most of the west side. The hospital is on its emergency line." The voice paused, and when it came back it had gone careful. "Listeners, that line is a battery of hearts, and it does not run forever. If anyone knows a soul who can get to the grid, tell them to find Ozzy Kwan. I'll be here."

Sol stood in the rain and her hands were shaking, and it wasn't the cold.

"Child," Dell said.

"I'm reading," she said. "I'm reading the city." Her voice was clipped and short. She made herself breathe. "The hospital's still up. Four breakers, you said. Three in the book. Where's the first one?"

"The waterworks. Down the street and around, where the canal bends. You'll have to swim to the vault, more likely than wade."

"Then I'll swim."

"That's the way." He put his hand on her shoulder and squeezed once. Then he let go and looked at the bag, and at her, and at the bag again. "Now listen to the last of it. Three of them are in the book, and the book's a good map. But the water's already making the map a liar, so read it by sound and touch before you read it by paper. And the fourth." He stopped. He looked out at the dark city with the one lit hospital in the middle of it. "The fourth is off the map because I drew the map wrong, and I'll not tell it on a roof. When the three are back, ask me where it lives. Ask me on the air so Ozzy hears. Then go and do it."

He picked up the bag and put the strap over her head, one arm and then the other, like a coat. "The bag's got gutted toasters in it. Don't look at me like that. Coils, fuses, a coil of good wire, a jumper I made out of a tram bond. Everything in that bag is dead, and everything in that bag will bring something back to life. That's all the trade is, and it's all I ever had to teach you."

Behind him, far off, a transformer let go with a flat boom that rolled down the river, and somewhere a crowd made a sound, a long low sound, and then the rain took it.

Sol thought of the boat, half-planked in some shed she had never been shown. She thought of the chair that squeaked. She thought, with a sudden and unreasonable fury, that he had had a week left, a week, and the storm couldn't read a calendar.

"Go on," Dell said, and it was a rasp, and it trailed off at the end. "Go on. I'll be on the radio. I'll be the ugly voice behind Ozzy's pretty one. And Sol."

She stopped at the door.

"Read it by sound and touch. Not by my book alone. My book's a map, and the water's already made the map a liar." He turned up his collar. "Three in the book. One off it. One circuit. That's all a city is, in the end. Somebody's got to close it."

Sol went down the stairs into the water, and the water was cold and busy and full of the wrong kind of sound, and the notebook was dry in her coat and the dead things in the bag were waiting to be alive. Above the rain, small and crackling and warm, Ozzy Kwan said, I'll be here, and Port Alaric in its dark listened, and Sol listened with it, and went.
