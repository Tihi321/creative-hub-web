The Chain

The first three rungs were dry. The fourth was wet. By the sixth she was in it to the knee, and the cold climbed her leg like a hand closing.

She went slowly. Two rungs had furred down to the iron and one was gone altogether, so she found the stile with her boot and took her weight on her arms and went down past it. The bag rode high between her shoulders. The line came off her waist and up behind her, and she could feel Ife at the top of it, taking in the slack a hand at a time.

"Past the gap," she called up. "Leave me a fathom to work with."

"Leave it," Ife called back, and then, to someone behind her, "you heard her."

The water took her to the waist at the eighth rung. Black down here, and it moved in a slow circular way that said the vault was breathing under it. Every push found the wall and ran round her.

She pressed her knuckles to the wall and listened. The vault came up through the stone. The flutter was a tick now, steady, and under it a low burring note in the casing, the sound a thing makes when it is being asked to be a kettle and is trying to oblige.

"Talk to me," the handheld said from her hip. "The city's listening and the city's quiet."

"The tracking's found a path and it's holding it," Sol said. "The casing's burring. It's not hours and I never said it was hours." She got her boot onto the landing, a foot down under the water, and stood with the flood to the bottom of her ribs and made herself breathe twice. "I'm on the landing. I can see the box."

The box was a squat iron thing under the service stair, hinge on one side, hasp on the other, and the grille Dell had put over it in his second year was over it still. The hinge bolts had eaten themselves into the iron. When Sol got her fingers through the grille and pulled, the whole box shifted on the wall and the grille did not move at all.

"Grille's seized," she said. "The box is on a bracket and I don't want to pull the bracket off the wall with a live link in it. Dell, the grille you put on has seized. Tell me you didn't bolt it through the bracket—"

"I bolted it to the box," Dell said, old and careful and wide awake. "There's a set screw at the bottom left of the hinge plate. Small, slotted, under the water. I put it there because I was twenty and I thought a hidden screw was a clever screw. You'll need a driver with a bit of length and a blade no wider than the slot, or you'll chew it. And Sol—" He stopped. "The box will be live to touch with the grille on and the water on. The earth's good, I was young, I wasn't stupid. But the hinge plate will be floating. Don't put the driver in and lean on the box."

"Then I'm not leaning on the box."

She found the set screw with her fingertips and knew at the touch of it that her screwdriver would be too thick, and she had not brought the small blade because she had not known about the screw.

She came up out of the stoop and wiped her eyes with her wrist.

"It's a small slot and my driver's too wide, and if I chew it we're out here with a grille and no bit to fit it," she said. "And I can't get the bag off my back with one hand in the water and the other on the ladder. I need it passed. A thin blade. A stub wrench, four-inch, for the hinge nut once the screw's out. And a fuse."

"A fuse," Ozzy said. "Say it for the city."

"The box has a cartridge fuse and it's been under water over a live link all night, and I'll not put the link back on a wet fuse. A cartridge, or fuse wire and a holder, or anything a man in a workshop would have in a tobacco tin. And copper. Clean strip, because when the grille's open the bracket will want a shim or the box will ride on the wall. That's what I need. A thin blade, a stub wrench, a fuse and copper. And I need it now and not in a boat."

The top of the ladder was quiet for two breaths.

Then Ife said, in the voice she used on a barge when she had costed something out, "You heard her. Nobody moves. Nobody goes down that wall and nobody stands on the edge to be helpful. Sol Vieira needs tools and she needs them passed, and I have done this on a deck since I was six. You pass hand to hand and you do not throw. Anything that will sink goes into a hand and stays in a hand till another hand has it." A pause. "Who's nearest the stair."

"I am," said the old woman from the culvert.

"Then you're first and you don't move. Boy with the tools, you're second. What's in that roll?"

"Wrenches," said the sister's boy. "Drivers. Pliers. A little torch with a bad switch."

"Then you're the shop." Ife's boots scraped on the stone. "Engineer. You're tall and you've got a light and you've been standing in that boat doing sums. Come here and be the top of my chain."

There was a pause, and the searchlight did not move, and Sol, down in the water with her hand on the ladder, heard the engineer say, "I'm not sure it's proper for me to—"

"It's proper to hand a wrench to a woman," the old woman said. "It's on no section of any code and I've looked."

The engineer's boots came along the parapet, and Sol heard it: the small scuffle and settle of people standing shoulder to shoulder in a wet place and agreeing, without a vote, to be a line.

It came down the ladder hand to hand.

Sol had thought, in the control room, that a city was a circuit. She had thought it again at the market and at the pumping house, and she had thought she understood it. What came down the ladder to her in the dark was the thing itself.

The stub wrench came first. She fitted the jaws to the hinge nut, got her boot on the box bracket and bore down, and the nut gave, and she got it into her pocket.

"Nut's off," she said. "Small blade."

The small blade came down with somebody's initials burnt into the tape with the side of a soldering iron. She put it into the set screw with two fingers and a steady wrist and turned it a quarter turn and felt it slip and reset it and turned again, and the screw came out with the small grinding sound of a thing that has waited thirty years to be asked.

"Grille's loose. Hold the light right there."

She got both hands out of the water and swung the grille wide on the open hasp. The box's door was bare, the same soaked iron as the pump house, the lock gone green and the hinge to rust, and the door did not want to open on a cold night in a flood.

"Wrench. The stub's too small for the lock. Six-inch."

"Wrench," Ife called up the ladder, and the word went up the chain of hands, and back down it came. Sol fitted it to the lock's square shank and pulled, and the lock turned stiff and hard and then all at once like a key in a door that has not been opened in a summer, and the box door came open against her chest.

Water poured out of it. The inside smelled of hot dust and wet iron, and there in the middle of it was the section link, and it was hot. She put her hand near it and did not touch it. The handle was down. The water had got into the mechanism and the old grease had set like wax.

"It's the link," she said, and her voice arrived battered and thin. "The handle's jammed in the off position. It went off at seven and it's sat off in salt water ever since and the grease has set. I can put a strap wrench on it or break the seal on the shaft and get the lever to give, and either one is a pull on a live box in a flood. Get me the copper and the fuse, because when this goes back in it goes back with a clean fuse and a shimmed bracket or it doesn't go back at all."

The copper came down in pieces. A strip of clean building copper off a coil, and a piece folded double, and a stub of half-inch pipe somebody had hacksawed for a shim and not finished smoothing, and the old woman's spare lamp lead with the plug cut off.

"Copper," Sol said, and put the pieces on the landing under the water and got her hand back on the lever.

The fuse came down in a tobacco tin wrapped in a rag, and the rag was wet through and the tin inside was dry. Sol opened it with fingers stiff with cold and found three cartridges, one used and two new, and a coil of fuse wire and a ceramic holder with the wire already cut, and a note in handwriting she did not read because the ink had run.

"Fuse," she said. "That's a fuse. Whoever had the tobacco tin is a good man and I'll say his name on the air."

"Can't," Ife said. "He's left. He gave it and went back over the parapet. The woman up the top says he has the arcing feeder in the fish gutter and he wants it safe before tomorrow's school run. He says if the market's going to be dark, it can be dark and grounded."

Sol laughed, and it hurt her chest, and she stopped.

She put the new cartridge in her coat pocket where it was dry, and set the folded copper behind the bracket and turned the hinge nut onto it and drove it home, and the box sat back against the wall and stopped riding.

"Bracket's shimmed," she said. "Now I'm going to break the lever."

She put the six-inch on the lever and both hands on the six-inch and set her boots and put her shoulder in, and the lever moved a hair and stopped. She reset and put her shoulder in again and felt the seal give with a crack she heard through her arms, and the box rattled, and the vault under the courtyard said something in its burring note, and Sol held the lever still and waited.

"Off," she said. "It's off. Oh, that's quiet. The burring's gone. It's still ticking, it'll tick a while, but the tracking's lost its path. Now I wait. Ife, hold the line, I'm going to count."

She counted the way Dell had told her, with her palm pressed to the box in the cold and the searchlight on the ladder above her and the city on a handheld in her wet pocket. She counted to thirty and then to thirty again, and on the second thirty the tick under her hand slowed and the note over the top went away, and the vault came back up through the box into her hands as the thing it was supposed to be, a quiet heavy thing sitting in its water, waiting to be needed.

"Tick's going away," she said. "Fuse. I need a hand that isn't wet to hold the inside of the tin."

A hand came down over the top of the ladder. It belonged to the old woman, who had come down the ladder while Sol was counting, with the line through her elbow and the lamp on her belt, and she was standing on the third rung with her boots in the water, and her hand was dry because she had held it up out of the rain all the way down.

"Inside the tin," she said. "Put your wet hand on nothing that must be dry."

Sol put the wet hand on the box and the dry hand in the tin and got the cartridge out and set it into the carrier and turned it home, and the old woman held the tin's lid up as a roof against the drizzle. When the cover was on, Sol put her hand flat on the door of the box and listened to the whole of it through the iron.

"Off and quiet," she said. "Binding it now. And then back. Ife—"

"I hear you."

"If the vault talks when I put it back, I take it off again and I say so and we all go up the ladder and the city hears it from me. If it doesn't talk, we're done and I'm coming up."

"Then put it back," Ife said. "The tea's on the parapet and the engineer's holding the light and nobody here is going anywhere."

Sol set her shoulder and turned the lever back, slowly, the way you turn something that has been out of its proper place all night. The link seated. The box took the load. The vault said nothing.

Nothing. Just the quiet hum of a transformer doing its work under a flood, and the rain, and up above them a voice saying, "She's got it," and then the handheld opened and Ozzy's words filled it, not flat at all but with an old broadcaster's crack in them.

"Port Alaric, on the wall of the hospital district. The section link is bound and back, and I am going to say this once and say it plainly. The hospital district is still lit. The wards are lit. The lifts are running. The blood fridge is cold. The flats in Barrow Row are reading their own meters and Mrs. Okafor has put on her hall light. The district held."

The climbing up was worse than the climbing down, because her legs had gone to something that was not quite her own.

But the line came down and she put it round her waist and Ife took it, and the old woman came up behind her and put a hand on her belt and said, "One rung at a time. I've got the back of you and the girl's got the line and the man's got the light, and you don't have to do any of it by yourself."

She came up the rusted ladder one rung at a time and got her boots onto the stone of the district wall and stood in the rain with her arms hanging.

The parapet was full of people. Ife got the line off her waist and put it round her shoulders without a word, because Ife Aduba did not hug people but she could rig a line. The old woman went straight to the sister's boy. The old man from the vending machine came forward with Sol's coat and helped her into it. It had been dry in his arm and he had stood in the rain all two hours of it and it was sodden, and she put it on and did not mind, because it was her coat and he had held it.

"You kept your eyes on the switch," he said. "There's no shame in that. It was the wrench I asked for."

The engineer came along the parapet with his folder and his helmet off and his hair flat to his head.

"Miss Vieira," he said. "I want to say it correctly and I want to say it while everyone's here, because the record's going to say it and I don't want the record and me to be two different men." He turned the folder over in his hands. "The link is reset. The vault is stable. The district is lit. The record will show that the link was unrecorded, and that the pumping-house island switch was unrecorded, and that both were installed by a licensed electrician of this city on his own initiative and never submitted for survey. That is a code matter and it's not a small one." He paused. "The record will also show that they held the wards of a hospital through a second high tide, and that the man who installed them put it on the air with his own name, and that he sent his apprentice down a flooded wall to fix what he had left off the paper. I'll be submitting both in the same report."

"What does that mean for Dell?" Sol said.

"Miss Vieira—"

"No. What does it mean for him. Not for the record. For him." She stood straight, which took doing. "He's a week off his retirement and he's been thirty years on this city's plant and he put a switch in a wall where nobody could see it and it saved a hospital tonight, and I want to know what you're going to do about it, because the city's listening and I'd sooner it heard it from you than read it off a form."

The engineer looked at her a long moment. "His license will be reviewed," he said. "That's not mine to decide and it's not mine to withhold, and I'd be lying if I said otherwise. A review is a process. It has a hearing and a panel and a man is allowed to speak at it. He's already done the hardest part of that tonight, which is to say it himself, out loud, with his name on it, before anyone asked him to." He looked at the folder, and then at the people on the parapet. "But the review will also have the district's word, if the district cares to send it. You might see that the district has its say."

"Then the district will have its say," the old woman said. "I've got a complaint already drafted in my head. It's got a culvert in it and a kettle."

"There," the engineer said. "An unpermitted installation and an undrafted complaint. The record will be a lively one."

Dell's voice came off the handheld then, dry and old and still on his loading arch with his bad leg out in front of him, and he said, "I'll take the review. I'll sit in the chair and be civil and I'll not say the law was wrong. But you'll put one line in your report for me, Engineer. A city's wires and a city's people are not two different circuits. You can't survey one and leave the other dark. That's the whole of it."

The engineer looked at the handheld, and then at Sol. "That line's going in the report," he said.

Ife Aduba had not moved from the top of the ladder. She stood with the line coiled over her shoulder her jaw set and a thing working in her face, and at last she said, "The tram. The water's going down on the yard. I have been up all night and I am going to need a person who can read a motor."

"Read the room, not the page," Sol said, and Ife's mouth twisted, and it was not quite a smile. "The barge engine's full of the yard. I'll pull it with you."

"I'll come to the wall for you and I'll come to the depot. That's working. We'll talk terms in the boat."

"We'll talk terms in the boat," Sol said.

The rain had eased. It was not done with the city, but it had stopped coming in flat and was coming down soft out of a long grey that was going lighter above the hospital's roof, and the floodlights of the district were on, and along the far reach the market's lamps were coming on in ones and twos as the feeders found their feet again.

Sol got down the service stair with a hand on Ife's shoulder and got into the boat, and the engineer put the motor into gear and turned it in the courtyard water. Beside her on the thwart the bag lay where she had set it, and her hand found the strap by habit and did not find the wrench that should have been looped through it. She tilted her head back and listened for the far reach, and where the market's lamps came on she heard it, the soft knock of a skiff under a low bridge, and she thought of a man in a tobacco tin and a strip of canvas on the fat main, and of a promise she had made in a doorway of the drowned hall.

Above them the chain did not come apart. The old woman stayed with her lamp. The sister's boy put his canvas roll on the coping and sat down beside it. The woman in the window did not put down her light.

They went out of the courtyard and round the corner and up the flooded street toward the footbridge and the loading arch, and behind them the district held its light, and Sol sat in the bottom of the works boat with her hands in her coat and her bag against her knee and the sound of the city coming back up through the hull into her boot soles, street by street, note by note, the way the grid did when she put her hand on the stone.

It was not all of it. It would not be all of it for a long while.

But it was enough to hear.
