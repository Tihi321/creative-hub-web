The Third Breaker

The Drowned Tram Depot came at them through the dark in pieces. First the smell, wet iron and dead oil, then a long low shape with the shed's roof sagging at the middle, and then a sound.

Sol caught it before the water showed her anything. A hum, deep and half a note flat, the noise a fridge makes when it gives up.

"Under the shed," she said. "You hear that?"

Ife had a hand on the pole and a hand on the throttle and was doing neither one properly. The reach had opened into what used to be a tram yard, and the yard sat full. Half a dozen tram cars stood in the water to their windows, roofs like stepping stones that led nowhere.

"That's her," Ife said. "That's my engine sitting in the water, and I can see her, and I can't have her." She brought the barge in slow. "Sol. Look at the shed."

The shed's side gaped where the roof had come down. Inside, the water lifted and settled in slow swells, as if something under the floor drew breath.

"The whole yard's filling from under," Sol said. "A spring's broken through the floor and it's running in."

"Then we do your breaker quick."

"We do your breaker right." Sol already had the notebook out. "Shed or the office block at the end. We find it, we put it back, and then we do the fourth, and the depot's whole and it's all yours. All four, Ife. That was the bargain. Four breakers, then your motors and first claim and we go. Not before."

Ife's mouth went flat. "You think I've forgotten the terms. I named them. I'm not asking you to cut them loose. I'm asking you to make the water stop coming up over them while you hunt your switch. There's a difference, and I know the difference, and it's eating me." She nodded at the trams. "My mother's block is across the yard from that lot. If the yard's a lake by morning there'll be nothing left to claim."

"Then we're quick and right and this one goes first. And when all four are back, I'll stand in the water and help you cut them loose myself. You have my word."

"I'll hold you to the word." Ife brought the barge in. "And I'll be eyeing my motors the whole time."

The depot office stood at the end of the yard, a two-storey brick box with its door under water. Sol laid the barge alongside the wall, where the bottom steps of a fire escape sank into the water.

"Tie off and stay with her," she said.

"If you don't come up I'm coming in after you. Not for my motors. For you. You're my crew now, and that's a worse bargain than the one I named."

"You'll come up. I'll make a noise." Sol swung a leg over the rail. "If the engine goes while I'm under, don't follow it down. A hot engine in cold water cracks. She's been running rough and hot since the lane this afternoon, and old since the spring. She's not a young engine."

"She's an old diesel that's been on her last summer since the spring, and tonight she's decided the summer's over. I know what I've got under that hatch." Ife was already looking past her at the trams. "Go on. Move."

The water in the office reached Sol's chest and climbed a finger a minute. The breaker room had no windows, and its steel door had warped in the frame. She shoved at it a full minute before it gave.

Inside, the vault ran dry to the walls. Two pillars of switch-gear stood there, older than the grid's, smelling of dust and copper and nothing burnt. The vault had held. The labels were the trouble. On the left pillar, six grey plates, each with a wire running out into the wall behind, and each plate carrying a paper tag wired on in Dell's square hand.

Sol read the tags and her breath stopped in her throat.

The first read DEPOT IN, its wire going down under the floor. The second read DEPOT OUT, its wire running up and out over her head. Fine. But the third said DEPOT IN as well, in the same hand, and the fourth said something she could not read, and the fifth said OFF—DO NOT, and there the pencil had stopped, and the sixth had no tag at all.

She held the notebook up against the plates. Dell's drawing was a wheel with six spokes, and beside it: in at the top, out at the bottom, the middle four are the section links, mind the two that look alike, and then the words with a smudge over them where he had set his cup down.

The drawing did not match the room. In the drawing the in-line sat at the top. In the room it sat at the bottom, and where the drawing said it went, a plate sat with no tag at all, and the wire behind it hummed.

"How are you doing?" Ife's voice carried down the stairwell.

"Fine. Give me a minute."

"Water's up a step. Give me one minute."

Sol pressed her knuckles to the pillar and felt the hum come up through the leather and into the bones of her hand. The long note came in low, by the bottom plate, and pushed up the pillar and away. That was the depot's supply off the trunk. The plates with no tag and with the wrong tag were the section links, and one of them was carrying more than a section ever should.

She put her ear to each plate in turn. The one marked DEPOT IN, the second, rang high and thin and stopped fast, feeding the shed's dead lights. The one with no tag rang low and long with a bad harmonic, and the housing below it ran warm.

"That one. The third switch in. It's loose in its housing, and it didn't trip when the yard went under because the trip's downstream of the bad connection. It's holding off the trip and letting everything through."

"Then put it back."

"It's a make-and-break switch, Ife. It's on. I'm not putting it back, I'm putting it right. The housing's loose and I can bind it, but I can't work a switch that's on and hot."

"Then turn it off."

"It's a depot section. Off means the shed's pumps go off, and the water's coming up faster."

"Sol." Ife's voice had climbed out of its flat. "The water's over the outside step. Two minutes and it's in your stairwell."

Sol took the reading again, felt the pillar go warm and cool and warm under her knuckles: the long note low, the bad link warm, the two alike plates, the one with no tag. Dell's page was wrong and Dell's hand had been steady. He had drawn the wheel from the far side of the room, and here were six switches and five tags and one he had given up on.

"Write it down," she said to the air. "Write what you did when you couldn't read it." Then, "Ife. I'm going to turn the bad switch off and the in-line with it, strap the housing, and put them back in the right order. If it goes wrong, get up the top of the stairs and out the top window onto the shed roof. The shed roof is dry. That's your way out."

"That's a long way to go for a switch."

"It's the only way to go. Stand back."

She put her weight on the bad switch's lever and it came down, and the hum died and left a silence that rang. She bound the loose housing with the strap and the turnbuckle, and felt it go tight in her wrists the way the market's had. Then she brought the in-line down, and the bad switch back up, and the hum returned and it was even.

She felt for the plate's heat. Warm, cool, cooler, cool.

"Somebody write that down." The pencil was out before she thought, and she wrote in the margin of the tram page: turned off, bound, back on, cool. And under it, smaller: Dell's drawing is upside down. Read the room, not the page.

Then the dark came down.

No flicker. A flat black drop, and the torch still burned, and the torch was not what had gone dark.

"The shed's gone," Ife called. "The yard's gone dark."

"The in-line's holding. Somebody upstream's put a fault through." Above the tin roof of the reaches Sol heard it, the flat slam of an arc that had opened and stayed open. Under that, the small dying noise of the shed: the hum gone, the pumps gone, the water moving in under the floor with its slow breathing.

She came up into the barge's lantern with the toolbag over her head. Ife had the pole up, holding the barge off the wall while the water pushed her.

"Something upstream opened and took the depot with it. My switch is right and there's no power to it and the water's coming up."

"The shed's dark and my motors are in the dark." Ife's jaw was a knot. "Answer me what we do."

Sol was already on the shed's roof, where the collapse had left a flat ledge of tin. "You keep the barge off the wall. I listen for the depot trunk. If the trunk's alive the shed can have it back, and if the depot comes back the pumps come back."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then we've got a barge with an old engine and a city with a worse one, and we do the fourth in the dark." She got up on the tin and it rang under her boots like a drum. "If the shed stays dark I lose you a night's work, and I know what a night's work costs. All four breakers and then your claim. I'll make it right. But not now."

Ife gave her no answer. She put the pole down and pulled the throttle back, and the barge slid away from the wall and held in the middle of the yard, and Sol watched her do it with a face like a closed door.

Sol lay on the tin and put her ear to it. The trunk was in there, the long even note running under the floor to the far end, and it was alive. Between it and the shed, the arc above had taken the depot's feed the way a man leaning on a gate takes the whole fence down. And under the roof there was a smaller sound, a thin high ringing off the trams' wet shells, and it started from somewhere in the third tram, and Sol did not know what it was.

She got the radio up out of her coat and turned the knob.

"—still with us," Ozzy was saying. "Two breakers done. Two left. The river is over the hundred-year mark and the second high water is coming behind it. The depot quarter has gone dark and I have no word from it. If you are there, say a word into any radio you have. I will put it on."

Sol pressed the key. "Ozzy. This is Sol. Dell's apprentice. The depot's dark on a bad arc upstream and the shed pumps are down and the water's in my vault and the breaker's reset and it's not enough. I need the depot trunk back. Whoever's got a hand on a remote upstream, put it back now, and if you're a looter with a wrench, put it back now, and I'll not ask your name."

The radio was quiet a long moment, and then it said, "Held. She's held the breaker and she needs the feed. Hear her." And then, small, close: "Sol Vieira. Say another word when you've got it. I'll be listening."

The dark held.

Sol lay on the tin and the water spread black and busy under the trams, and Ife held her off the wall and never looked up, and nobody put the depot's trunk back.

She drew the six plates in the notebook as they actually stood, and wrote what she had done to each. Then she thought about what she had not written. The culvert in the vault's back floor took the shed's water out into the yard when the pumps ran; with the pumps dead it was only a hole. She had seen a grate in the yard's wall on the way in and thought nothing of it. She thought of it now.

She went to the edge of the tin and looked over. The yard's wall ran off to a low stone bridge where the old road crossed the culvert, and on the bridge a shape stood with a lantern at its feet and a line in both hands, a bucket coming up dripping.

"Bridge," Sol called. "You. What are you doing?"

"Bailing." A woman's voice, rough and plain. "The culvert's backing up and the yard's the low part and my sister's in the block the other side of it. If the yard goes she's got a wet floor and no way up." She set the bucket down. "And you?"

"Electrician. I reset the depot's breaker and it's dark anyway and the pumps are dead, and the water's coming up in the shed."

The woman picked up the lantern and came along the wall. An old woman in a waterproof cape and rubber boots, with a face like a woman who had decided something a long time ago. "Then you'll want the yard's water down before you want the shed's. If the yard comes down, the shed empties, and maybe your pumps can breathe." She set the bucket down. "I've a second line and bucket under the bridge. Fetch them yourself. Get off that roof before it puts you in the water."

Sol got down, and by the time she was aboard, the old woman had come round with a line in each hand and a bucket in each.

"Fill and haul and tip. There's a stone step by the bridge. Don't stand in the yard. The yard's got the culvert in it."

"I've got an engine and no time," Ife said, flat, but she was looking at the buckets.

"An engine that runs is a better pump than an engine that's hot and dead in a shed. Come on."

They bailed. Two lines over the side and two buckets coming up, Ife on one and Sol on the other and the old woman keeping the lantern and the count, and the yard went down an inch and then two, and the shed's floor showed black and wet and no worse. It took so long that Sol stopped feeling the rope in her hands and felt it only where the rope had been.

Then the light came on.

First a flicker at the far end of the reach, then a steady glow, then the hum came up through the barge's deck and into her teeth. The shed's lamps lit one and two and then the whole row, and the pumps started with a shudder and a scream of old bearings and settled into their work.

And the barge's engine coughed, and stopped.

Ife stood with her hand on the throttle and did not move.

"Intake." Sol was already at the hatch with the torch. "The wake put a wave over the gunwale and it went in at the air, and she's flooded." She got the hatch up and the smell came out, hot oil and river water. "But that's the half of it. She was running rough and hot before we ever came into this yard. She'd have shrugged off that wave in the spring. She didn't flood because tonight was bad. She flooded because she was finished, and tonight found the wave that would prove it."

"I know what she was." Ife did not raise her voice. "I heard her all afternoon, running hotter than she had any right to, and I told myself it was the water and the load and the dark." She came aft and stood over the hatch with her hands loose at her sides. "My engine's been on her last summer since the spring. She was gone the minute the wave came over. That's none of your doing and all of mine for asking her for one more night." She looked in at the dead iron. "Three hours of bailing and I can't even get the barge over to my own motors, and the motors are safe and lit and the barge is the thing that's broken."

The old woman had come to the bridge's edge. "You'll not get her going in the middle of the yard with no light on the water. Tie her to the bridge and I'll get my sister's boy down with his tools, and my sister's got a fire and a pot of something. She's in the block the other side of the bridge," she added, as if that were the whole of the answer.

"We can't stop," Sol said. "The fourth breaker's off the map and the second high water's coming."

"Then you can't stop with a dead engine either. Come alongside. It's twenty feet. You'll take a cup and a dry minute and go on. A dry minute is half a mile, out here."

Ife poled them in, and the barge came up against the bridge's wet stone with a bump and a long scrape, and the old woman had a line round a bollard before she could ask. Then there were footsteps on the bridge and a boy's voice and a torch, and a second woman calling, "Up here! I've got the kettle on! Bring them up!"

Sol sat down on the engine hatch hard, because her knees had decided things without her. The shed's lamps were on down the wall and the yard was going down, and the notebook was in her hand with the tram page on top, drawn right this time.

She thought of Dell on the roof, and the promise he had made her make, and the word waiting under a wet cup for however many years. She got the radio up and pressed the key.

"Ozzy. Sol. The depot's lit. The third breaker's done. Three done, one left." She looked at the old woman and the boy and the light on the bridge, and at Ife, who had come to the gunwale with her arms folded, listening. "And I'm calling in my promise to Dell, and I want you to hear it and put it on, because he made me promise to ask it on the air so you'd hear. Dell. The fourth breaker's off your map. You told me on the roof you were the reason. So I'm asking. Where is it, and what did you do?"

The handheld made a small paper sound, and then the voice that filled it was not Ozzy's. It was older, and it had a roof in it and rain and the sea.

"Sol Vieira." A breath. "I'm at the loading arch and my leg's gone out from under me and I'm not coming down, so hear it on the air the way I said. The fourth is in the pumping house under the Silt Market footbridge, the low arch where the old storm outfall comes in. It's off my map because I drew that map when I was twenty and I drew it from the wrong side, and I found out at forty and never fixed it, because a map you've drawn wrong once makes a man ashamed to look at it. There's a page in the back with a wheel on it. That's the pumping house and it's upside down, and the fourth is the section link between the outfall pumps and the hospital feeder. It's a make-and-break and it's had water on it since the first surge. Turn it off before you put your hands in. Off, Sol. Not like the depot. Off first, then bind it, then put it back. Say it back to me."

"Off first," Sol said, and the pencil was moving. "Then bind it. Then back."

"Then go, and go now, because the second high water's closer than Ozzy knows. That's my question answered, Ozzy Kwan. Put it on the air and let the city hear what an old man was too proud to draw."

Ozzy came back doing the broadcaster's rhythm, and under it he was not steady at all. "You heard him. Three done and one left, and it is the one that holds the hospital line, and the man who drew the map has told the city where it is. The Silt Market footbridge, the low arch, the outfall. Whoever is out there with a boat, hear that and go."

Sol closed the notebook and looked at Ife across the dead engine.

"The outfall's two reaches back the way we came. The engine won't turn and the barge is heavy. The barge is done and the fourth isn't, and I can't carry a boat on my back."

Ife looked at her a long moment, at the shed's lamps and her motors under them, and then at the bridge and the boy with his tools and the old woman with the lantern.

"Then we carry what we can," she said. "The boy's tools, my line, your torch and your book. And if the engine can be made to cough by morning it'll cough, and if it can't, my motors sit and wait for the water to go down like everything else in this city." She picked up the coil of line. "All four, you said. And you'll help me cut them loose after. I'll be standing in the water when you do."

"I know you will." Sol got up off the hatch. "Bridge, then. And the fourth breaker, and Dell's map read the right way up at last."
