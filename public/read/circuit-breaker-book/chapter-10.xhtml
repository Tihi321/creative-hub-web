Apprenticeship

The review sat at the training hall on the Saturday, three days after the surge. Long table, photographs of the old gasworks hanging crooked on the wall, the clock over the door ticking like a clock ticks when everybody in the room has decided to be fair and is working at it.

Sol had been in that room twice before. She knew the smell of it: floor polish, paper, the dust that comes off old photographs when the radiator heats up.

The board took the long table. The woman who had taught cable theory for thirty years still had chalk on her cuff. The man from the substation had a grey moustache and said nothing yet. The inspector from the licensing office opened his folder and announced that this was a review of a license and not a trial of a night. A fine distinction, Sol thought, drawn by a man who had spent the surge in his own bed.

Dell Marchetti sat at the end of the table with his bad leg out and both hands folded on his stick. Clean shirt. Shaved. Sol had never seen him look so much like a man who had decided to be judged, and would take it standing if the leg had allowed.

She sat behind him with her notebook on her knee. A notebook is a thing you hold when your hands want something to do.

"Mr. Marchetti," the inspector said. "The city has heard several numbers on the air, and I would like one of them to be true. How many switches are at issue?"

"Four breakers on the survey," Dell said. "Two off it. Six in all. The sixth is the section link on the district wall, and it is the one I am sorry for, and the four on the survey are the ones I'm not."

"And the two off it?"

"The island switch in the pumping house. I put it in when I was young and proud and did not want a thing that saved a block to sit in an office for a year. I knew it was wrong when I put my hand to it, and every year after. The wall link I put in when the survey went to the wrong address and the courtyard flooded and I could not wait for a man with a folder to find the right door." Dell's jaw worked. "I should have made the drawing. A wire off the drawing is a wire the next storm can catch a man on. The man from Kirk Row said that and he was right, and I've said so to him at his own door."

The inspector wrote it down. "The code is not sentimental about that. A licensed man who installs unrecorded apparatus has broken the terms of his license. He can be fined, and in a serious case he can lose the license and the pension it carries." He looked up. "I have read the file, the engineer's letter, a complaint from Kirk Row and a list from a woman at the depot. And a report from an apprentice. Miss Vieira. The board will hear you now."

Sol stood.

She had expected to be frightened. She had been frightened on the roof, in the black vault, on the ladder down the district wall, and she had expected the room to be worse than all three. But the room was dry and Dell was in the chair, and this was a job. You did a job by the sound of it and the feel of it, and by telling the truth about what you found.

"I'm Sol Vieira," she said. "I've been Dell Marchetti's apprentice since I was fifteen. I've written a word and I'm going to read it, because I want it exact."

She opened the notebook.

"In the surge I put my hand to six boxes. The waterworks breaker, when the vault was flooded and the fault was cooking the water and I swam under it. The market hub, when the lug had been cut away and I rebuilt it with a busbar and a turnbuckle. The depot's third breaker, when the tags were wrong and the drawing was upside down and I wrote the correction in my own hand in this book. The pumping house island switch, which is off the drawing and hot and ticking, and I turned it off and bound it and put it back. The section link on the district wall, which is off the drawing, and which I put in with a chain of neighbours passing tools down a ladder in the dark, and its second cartridge in the tin the old woman held the lid over so it stayed dry. And the depot yard, where I found the third switch and set the in-line and the switch back in the right order. That's the same box twice, but I'll count it once and be honest about the counting." She turned the page. "Six boxes. Every one is written in this book with the time and the fault and the fix and my name against it. If there's a seventh in this city it's not mine, and I'll find it before the next storm and write it down."

"Your mentor's note in chapter four was smudged and unreadable," the inspector said. "You wrote your own. The board would like to know why you did not do as the note said."

"Because the note was wrong," Sol said. "It was a man's memory of a room as he drew it when he was twenty, and he got it upside down and never went back. Dell drew carefully all his life, and a man who draws carefully can still draw a room from the wrong side, and then the drawing lies to the next person who reads it. The next person was me. I read the room instead. That's not a fault in the man's care. It's a fault in the paper, and the paper wants correcting, and I'm the one holding the pencil."

"And the switches off the paper," the inspector said. "You have heard the code's opinion. What is yours?"

"My opinion is that a city's wires and a city's people are not two different circuits. You can't survey one and leave the other dark. That's Dell's word, and it's mine now, because I've been in the water and I know what the water was doing. It was at the hospital transformer. The four breakers on the survey would not have held it alone, because the survey did not know about the flood pumps and it did not know about the courtyard and it did not know about the wall." She looked at the inspector. "But a wire off the paper is still a wire that can catch the next man, and the man from Kirk Row is right, and both those things are true at once. A board that can only hold one of them at a time is a board that will get a district killed. That's my word."

The room went quiet except for the clock.

"The board will deliberate," the inspector said. "The record will show the apprentice spoke without notes."

"I had notes," Sol said. "That's a notebook. It's a different thing."

The woman with the chalk on her cuff put her hand over her mouth, and it was not the clock that made the sound in the room after that.

They went out into the yard to wait. Ife Aduba was sitting on the low stone with her boots off and her feet in the sun.

"Your boots are off," Sol said.

"My boots are wet and it's Saturday," Ife said. "Sit down. You look like a person who's been read at."

Sol sat down beside her and put her notebook on her knee.

"I said the thing about the paper," she said. "The inspector didn't like it and he didn't dislike it. He wrote it down."

"The inspector's a fair man and a slow one. He'll take the truth home in his folder and think about it at supper, and nobody will ever know what he thought." Ife squinted at the sun. "How's the old man?"

"Quiet. He's been taking the code like a man taking medicine and not making a face."

"Then he'll be all right." Ife picked at a callus on her palm. "The gearbox note was the bearings. The man with the wrench found a set at the tram depot in the second car, and the tram's mine so it's legal, and he fitted them this morning and shimmed them by feel, and the motor's dry and the sump's filled. If the starter takes it she'll run before dark."

"She'll take it," Sol said.

"You don't know that."

"I know the sound of the gearbox now, and I know the man with the wrench fitted the bearings and shimmed them by feel, and a bearing fitted by a man who's done it forty years will hold. I'll be there when you put the starter to it. If it doesn't take, we'll find it together."

Ife was quiet a while. Then she said, without looking at Sol, "The barge is my mother's block's barge and it's going to be a barge again by tonight. I've got the tram's salvage and the first claim and the man with the wrench and a claim in writing with a foreman's signature on it. What I haven't got is a person who can hear a bearing shim before it's shimmed."

Sol turned her head.

"I'm not asking for charity," Ife said, fast and clipped. "I'm asking for terms. A partnership. The barge is the barge and it stays my family's. But there's work on this city's water that wants two people. One can pilot a barge and haggle a foreman, and the other can lay her ear to a casing and tell you what it's thinking, and I'm not too proud to say it's worth something. Terms in writing. Winter work and summer work. You come when the water's up and I'll come when it's dry." She put her hand out, flat, the way she had at the loading arch. "And the hall grievance is behind us and I'll say so when the hall sits. What do you say?"

Sol thought about the lock keeper's lane and the chain under the bridge, and the barge coming out of the dark into the market's lamplight, and the forty feet of line going up to a roof. She put her own hand in Ife's.

"Partnership," she said. "In writing. And if you shout at me when I'm right about the gearbox I'll shout back, because a partnership that only goes one way is a partnership with a dodgy bearing in it."

"Shout all you like," Ife said. "Just be right."

The board came back at two. The inspector read out the sentence in the flat voice of a man reading his own writing.

"The board finds that Mr. Marchetti installed two items of electrical apparatus off the survey and off the record, in breach of the terms of his license. This is a serious breach and the board does not make light of it. He is reprimanded in the strongest terms the code permits, and the reprimand is entered against his license. The unrecorded apparatus will be surveyed, drawn and recorded at the city's expense, and Mr. Marchetti will not do the drawing, because a man does not draw his own fault. The apprentice Vieira will do the drawing under supervision, because her notebook shows she can. That is the board's sentence on the paper."

The room waited.

"The board has also considered the terms of his license as they apply to an electrician past the age of retirement, and finds that a reprimand is a reprimand and not a revocation. The license stands. The pension the license carries stands. The man's word at the review was true, his drawings before the fault were careful, and the board has read the engineer's letter and the complaints and the report of the doctor from the ridge, and it finds that this city's lights were held by his care and not in spite of it. That is the board's sentence on the man."

Dell did not move for a moment. Then he put his hand up to his face and took it down again and it was steady. "Thank you," he said, thin. "I'd like it minuted that I'm sorry for the paper and not for the switch, and I'll draw nothing that saves a block and hides in a wall again."

"It's minuted," the inspector said, and wrote it.

The papers were signed on the air the next morning, because Ozzy Kwan had asked for it, and because a city that had heard the whole night had a right to hear the end of it. He set up a little table in the loading arch at dawn with a lamp on it, and the lamp worked, because the district was lit.

Dell sat at the table with the papers in front of him and a pen in his hand, and Sol stood beside him with her cap in her hands.

"Port Alaric," Ozzy said, warm and rapid and a little ragged now, "it's Sunday morning and the sun's coming up on the arch and this is the last thing this station will do before I go and sleep for a week. On the table is an indenture and a pen and a retired man who was reprimanded yesterday and kept his license, and beside him an apprentice who was in six boxes in one night. Mr. Marchetti. Say your word."

Dell picked up the pen and looked at Sol.

"Her father wired his first lamp with me," he said. "He was a better hand than me and he's been gone eleven years. When the surge took the control room I sent her out with my book and my bag, because a man in a chair is no use in a flood and a girl who can read a wall by the sound of it is the whole of the use there is. She read the waterworks with her hands under a live fault and she rebuilt a hub the looters had cut and she put the depot's third breaker in when my drawing lied to her and wrote the correction herself. She went down a wall in the rain and put a switch in that was my fault, and she stood in a room yesterday and told a licensing board that a city's wires and a city's people are not two different circuits." He looked at the paper. "I've had a hand in this trade forty-one years. This is the best hand I ever put to it. I sign her out today as a full electrician. And I'll say the other thing the city's owed since the night of the surge. I put two switches in a wall and I did not draw them, and I was ashamed and stayed ashamed and it got worse for the staying. I've been reprimanded and I'll take the reprimand and the survey and the drawing the girl will do, and I'm sorry for the paper. That's my word and the whole of it, and I'll not say it again."

He signed. The pen scratched. He pushed the paper across to Sol.

"Sign your own name," he said. "Nobody signs it for you."

Sol took the pen. It was a good heavy old pen and it did not shake once she had it in her hand. She wrote her name where the paper wanted it, and her address, and the date, and by the third line her hands had stopped being anything but useful.

"Read it out, girl," Dell said. "The city wants to hear what it says."

"No," Sol said. "The city's heard enough of me on the air. The city wants to hear it run." She stood back from the table. "Ozzy. Aim the microphone at the wall."

Ozzy aimed it. Sol laid her palm flat on the loading arch's stone where the cable came through and listened, and the radio carried the sound across the delta and into the flats on Kirk Row and the wards and the market and the culvert bridge and the depot yard: a low hum, steady and deep, the note a big transformer makes when the load is true, and behind it the smaller notes, a motor, a fan, a lift, the choir of a district that had been at the bottom of the water three days before.

"That's the wall," Sol said. "That's the district feeder carrying load. That's the hospital and the lifts and the blood fridge and Barrow Row and the market's lower lamps. It's been through the flood and it's dry and it's true. That's my paper signed and that's the sound of the city it's signed to."

Ozzy let it run ten seconds. Then he said, "That's a good sound to end on," and his voice had gone soft in a way the broadcaster's rhythm did not allow.

Ozzy signed off at six, and it was the shortest thing he did all week.

"Port Alaric. The surge is down and the streets are drying and the hospital district is lit. There are streets that will stay dark tonight and tomorrow and I've read you the list and I'll read it every evening until there's no list. There's a review that sat yesterday and a man who took his medicine and a partnership at the depot yard and an old woman with a complaint on file and a man on Kirk Row with a socket to make safe, and every one of those is a thing this city did with its own hands in one night. This station is going to keep saying both truths as long as it has a mast, and it has a mast, because there's a man with a coil of clean wire and a boy with a tool roll who've promised to keep it up. If you hear nothing from us for a day it's because I'm asleep. If you hear nothing for two, come and knock." The hiss came up and died. "This is Ozzy Kwan, and this is Port Alaric coming back up."

The carrier clicked off, and the mist was gone, and the sun stood over the water in a blaze of orange on the wet stone.

Dell got up and took his stick. "Come on," he said. "The gearbox is dry and the day's not done."

The barge ran before dark.

They put the starter to it in the depot yard with a crowd on the wall, and the engine turned slow and wet and coughed and caught, and Ife stood at the tiller with her hand on the throttle and her jaw set, and the note of the exhaust came up out of the dead iron clean and true.

She let it run a minute and pulled the throttle back and cut it and stood with her hand still on the tiller.

"It's got a bearing in it from the second tram car," she said to the yard, because the yard was listening. "It's got a coil of clean wire from the man with the wrench and a shim from the shop and oil from the depot's store. It's not a new engine. It's the same old engine with a new heart out of the same scraps, and it'll run." She looked at Sol. "Terms are in writing. It's a partnership."

The yard made a sound that was not applause and was better.

Sol walked home at dusk through drying streets with her boots wet and her notebook in her coat, and the city was lit and dark in patches and she heard every patch.

She went down the lock keeper's lane, past the stoop at number eleven, where the dead service pipe and the dead cable were still dead and somebody had chalked a note on the door to say the water was down here, and she marked it in her head for the survey. She went over the bridge with the chain, and the chain hung in the low water and rang when the current took it, and it was only a chain now and not a channel. She went into the market, and the market was dark in the upper streets and lit in the lower ones, and the old man was in the lower ones with a lamp and the wrench in his good hand, and he held it out, and she gave it back.

"It's yours," she said. "I said I'd come down for it and I've come down for it, and I'm giving it to the man it belongs to. That's a different thing and you know it."

"It's a different thing," the old man said, and put the wrench in his coat, and his face did a thing she had not seen it do before, which was to be pleased.

She went up the reach past the guttering and saw the torch on the fish-gutter feeder burning steady and safe. She stood on the district wall where the chain had come down, and there was no chain now, and the courtyard below was dry and the transformer hummed the note it had hummed all night without one tick in it. She went down into the flats on Kirk Row and knocked, and the man came out with his mother in the chair behind him and the socket made safe with a new cover and a test tag, and Sol put her hand on the wall by it and listened and it was true, and she said so. The man said, "That's the girl that went down the wall," and his mother said, "Ask her in for her tea," and Sol said she'd come back for it when the survey was done, and she meant it.

She went up Canal Street to the grid control room, and it was still full of water, and it would be drained and dried and rebuilt and she would be on it. She stood on the roof and looked down at the city, and the city was lit and the city was dark and both were true and it was enough.

She got out her notebook and wrote the last line of the night the way she had written every other, the time and the fault and the fix and her name against it, and it said:

Sunday evening. Six boxes, all holding. Survey Monday. Draw the wall, draw the pumping house, draw the lock keeper's lane, the lot of it, where the next man can read it. The partnership's in writing. The license stands and the pension stands and the man's sorry for the paper. The city's up. Not everywhere. Enough.

She shut the notebook and put it in her coat and stood a moment with her cap off and the wind coming up the delta, and under her boots through the stone and the iron the whole city hummed, true and steady and full of load, and it was the sound of a circuit that a great many somebodies had closed by hand in one night, and she was one of them, and she had her papers signed.
