Shifting Ground

She started with the water.

Not the water in the third bay, spiralling down its throat into the dark under the slab. The water in the runnels, the water that had found every low corner of the basement floor and pooled there in sheets the length of a man, the shallow tidal puddles that had been there when they came down and were deeper now by the width of a boot-sole. She had read them on the way in as a single level, one datum, the flood surface sitting flat across a flat floor. That had been wrong in a way she should have caught at the door, because the floor was not flat. The floor was a lid over voids, tilting east and south on the soft silt, and a flat water level on a tilting lid does not read as a line across the room. It reads as a gradient.

She took the torch off Kestrel — he let her take it, which told her how much his wrist still remembered the floor moving — and she laid the beam along the floor at ankle height and walked it slowly left to right across the room, and she watched what it did. It did not stay level with the silt. It crossed the first runnel and the runnel was a finger deep. It crossed the slab panel between the first and second columns and the water there stood a knuckle deep against the base of the column, pencilled around the cast-iron flange with a dark line, and the dark line went all the way round the flange without a break, which meant the water had stood at that height long enough to stain the iron, which meant it stood there often. It crossed to the sagging south beam row and the water had climbed the wall to the second course of brick and left its own tide mark, fine as a ruled line, two inches above where it had stood when Bram went through the dry store door.

The middle tower ticked. One. Two. She did not look up. She had made the tick a fact with a rate two hours ago and the rate had not changed, and a clock that does not change its beat is a clock that is still keeping time, and she had a better clock now anyway: the water was climbing the wall in front of her, and it was climbing it in the same rhythm the tide was climbing the revetment out on the river, and the two were the same water. There was a comfort in that she let herself take and did not examine, because a clock that agrees with a clock tells you your instruments are working, and she had not trusted her instruments in eleven years.

Then she saw the second mark. It was below the fresh one, a fainter line, dry now, a hand's width lower on the brick. And below that, hardly there at all, a third. Three lines on the south wall a hand's width apart, like the rings of a tree, like the marks a parent cuts in a doorframe for a growing child, and between the third line and the silt below there would be a fourth and a fifth if the silt would let her see them, and the whole wall was a ledger of every tide the foundry had ever let indoors.

"It's been up and down," she said. "Twice a day. This wall's been breathing."

"What?" Bram said.

"The water. It's not rising. It's already been up to here —" she put the torch on the dry line, "— and here, and it's coming back up now for the second high tide since we arrived, which means it's the tide, not a leak, not the river over the bank. The ground under this slab is a sponge, and the tide is the hand that squeezes it."

She straightened, and she made herself stand still for a second with the reading, because the reading was the thing she had come down here to do and had been doing badly, and she wanted it to land properly before she used it. Three lines. Water in, water out, water in. The slab had not sagged because the ground had failed once. The slab had sagged because the ground worked it like a bellows, and the voids under it had been washed and re-washed every tide since 1961, and the only reason there was still a floor at all was that a floor is patient and a slab is strong in the way that only a slab can be, distributing its own weight honestly and taking a long time to lose.

Kestrel was watching her read the wall the way she had watched him price the flywheel. "So the floor's a raft," he said, slowly, "and the raft's sat on a sponge, and the sponge goes up and down twice a day." He was not asking. He was reading it back to her to check it. "That's why the hatch was wet when I came through it. That's why the silt on my boots is wetter than yours. I come in at near-high water."

"Near-high," she said. "Which means you came through the one window of the day when the ground's at its firmest and the hatch is at its wettest, and walked into the middle of a room that's hardest to stand on right now. You got lucky and you don't know which way you got lucky."

"So it weren't a trap."

She stopped. That was the thing, said aloud, in a scrap man's voice, and it came round to her from an angle she had not expected, and it was right, and it landed the way the right thing always landed on her: not as a revelation, as a correction. A trap is something that waits for you. A trap has intent. She had come down here reading the foundry as a building that was failing on her, section by section, and she had read every wrong step as a wrong step, a thing she had misjudged, a mark against her reading. She had stood at the foot of the stair and told Bram the building was not on their side. She had said it as if a building could take sides, as if the thing that had stood here for a hundred and thirty-one years had turned its attention to her personally and decided.

It was not on anyone's side. It was not waiting. It was moving — was moving the way a river moves, by rule, by timetable, by the weight of water entering and leaving the silt beneath it twice a day, and every "trap" was only the load path of that second shifting under the first. The slab was not sagging. The slab was being held up by water one hour and dropped by water the next. The columns were not falling. The columns were walking, a millimetre at a time, on a raft that breathed. The arch over the third bay had not been waiting to take them. It had failed because the ground beneath its abutment had gone soft at low water the previous evening, and gone hard again with the flood, and gone soft again this morning, and its own weight had finally done the arithmetic on itself. It would have come down if no one had ever walked through this door. It would have come down for the rats.

Two years of a career, she thought. Two years of standing outside buildings and telling people the wrong story about them. Not the wrong danger. The wrong kind of danger. Every report she had signed since Rotherhithe had been honest and careful and about a body that fails once, and the buildings she had signed them about had all been systems that moved, and she had known that, and she had not let herself know it, because a system that moves on a rule is a system you can read, and she had not been ready to read again.

"I misread it," she said, and Kestrel's face did something behind the torchlight, and Bram's face did something else, and she held a hand up before either of them could say a word of it. "Not the danger. The danger is real, the danger is the whole of this room. I misread the kind of thing it is. I've been reading it like a body, like something that fails once. It doesn't fail once. It fails every twelve hours and un-fails for six of them, and it's been doing it for a hundred and thirty-one years, and it's still standing, which means the system they built —" she swept the column bases with the torch, the little bright rings of corrosion, the silt packed hard under the flanges where the water had left its load of mud — "is still working. Badly. But working. The building isn't dying. It's migrating."

The word came out of her and she heard it and did not take it back. Migrating. Not failing. It had been migrating for sixty years, a foot at a time, down toward the river, going where the silt went, and the men who wrote the 1961 survey had called it a defect and the city had called it a hazard and she had called it a failure, and none of them had been exactly wrong and all of them had missed the thing that mattered, which was that a structure that migrates does it in a direction, and a direction can be read.

Bram had come off the door and was standing where he could see the wall marks. He had his satchel against his chest and one hand flat on the strap. "You told me at the car," he said, quietly, "that a building tells you what it wants to do, and you read it, and you don't argue with it."

"I did say that."

"So it wants to go in the river."

"It's going in the river either way," she said. "The question is how fast and whether we're in it." She looked at him, at the way he was standing, and saw that he was not frightened of the water and never had been, and saw what he was frightened of, and answered the thing he had not asked. "The papers come with us. All of them. I'm not leaving the chest."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he did something she had not seen him do, and turned and looked at the wall marks himself — put the flat of his hand near the second line without touching it, the archivist's gesture, the gesture you make at something you intend to catalogue — and he said, "Two today. This cycle and the last."

"Two today. The 1953 flood mark upstairs is two foot one. That's higher than any of these."

"Nineteen fifty-three came over the top of the revetment and straight in. These come up through the ground. Different pipe, different man's problem." She picked up the rope.

She looked up at the crack in the middle tower, up through the ceiling grid, into the dark where the tick was coming from.

And the crack had stopped ticking.

She heard herself not hear it. One. Two. Nothing. Nothing. The space where the next tick should have been went on and on and filled up with the sound of water, and she stood there with the torch on the ceiling and every part of her that had learned to read a building said the same word at the same moment: settled. The tie had found its length. The tower had leaned as far as the ground under it would let it lean tonight, and the ground was as soft now as it was going to get before the ebb, and the tower had stopped moving because the ground had stopped moving.

Which meant the ground was about to start moving the other way. The flood was turning.

She stood with her neck bent and her eyes on the dark where the tick had been, and felt the calm go out of her chest the way air goes out of a tyre. A clock that stops is worse than a clock that hurries. She had been counting on the tick. She had been holding the tick in her hand like a stopwatch, and the tick had quit without warning and given her no reading at all, and the only reason a beat stops in a structure is that the thing beating has found its seat — for now, for this tide, for tonight, and not for the tide after.

"We have one hour," she said. "Maybe less. Not till the water reaches us — the water's coming up regardless and it can come up to my knee before it's a real problem, the hatch is a metre square and three men can't swim a satchel through it on a falling floor. But the ground is one hour from going soft again, and when it goes soft again everything that's moved today moves again. I do not want to be in this basement when the ground lets go the second time."

"That's a guess," Kestrel said.

"That's a reading, and it's the worst reading I've had all day, because it's a reading of nothing." She looked at him, at the gauntlets, at the wet silt on his boots. "I got down here by reading things. I just read a thing that stopped happening. You want to stand here and price flywheels while it starts happening the other way, that's your trade and I won't stop you, but the water's about to be at your boots and I'd rather we did this standing up."

"There's no way out but the hatch," Kestrel said. "The stair's a write-off. The door's above it."

"The stair's a write-off for a man who reads it like a stair. But it doesn't have to be read as the way out." She turned and put the torch on the dry store door, on Bram's hand still flat on the frame. "It has to be read as the dry store's support."

Bram looked at his own hand. He did not move it.

"Put the torch on the wall above the door," she said. "The one he's holding up."

Kestrel swung the beam. The dry store was set into the corner where the south wall met the cross-wall under the middle tower, and the wall above its door ran up into the arch of the ground floor, and the wall was leaning — had been leaning for sixty years, the same two-degree lean as the tower above it, and Nadia had called it an unintended load path in the first hour and had been right, but "unintended" was a word about the men who built it, not about the wall. The wall had taken a load and it had not failed. That was the thing she had looked at and not seen. She had looked at it and read danger. She should have read it and read capacity. She had spent the whole morning putting every member of the foundry on trial and convicting the lot of them, and the one that had been holding the crown of the tower off the archive door for sixty years was the one she had called the culprit.

"South wall's carrying the tower, and the tower's sitting quiet because the ground's hard right now," she said. "The wall's got more in it than the drawings ever gave it. I read it as a fault and it's been a support since before any of us were born. Kestrel — your flywheel. The one-and-a-half tonne, by the engine."

"What about it."

"How fast can you get it off its bed."

He looked at her for a count of three — she watched the arithmetic go through him, tonne against hour against tide — and then he was moving, because whatever else he was, the man read a load when someone drew him a picture of one. "Ten minutes with the sledges and the pins out. But I'm not stripping a machine for a —"

"You're not stripping a machine for a building. You're stripping a machine to keep the ground under your own boots from going soft with the weight still on it. Same job you came to do and the same tonne you came to sell. You can sell it off the roof of the flatbed tomorrow if we're still here tomorrow."

That put him in motion. He took the gauntlets off his belt and went down the beam row toward the boiler house with the torch swinging off his shoulder, and the light went long down the room, and the machines came out of the dark one at a time, sunk to their axles, wet and black, waiting — the line-shaft on its hangers, the travelling crane on its rail, the engine with its cylinder still lagged in crumbled magnesia, and the flywheel standing out of the silt like a moon. He put one gauntlet on the rim of it and turned back to her.

"Which way does it pull," he said.

"Across the beam. Toward the cinder bed. I'll mark it on the floor with the rope."

"And who holds the other end while I get it up there."

"Nobody. You free it and you leave it on its bed. It's a counterweight on the shaft, not a tow."

He grunted and went to work. Bram took his hand off the dry store door and straightened.

"What are we doing," he said.

"We're going to move the centre of gravity." She was already reading the route as she said it, as she had been reading it since the tick stopped: the beams, the flanges, the span of the rope, the corner where the arch had gone down and left a gap she had counted as a wall. "The ground under this corner is silt. Soft on the flood, hard on the ebb. The east corner — under the boiler house — is the old ash pit and the cinder bed, that's compact, that's been compact for a century, that's where the foundry put its heaviest machinery because the heaviest machinery can stand on it. The wall above that door is holding a tower and it has one counterweight's worth of spare capacity left before the silt under it lets go. A tonne and a half sitting on the cinder bed, pulling against the direction of the lean, buys back exactly that."

"You're shoring the building with its own engine."

"I'm reading the building and using what it already told me." She took the end of the rope off the middle beam and paid it out through her hands. "It's soil and water and load and it's doing it every twelve hours whether we want it or not, and I've stopped reading it as a thing that's trying to kill us. It isn't. It's just a system, and I finally read it right, and the right read says there's a way through the south bays that is not the way we came down."

Bram looked at the dry store door. The plan chest was behind it, the 1944 drawing in the chest, the only copy, the proof. And behind the dry store, in the corner the wall was holding up, was the room Nadia had read on her first pass as the one room in the foundry nobody could stand up in. The one she had ruled survivable by no weight, hers or anyone's.

"The drawing's in the safest room in the building," she said, and she heard the phrase come out of her mouth whole, and she heard what it was, and she did not soften it. "I told you a hundred times today I wouldn't take you in there. I was reading the lean and the wall and the water and adding them up and getting danger. I should have been reading what they do across the tide. That room is in the old core, Bram. It's got the arch above it and the south wall carrying it and the dry ground under the cinder bed just beyond it, and the wall we've both been calling the fault is the thing that's been keeping the room dry since 1944."

He didn't move for a moment. Then he picked up the satchel off the floor where it had been set down during the argument with Kestrel, and slung it back across his chest, and tightened the strap with both hands — a gesture she had now watched him do three times, and understood every time, and understood fully only now: a man getting a loan ready to be given back.

Kestrel's sledge rang off a pin, once, twice, a sound with the whole foundry in it, and the ring came back off the water and off the ceiling grid, and the machines stood out of the silt, and the tide crept up the south wall, and Nadia coiled the rope and started reading the way out.
