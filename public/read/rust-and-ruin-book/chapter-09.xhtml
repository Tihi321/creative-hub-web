Twelve Minutes

The decision came to her as a reading, not as a thought.

Nadia was still crouched at the jack with her palms on the screw, and the water was over the boards by a hand, and she was watching the joint in the south wall the way a hawk watches a field, and that was when she felt the second change come up through the boards and into her knees. It was not a report this time. It was a slow lean, a settle, the whole north half of the room rotating on the rail the way a loaded table rotates when one leg gives by a quarter inch, and the rotation had a direction to it and the direction was west, toward the shaft, toward the wall she had spent the whole night jacking back into line.

The line shaft answered it.

She heard the bell-note go up the hundred feet of iron in the wall and she heard the second flywheel take up the slack, and this time the flywheel did not wake and hold. This time the flywheel moved, and the movement came back down the line shaft and into the room as a low sustained groan of turning metal, and every head in the dry store lifted at once, and Kestrel said, very quietly, "That's the shaft."

"That's the counterweight losing its footing," Nadia said.

She was on her feet before she finished the sentence. She did not explain it. She had explained enough for one night, and the explaining was for men who still had time, and the time was gone. What she knew, in the half-second it took her to cross the room, was this: the north column had settled the half inch she had predicted and the rail had caught the floor, but catching the floor had thrown the load out to the walls on both sides, and the west wall was the wall the shaft ran through, and the counterweight was holding a wall that was now being asked to hold the north half of the room as well, and a lever balanced against two loads is a lever balanced against neither.

The jack's screw had backed an eighth of a turn. She saw it without putting a hand on it. The joint in the south wall had opened a hair, then a hair more, and the dust coming off it was a fine grey line in the torchlight, and a wall that sheds dust is a wall that is moving, and a wall that is moving on a soft foot at high tide is a wall with a schedule, and the schedule was measured in minutes.

"Kestrel. Bram. The service tunnel."

"The hatch is welded," Kestrel said.

"The hatch is welded and the hatch is where you came in and the water's through it to the second course," Nadia said. "There's a service tunnel. The 1961 survey drew it. It runs from the cinder bed under the boiler house east, under the middle tower's foot, and it comes up in the yard on the far side of the revetment, and it's dry above the string course and wet below it and we are going to walk the string course. Bram. The plan chest drawers. Leave them. Take the sheet."

He did not argue. He had stopped arguing three hours ago and she kept finding new reasons to be glad of it. He came off the chests with the 1944 sheet folded once along its own crease and pushed flat inside his coat against his chest, and he put his hand under Kestrel's arm, and Kestrel stood, and then the wall did something none of them had heard yet.

It did not groan. It clicked. Three quick clicks, evenly spaced, from the brick courses over the dry store door, the way a rail clicks when it cools, and Nadia read them for exactly what they were: the first bricks of the south wall's heel giving up their bearing one course at a time, top down, the way a wall always fails when it is failing on a soft foot, one brick at a time, politely, until it is not polite at all.

"Go," she said.

They went.

The gap in the dwarf wall where the cinder bed met the boiler house foundation was low and tight and full of standing water to the second brick, and Nadia went through it first on her hands and knees with the torch in her teeth, and behind her she heard Bram bringing Kestrel through the same gap with a hand flat on the back of his belt, and behind them both the dry store made a sound she had never heard a room make in her life: a long slow exhalation, as if the whole thing had decided at last to stop holding its breath.

The tunnel was real. That was the first thing and she let herself have it for one step and no more. It ran east and slightly down, brick-lined, three feet wide and four feet high, half full of water that moved against her hands with the slow push of the tide, and the string course of dressed stone ran along the eastern wall about ten inches above the waterline, and the waterline itself was climbing the string course in front of her eyes at a rate she could only compare to a clock, because that was all it was, a clock, a wet clock going up a wall.

The clicks followed them in.

She counted them without deciding to. Three, then two, then two, then three again, and the intervals between the sets were getting shorter and the sound was coming from behind and above and from the east wall of the tunnel itself, where the middle tower's foot sat directly over the brick arch of the tunnel's ceiling, and a tower foot sitting on a tunnel arch during a running tide was a load path the 1961 survey had drawn in a dotted line and a note, and the note had read, on no authority Nadia could now remember, that the arch was sound.

It was sound. It held like a bridge does, right up until it doesn't.

She counted the clicks and the clicks became a rate and the rate became a number, and the number sat in her chest like a swallowed coin. Eleven minutes, maybe twelve, before the middle tower's foot took the tunnel arch down on top of them. She said it over to herself and made herself doubt it, because a number you trust too easily is a number that has been reading your hopes instead of the wall. The clicks could be a brick spalling in a cold joint. The rate could be her own pulse in her ears. The arch could hold twice as long. But the water in the tunnel was now connected directly to the tide past the failed store, and the tide was rising, and the two clocks were not the same clock, and when she set the worst reading against the best reading and took the worse of the two, as she had been taught and had once forgotten, the number came out the same both ways.

"Faster," she said, and her voice came back at her off the brick, flat and thin, and she hated the sound of it, and she had never once in eleven years said a word she could not defend, and she said it again, "Faster," because it was true.

They could not go faster. That was the thing. Kestrel was on his feet but only on his feet, and Bram had Kestrel's left arm over his own shoulders and Kestrel's left boot was dragging in the water, and when Nadia stopped and turned the torch on him she read the leg by the way he was carrying it, not by any blood, and what she read was a joint she knew the name of and would not say out loud. It had happened when the store exhaled, behind them, in that long moment the room had taken to fall — the slide of a joist end off the rail and into the gap, the drop of the jack's base plate as the cinder bed went, the roll of the brass in the tool roll when the bench went over. It had been one of those, and she had not seen which one and never would, and it did not matter which, because a bone is not a load path and cannot be read and has to simply be dragged.

"Give him to me," she said.

Bram did not give him to her. Bram shifted Kestrel's arm off his shoulders and got his own shoulder under Kestrel's hip and took the man's weight crossways the way a man is carried when there is no rope and no litter and the ceiling is four feet high, and Nadia understood in one blind second that Bram had carried living weight before, and she filed it, and she went ahead with the torch.

The tunnel bent. The 1961 survey had it bending a shade south of east around the boiler house foot, and the survey was right, and the bend was where the water was deepest because a bend in a drain is a place where silt gathers and a bend in a service tunnel is a place where the tide comes up fastest, and she went through it with the water at her chest and the torch held up out of it and the string course sunk below the surface entirely, and she felt rather than saw the line of the old wall pass over her head. To her left the water lay deep and still and brown; to her right the brick came down close, and she kept her shoulder against it, because the brick on the right was the boiler house foot, and the boiler house foot went down to the cinder bed, and the cinder bed was the one thing in this building the tide did not soften.

Behind her she heard Bram breathing, one long breath for every two steps, and Kestrel not making any sound at all, which she did not like and could not afford to spend a thought on.

At the bend the arch over them clicked twice in quick succession and dropped a fistful of mortar into the water beside her ear.

She found the shaft by touch. The survey had put a rise — an old ventilation shaft, brick-lined, nine feet, rising from the tunnel's ceiling to a cast-iron grate in the yard — and the drawing had marked it with a small circle and the word vent, and Nadia put her hand up out of the water into the dark of the rise and found the lowest of the iron rungs set in the brick, and found the second above it, and the third, and turned and put the torch up the shaft and saw the grate and saw, beyond the grate, the grey-white of the sky over the yard.

It was twelve feet up the rise to the grate, and the grate had been laid in 1891, and a cast grate four feet by two, laid in a running tide zone, is a very great deal heavier than it looks.

"Bram," she said. "Kestrel at the foot of the rise. There's a ladder."

Bram brought him. There was no other way to put it. Bram brought him through the water to the foot of the rise and stood him against the brick, and Kestrel's head went back against the wall and he looked up the shaft, and his face, in the torchlight, was exactly the same face he had worn all night at his weighbridge, transactional, flat, a man pricing a load, and he looked at the twelve feet of shaft between him and the sky and he said, "You can't carry me up that."

"No," Nadia said. "We can carry you up it one rung at a time and you can hold the rungs with your arms and take your leg out of the load and let it hang, and if you put your weight on it once it will not hold you and you will fall on Bram and that will be the end of all three of us. So don't."

He looked at her for a beat longer than he needed to.

"Fine," he said, and it cost him something to say it, and she saw what it cost, and she did not have the room to care.

She went up first because the grate was the door and there was no point in bringing a man up a shaft to a door that would not open. The rungs were rusted to the thickness of her thumb and the fourth one was gone and she put her boot on the pit of it and went past, and the torch was in her teeth again, and above her the grate sat in its brick rebate with about a finger of clearance all round, which meant it had been set for a grate and not for a hinge, which meant it would lift out of the rebate if it could be lifted. She did the arithmetic on it while she climbed. She could move two hundred pounds with her legs on level ground. She could not lift it from the top of a ladder with her arms over her head, and if she tried she would fall twelve feet into the water and take the whole exit with her.

So she went to the top rung and put her back and her shoulders against the brick of the rise and set both palms flat on the underside of the grate and pushed and turned it.

The grate did not lift. The grate rotated.

It came round off one corner with a shriek and threw a rod of daylight down the shaft, and she took the corner and walked it round like a man walking a heavy door off its latch, and then she had one corner up and two fingers under it and could not lift, and she called down the shaft, "Bram. Up. Bring him up. I need a second pair of hands at the top and I need them now."

The ceiling at the bend clicked four times and then a fifth and then a long groan, and a slab of the arch came down into the tunnel where they had been standing a minute and a half before.

Bram came up the rungs with Kestrel roped under the arms with Bram's belt and both of them moving one rung at a time, and Kestrel's leg hanging, and Kestrel's hands on the rungs, and Bram's breath coming in one long sound, and the shaft narrowing above them until Bram's shoulders scraped the brick on both sides, and Nadia lay across the top of the shaft with both feet braced on the top rung and hauled on the grate, and Bram put his forearm up beside hers and the two of them lifted it together, and it came out and fell back onto the cobbles of the yard with a clang that anyone within a quarter mile would have heard.

Daylight. Grey, low, water-light, and the smell of the river blown across it, and the noise of the river, which was the loudest sound in the world.

The clicks had stopped. That was worse than the clicks.

She reached down and got Kestrel under the armpits the way Bram had, and Bram got him under the knees, and they brought him up through the grate one shoulder at a time — his bad leg trailing, his boot scraping the brick lintel — and laid him on the cobbles of the yard, and Nadia came out last and stood up in the open air for the first time in five hours and did not let herself look back.

Bram was on his knees over Kestrel, and the 1944 sheet was still flat against his chest inside his coat, and Kestrel was lying with his eyes open looking at the sky, and then Kestrel sat up on one elbow and looked back across the yard at the foundry, and Nadia looked too, because she had to.

The dry store had gone. Not fallen. Gone — the south corner of the basement level had folded into itself with the south wall still standing over the hole like a man standing over his own shadow, and the middle tower's foot had dropped with it and the tower had a bow in it now, a real bow, the sort of bow you see in a tree that is coming down, and the top of the tower was leaning off its own base by a foot and a half and leaning the way she had read it leaning, at a rate.

Beyond the yard, on the far side of the revetment, the tide was up to the lower flood marks on the wall.

The line of the river was the line of the sky.
