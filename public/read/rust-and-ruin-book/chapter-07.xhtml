The Archive

The room was smaller than the doorway promised, and it was full.

Nadia held the torch up and let her eyes take the shape of it before they took the things. Twelve feet by fourteen. Brick walls on three sides, the south wall on the fourth. A boarded floor on the same dwarf walls and piles as the corridor. A ceiling of joists and boards with a hatch in the middle and a ladder bolted to the wall beneath it, rungs furred with rust, going up into the dark of the ground floor. Along the east wall, two plan chests, one stacked on the other. Along the north wall, a shelf of box files and wire-bound ledgers, more than the satchel could carry, more than Bram had said were waiting. Against the west wall, a bench with a vice and a row of brass fittings laid out in a line — taps, valves, gauge bezels, a brass nameplate the length of her forearm — all of it green with verdigris, all of it worth money. Kestrel saw it before he saw anything else.

"There," he said, and started forward.

"Stop." Nadia put the light on his boots. "Floor first. Look at it before you walk it."

He stopped, engine still running. Nadia crouched and read the boards.

Dry. That was the first thing and the best thing. Dry boards, dry dwarf walls, dry brick to the height of her knee. Below that the brick went dark, the dark went down, and the water stood in the dwarf gap, not over it. The pile heads were dry. The rebate was dry. The room had been built dry and the south wall had kept it dry for eighty years, the crown of the wall outside down on one knee, pressing itself into the silt, while the room behind it had never once been wet.

But the water was higher in the gap than in the corridor. She measured it against her fingers, measured again, did not like the answer. In the corridor the water had stood two fingers below the floor boards. Here it stood one. Same tide, same minute, one finger more. The room sat lower, or the ground under it was softer, or the wall holding the room was giving the water a way in under the rebate. Any one of those was a reading to act on. All three together were a reading to leave on.

"Dry," she said. "Get to work. Bram, the chests. Kestrel — no. Slow. The floor's a piled floor. It takes weight on the piles, not between them, and the piles are wherever the dwarf walls are. You walk on the line of the wall or you don't walk."

"I heard you the first time." He moved along the west wall, heels on the dwarf wall line, reached the bench, leaned both hands on the top. The bench did not move. He began lifting the brass into his tool roll, piece by piece, each one a small dull clink, counting under his breath. Nadia let him. The brass was not the point and not hers.

Bram went to the plan chests.

He did not open the top one first. He set the satchel down beside the lower chest, crouched, placed both hands flat on the lid of the upper chest and held them there, the way a man holds his hands over a fire that has gone out. Then he pulled the top drawer.

It came out whole. The chest was oak, the runners oak, the drawer sliding on a long dry sigh. Inside, drawings lay flat, linen-backed, rolled nowhere, pressed flat under a sheet of cartridge paper gone the colour of weak tea. Bram lifted the paper off with both hands, set it on the floor, looked down.

Nine sheets. The top four were shop drawings, ironwork details of a barrier gate, inked in a fine hand with dimensions in feet and inches and pencil notes in the margins. The next three were a site plan of the river bank with a line run across it and hatched, labelled in block capitals. Bram read it aloud without meaning to.

"Aurel Flood Barrier. Section A–A. 1944."

The last two sheets were the ones that mattered. General arrangement, the barrier in plan and section. In the bottom right corner of the section sheet, a title block with four names inked in a neat row, and under them a fifth line: Erected by the workmen of the Aurel Foundry, 1944, in eleven weeks.

Bram put his hand on the sheet and did not move it.

"There's their names," he said. His voice had gone thin at the edges. "Four of them on the drawing and the line underneath says the rest. That's what I came for. That's the thing the city says never happened. There's a wall down there on the river bank that held the water off these streets in the spring of forty-four, and the men who poured it worked here, and the men who poured it are in a pay book in my satchel, and nobody in the planning office has ever seen this sheet."

He looked up at her, eyes wet, not seeming to know it.

"You have it," Nadia said. "Roll it flat, don't roll it up, put it between two sheets and under your arm and keep your arm over it. We came a long way for that sheet and we're not folding it."

He nodded and did what she said. His hands were steady, which was more than hers would have been.

Kestrel had the brass in his roll and the roll in his belt. He was looking at the bench.

"Whole bench is wrought iron," he said. "Hand-forged. They don't make it any more. Vice is a leg vice, that's a hundredweight on its own, and the gauge plate's brass with a maker's mark." He rubbed the mark with his thumb, looked, put his hand back in his pocket. "Worth more than the flywheel. But it's bolted down and I've got one arm and a flatbed at six, so it stays. Don't say anything."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were going to say something. You've got a face that says things." He turned away, looked at the ceiling hatch. "Where does that go?"

Nadia put the light on the hatch. Square, over the ladder, iron ring in the leaf, no lock. Above it the joists ran out to a landing, and beyond the hatch the ground floor went on into the dark. She had seen the ground floor from outside that morning. Three bays. Heavy floor. Machine beds bolted through the joists. A line shaft overhead running the length of the building on hangers.

"The ground floor," she said. "Which is where the line shaft is."

"Which is where the line shaft is," Kestrel said, slowly, not looking at her.

She watched him. He stood with his head up under the hatch, eye fixed on something she could not see, hand out of his pocket, doing sums. Her own mind ran alongside his. The line shaft ran through the middle of the building. The middle of the building was the middle tower. The middle tower had stopped ticking. A tower that stops ticking has not stopped failing. It has only stopped announcing it.

"Water's coming up in the gap," she said aloud, to stop his sums and hers together. "Look."

He looked. The water in the dwarf gap had reached the top of the brick and was lipping at the underside of the nearest board. As they watched it came through, a thin dark line, spread a foot into the room along the board and stopped, lay there shining.

"That's one board," Kestrel said.

"That's the tide."

"Tide turns. You told me."

"It turns in an hour and it doesn't turn back out of here in an hour. It comes up under the rebate and along the dwarf walls and meets the water already under the room and presses up. A floor on piles lets water pass under. The room's floor isn't the problem." She was talking fast. The reading had arrived all at once and it was bad and she wanted it out of her head and into the room. She swung the light to the south wall, the wall that had held the room dry since 1944, read it, read it again.

The wall was leaning. She had known that. The whole wall leaned, had leaned for sixty years, two degrees, pressing its heel into the silt, holding the middle tower up, steady and slow. But the heel of the wall was in the dwarf gap, and the dwarf gap was filling, and the silt the heel pressed into was silt the rising water was loosening, a tap at a time, the way you loosen a nail with a cold chisel. The wall was answering the water: not fast, not with a roar, but with a slow settling. She could see the rear face opening its joints a hair at the height of the dry line, courses no longer flush with those below.

The wall was stepping out into the room. Millimetres, a millimetre a minute. But the rafters ran to that wall and the ceiling joists rested on it. If the rear course walked out from under the joists, every joist in the room lost its bearing at once, and the ceiling came down on all three of them, the plan chests and the brass and the pay books with it.

She stood with the light on the joint and made herself say it plainly. Bram needed to hear it plainly. Kestrel needed to hear it plainly. She needed to hear it from her own mouth before she acted on it.

"The south wall's foot is going soft. The tide's under it and it's stepping out. When it steps out far enough, the joists lose their bearing and the ceiling drops. Twenty minutes, maybe less. It will not announce itself. It'll go all at once, the way an arch goes."

Bram's face went still. "Can we get out before then?"

"Down the corridor, through the keyhole, up the wall foot and back to the hatch. The hatch is a drain and the corridor goes under the arch foot in about the time it takes us to walk back along it. I don't like the corridor."

"And the hatch?"

"When I priced the hatch for Kestrel an hour ago it was a metre square mouth to a falling tide and I told him it would fill like a drain. I still mean it."

Kestrel had come off the west wall and stood in the middle of the room on the dwarf wall line, hands out from his sides, head up, reading the whole room at once the way a man reads a lot he intends to buy.

"So we're in a box," he said.

"We're in a box with a lid losing its hinges," Nadia said. "So we hold the hinges."

She turned and put the light on the room's other feature, the one she had logged when she came in and set aside because it had not been the most urgent thing, and which was now the most urgent thing. The bench against the west wall. The leg vice. And bolted to the bench, at the end nearest the south wall, an iron screw jack — a heavy old floor jack with a square thread, standing under a roof timber with its screw run out to within two inches of the beam. Put there by somebody who had seen the same joint opening in 1961, during the condition survey, and had done the obvious thing and had never come back to pump it. The room had been reopened for that survey, the jack installed, then resealed.

There was a jack.

Nadia put her hand on it and got the whole shape in one second, then stood very still, because the shape was bigger than the jack.

The jack could hold a joist. A jack is a point load and a point load on a wet silt footing is a point load into the void. Holding one joist was never going to hold the wall. But the jack was not the only thing they had. They had the wall's own weight and the building's own iron lying in the room, and the cinder bed outside. Kestrel had walked the flywheel down off its bed in the basement and left it standing in the water against the south wall's foot, on the other side of that wall from where she stood now. Between the flywheel and the room there was exactly one wall.

The flywheel was on the wrong side of the wall.

She said it out loud. "The flywheel's on the wrong side of the wall."

Kestrel said, "You want me to go back down the corridor and get a tonne and a half of cast iron and bring it up a slope of rubble in the dark."

"No."

"Good, because—"

"I want to put it on this side. It's already standing against the wall's foot in the basement, which is where a counterweight wants to be. The wall foot in the basement is the east face. Our wall foot is the west face of the same wall. Put the wheel hard against the basement side of the wall's foot and the heel is pressed back down instead of lifting, the joists keep their bearing, the room holds."

"You can't reach it through a wall."

"No." She was already moving to the bench. "But the line shaft can."

She climbed the ladder to the hatch, shouldered it up, let it fall back on its hinge, came up through into the ground floor of the old core with the light in her teeth. She stood in a room she had only seen from outside and looked down the length of it. There it was: the line shaft, a black iron rod as thick as her wrist, running the length of the building overhead on iron hangers bolted to the joists, pulleys and couplings and belt guides still on it, going out from the old core through a bearing in the south wall and into the middle tower, where the second flywheel — the twin of the one Kestrel had freed — still sat in its bed at the far end, unmoved since the works closed in 1965.

She put the light on the bearing in the wall. An iron hanger bolted to a timber, the timber bolted to the joists, the joists running on top of the south wall. The same wall. The whole building stitched to its own failing corner with its own iron.

She came back down the ladder and found them where she had left them, both looking up.

"There's no flywheel on this side," she said. "There's a line shaft on this side, and it goes through the wall, held here by two hangers and two bolts. On the other side is the second flywheel. I can't see it through the brick but I don't need to. The shaft is continuous. The wheel is on the end of it and has been hanging there for eighty years. If I put the jack under the roof timber and pump, I pick up the joist and load the wall into the joists. The joists push the wall down through the shaft. The shaft acts as a torsion arm, pulling the flywheel out against the wall foot on the far side. The wall's own weight and the building's own iron, doing the work."

"That's a lever," Kestrel said. "A lever a hundred feet long."

"A lever a hundred feet long, with a tonne and a half on one end and a wall on the other. The fulcrum is the bearing in the wall. I don't have to move anything to the other side of anything, because the other side is already attached."

Bram, from beside the chests, the 1944 sheet flat under his arm, satchel on his shoulder: "Will it hold?"

Nadia looked at the jack, the joint in the wall, the water lipping up through the boards along the dwarf wall line. She ran the sums in her head the way she had run them a thousand times in eleven years. This time she did not let the Rotherhithe stair in at the door of them. The sums came out clean.

"It'll hold long enough," she said. "Rig the jack, seat it on the cinder bed through the gap, pump until the joint closes, then the shaft takes it, and we're out. Bram, the sheet goes under your left arm, your arm over it, you go first down the corridor and you don't stop for me and you don't stop for him."

"And you?" Bram said.

"I stay on the jack until the joint closes. Then I come."

Kestrel crouched at the gap with the light, clearing brick and silt from the jack's base, hands fast and sure, no argument. Nadia knelt beside him, read the bearing line by line, told him where to seat the plate, where to put his weight, where not to. Above them the ceiling joists rested on the leaning wall. The water crept up through the floor a finger at a time. The flywheel hung in the dark on the far side of the wall at the end of its hundred feet of iron, waiting to be used.
