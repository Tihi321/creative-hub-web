Load Paths

Bram brought the drawings in a cardboard tube sealed with a rubber band gone slack. He carried it the way people carry umbrellas in weather that has already turned, as if the sky had a claim on it.

"1961," he said. "Condition survey, before the last refit. Signed by the borough engineer. Ink on linen."

Nadia slid the roll out onto the tailgate of her truck and weighted the corners with two spanners and a thermos. The linen had gone soft as a shirt. Three towers in section, a boiler house, a chimney bay, and on the basement level a small cross inked in red with four words beside it in a draughtsman's slanting hand: DRY STORE — PAY BOOKS, KEEP.

"That cross," Bram said. "The pay books and the barrier drawings went into the dry store in 1943. The barrier was poured the summer after. The city has no record of it because the works was private and the war office never filed the return."

"And in sixty years nobody's walked in and taken them."

"Nobody's walked in." He said it gently, correcting a small arithmetic mistake. "The fence has been locked since 1998. The last tenant left in '96. Paper survives locked doors."

Nadia looked past him at the gate: two padlocks on a chain, and between them a third thing, a cut link, bright where the hacksaw had gone through and dull where the weather had found it since. Under the fence line, four cigarette ends, filter tips, all the same brand, all fresh enough that the paper had not yet bleached. Somebody had been taking an interest in the Aurel Foundry that was not the interest of an archivist.

"That's a week old, maybe less," she said.

Bram glanced at it and dismissed it with the same mildness. "Scrap boys. They come for the copper and leave when the weight beats them."

"They don't usually come back four times."

"They will not come back for paper," he said, and rolled the drawing into its tube as if he were putting a child to bed.

She left it. She had two hours in her head and a tide in her favour and a form in her kitchen that she had signed without reading, and none of that improved by arguing with a man about his own building.

She unloaded the rest onto the apron: the level, the folding rule, fifty metres of tape, a plumb bob on braided line, a chalk box, a four-pound hammer, a crack comparator card, a screwdriver, a headlamp, knee pads, and a notebook with a column ruled down the middle of each page. The apron smelled of river and hot tar and something under both, iron and wet lime, the particular smell of a building that has been crying into its own foundations for a century.

"What are you doing first?" Bram asked.

"Establishing what holds what," she said. "A building tells you where it hurts if you ask in the right order. Ask it about the roof first and it lies to you. You start at the ground."

She set the level on the plinth and then took it down again, because a level is only ever as good as the ground it stands on, and this ground was doing the backstroke. Instead she scratched her own datum on the revetment at the waterline, a short white line with the time beside it in chalk, and measured everything from that.

"The river's the only fixed point here," she said. "Twice a day it comes back to the same marks. Nothing else on this site does."

She took the first reading. The water was two inches above her scratch before she had finished chalking the second tower's base.

Inside, the light went the colour of weak tea and the sound changed, the way sound changes when a room has a soft ceiling and no people. Their footsteps stopped going anywhere. The dust had settled into long drifts along the wall footings, and through the drifts ran the trajectories of water: dark stains rising from the floors, following plaster, stopping at a line on every wall at the same height. She knelt and measured the line at three places. Eleven inches.

"That's the flood mark," Bram said. "1953."

"That's the spring tide that comes through the west bastion nine days out of ten," she said. "The 1953 mark is the one above it, the pale one, at two foot one. This is just Tuesday."

He was quiet for a moment. Then: "I had not considered that the building floods now."

"It floods twice a day. It never dries." She stood. "Nothing in here is dry at the level of its own rebar. That's why we're doing this the slow way."

The north tower was the first. Twenty-four metres from apron to parapet, built lean even in the survey drawing, and leaning more now. She stood at its base, sighted up the corner where quoin stones stepped back from the vertical, and read the lean by the gap it made against something straight. There was no straight thing left on the site except the tape she unrolled.

She hung the plumb bob from the second-floor window head and let it settle. The string came to rest a little over eight hundred millimetres clear of the sill below.

"Two degrees," she said. "Give or take a tenth. It was drawn at three-quarters of a degree."

"Is that a lot?"

"It's about twenty inches of travel at the top since it was built, and the last two inches of it have happened recently, because the crack in this corner hasn't had time to grow a beard." She put her thumb in the crack at the quoin. It was wide enough to take the first joint of it, and clean-edged, no moss, no lime bloom. New work by an old building's standards: probably this decade.

She wrote it down. Then she stood back and looked at where the tower's weight was going.

Here was the thing she had come to read. The tower had not simply leaned; it had leaned and then redistributed itself, the way a man with a bad hip shifts onto the other leg without deciding to. The northeast quoin had taken the load and gone hard and grey with it, the joints tight and shiny as slate. The southwest corner, relieved of duty, had opened. Above the door, a lintel had begun to bow, and beneath the lintel the masonry had cracked in a shallow arch, which meant that the lintel had stopped carrying and the wall was carrying itself over the opening instead. Brick can do that. It does it badly, and it does it loudly, but it does it.

"Load doesn't disappear," she said, half to the notebook. "It goes looking for another way down. That's all a load path is. It's the route the weight takes when it's tired of the route you gave it."

"You are describing the building as if it were alive."

"I'm describing it as if it were stubborn." She turned to the middle tower. "Which is more dangerous."

The middle tower's crack ran from the sill of the second-floor window diagonally down across four courses to the door head, and it did not run straight. It stepped, one mortar joint at a time, a staircase of twenty-millimetre treads, because the brick was stronger than the lime mortar and the crack had no interest in doing work it could avoid. She held the comparator card against it at three heights. Nine millimetres at the top. Seven at the middle. Four at the bottom.

"It's wider up here than down there," she said.

"Does that matter?"

"Everything matters in that direction. A crack that's wide at the top and narrow at the bottom is a crack that's opening now." She put her ear against the wall, then tapped it with the hammer, working along the joints. Some places rang. Some places gave a flat, papery thud and shed a little dust.

Bram watched her with his gloves on. He had put the white cotton ones on without seeming to notice he had done it, the way she put on her hard hat.

"Corrosion," she said, standing back. "There's an iron tie running through here. Two of them, probably, one at each floor. They were put in when the tower was built to hold the roof against thrust. Water gets in at the ends, the iron rusts, the rust is thicker than the iron was. It has nowhere to go, so it pushes. That crack follows the mortar because the mortar is the soft route out. So the tie that was holding the tower together is now the thing prying it apart, eight millimetres and growing."

"And the tie plates?"

"Are carrying load they were not designed for, in a direction nobody drew." She wrote the numbers down, each in its column. "The tower's still up. It's just not up the way it was specified."

She had, at the back of her skull, the other stair. The one in Rotherhithe with the terraced houses either side and the crane hire on the invoice, the one she had signed safe. She did not take it out and look at it. She had learned to leave it in its drawer while she worked, and only open the drawer at night. But she knew what it felt like when a structure was giving her a true reading, and this one was. It was the first completely true reading she had taken in eleven years.

"Good," she said quietly. "Right."

The stair was in the join between the middle and the north towers, a stone flight climbing through a brick shaft, and the door at its head was the way out of the whole west range.

The door had been hung true in 1908. It was not true now. Nadia lifted the handle and the leaf came forward two inches and stopped, binding on its top corner, and when she leaned her shoulder into it, it opened with a sound like a cupboard full of crockery and then swung free, dragging a clean arc through the dust that did not match the arc of any hinge.

"Doorframe's racked," she said. "About a finger."

She went down the first four treads. Then she stopped, took the chalk out, and struck a line along the wall from the landing, horizontal, following the line of the floor she had just left. She measured down to the stair nosings at the top of the flight and again at the bottom.

"Forty millimetres," she said. "The whole flight has rotated. It's dropped at the newel and lifted at the wall, which means it's still bearing at the wall and it's lost its bearing at the bottom, where the water is."

Bram looked over her shoulder at the chalk. "Is that bad?"

"This is the stair you came in on."

"There's another."

"There's a ladder to the boiler roof and a hatch you can't reach from the ground, and there's a service tunnel under the revetment that the tide is using as a drain." She capped the chalk and looked up at him. "So no. There isn't another. Not one you can use at high water with a bag of ledgers."

He absorbed that the way he absorbed everything, slowly and without flinching, and then said the thing she had been waiting for since the gate.

"Then we use this one. The dry store is forty feet from the foot of these stairs."

"The basement's flooded."

"Four inches."

"It was four inches at the door," she said. "It's four inches at the door because the door is at the top of a slope. At the far end the slab has settled and the silt has come in and it's deeper, and the deeper it is, the more the slab is under it."

She went down to the foot of the flight and crouched. The lowest tread sat on the brick pad of the newel wall, and the pad had been eaten by water so that the mortar had gone soft and mealy and washed out of the joint, leaving the tread resting on three small contact points and a shadow. She put the screwdriver in and it went in four inches with no resistance at all: wet sand, then a void with a lip of sound under it.

She took the hammer and tapped the slab beyond the door. Two metres from the threshold, a hollow ring. Four metres, a flat dead thud. Six metres, and the hammer left a pale bruise and a hairline crack opened alongside it, and the crack breathed a small bubble of air into the standing water, which meant there was a pocket underneath her that had been sealed and was now not.

She stood up and wiped her hands.

"1961 slab on silt," she said. "A hundred millimetres of concrete over nothing much. The tide fills the ground under it twice a day, and when the water goes out, it takes the fines with it. There are voids down there the size of a kitchen table. The slab is not a floor any more, it's a lid."

"And the columns?"

"The columns are fine. The columns go down to the footings. The load never liked the slab anyway — it goes beam to column to ground. So we stay on the beams. We keep our feet over the beams and the column caps, and we do not put our weight between them, not even to see."

"And the records are where?"

"Say they're at the far end, and I'll believe you, and I'll still tell you that the far end is where the slab has fallen most, because that's where the water sits longest." She looked at her chalk line and then at the waterline on the revetment wall outside. She had chalked the rise while they talked. Three-quarters of an inch in twenty-five minutes, and it was making up its mind to hurry.

Bram took the tube of drawings out of his satchel and set it down on the top step, out of the water, with a care that stung her.

"I have three months," he said. "My permit lapsed in March and it will not be renewed, because the building is coming down in six days and the department does not renew permits for buildings that cannot be accessed. I have one archivist's salary and one lapsed form. What I do not have is a second chance at this building, because in six days there is no this building." He straightened up. "I know what you think of me. I've been told I care about paper more than about the men who made it. But the barrier the men of this works poured in 1944 is still there, under the embankment, doing its work, and nobody knows it, and when the works is gone there will be nothing on earth to say who poured it. That is what I am here for. Not the paper. The names."

The water moved under the door, a slow deliberate tongue of it, and found a new low line and took it.

Nadia took out the rope, tied one end around the base of the nearest column with a round turn and two half hitches, and dropped the rest of the coil down the stairs so that it lay up the treads, off the slab, waiting to be used.

"Rules," she said. "We keep the rope on. We walk where the beams are. We take the readings as we go, because I want to know what the water is doing before it tells me. And when I say out, we are out, and we do not discuss it in the basement. Do you understand?"

"I understand."

"You're standing on the third tread."

"I know."

"It moved three millimetres when you stepped on it. I felt it through the wall. That's your stair, and it's the only one you've got." She pulled the headlamp down, clicked it on, and put her foot on the bottom landing, feeling for the contact through the sole of her boot, listening for the change from the deadness of the slab to the ring of the beam under it. The building shifted its weight. Somewhere above them a rod of iron let go of a century of tautness and settled back with a long low note, like a bass string being tuned.

She counted the tide in her head, and what she had left of it, and the small stubborn weight of the man behind her, and went down.
