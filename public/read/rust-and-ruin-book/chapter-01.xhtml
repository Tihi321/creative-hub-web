The Permit and the Tide

The tide was already turning when Nadia Voss parked on the gravel shoulder outside the Aurel Foundry. She could see it in the river’s edge — the waterline had crept up the concrete revetment by two inches since the morning, leaving a dark stain that would not dry until the next ebb. She sat in the car for a moment, engine off, watching the stain. It was the kind of thing she noticed without deciding to notice: load paths, water seeking the lowest point, the slow patient work of gravity on a structure that had stopped being maintained.

The stain was not a stain. It was a record. The concrete revetment had been poured in 1962, a year when the river was still managed, when the harbour authority maintained the banks, when the silt was dredged on a schedule that no longer existed. The concrete had been mixed with a retarder, a chemical that slowed the hydration, which meant the surface had cured slowly, which meant the surface had developed a skin, a thin layer of calcium carbonate that sealed the pores. The skin was what made the concrete look solid. The skin was what made the concrete look like it could hold water. But the skin was not the concrete. The skin was a record of the concrete’s condition at the moment of pouring, and the record was a record of the moment, not of the present. The present was the stain. The stain was the water finding the pores beneath the skin, the pores that the skin had sealed in 1962, the pores that the skin had sealed for sixty years until the skin cracked, until the skin was no longer a skin but a memory of a skin, and the memory was the stain, and the stain was the thing Nadia saw when she looked at the revetment: not a stain but a sentence, a sentence the river was writing on the concrete, a sentence that said: the water is here, the water has always been here, the water will be here after the concrete is gone.

She sat in the car for a moment. The engine ticked as it cooled. The river moved. The stain widened. She watched it widen for three minutes, which was longer than she needed to watch it, longer than the job required, longer than the two hours Bram had said on the phone the day before, in a voice that carried the particular urgency of a man who had been refused by three other engineers. Two hours. Walk in, find the records, walk out. The demolition permit had been issued six days ago, and the wrecking crews would arrive on the seventh. Bram’s permit to access the site had lapsed in March, and he could not enter without someone who could sign off on the structural safety. Nadia was that someone. She had signed the form in her kitchen, standing over the counter with a cup of tea going cold, and she had not thought about what she was signing. She had thought about the two hours.

The two hours were the thing that made her come. The two hours were the thing that made her sit in the car and watch the stain. The two hours were the thing that made her look at the building and see the building as a thing that could be read in two hours, a thing that could be assessed in two hours, a thing that could be signed in two hours. The two hours were the thing that made her think the job was simple. The two hours were the thing that made her think the job was a walk.

She got out of the car. The gravel crunched under her boots. The air smelled of iron and salt, the smell of a place where the river had been working on the building for longer than anyone had been keeping records. The foundry sat on a bend of the tidal river where the water ran brown and slow, carrying silt from upstream fields. Three towers rose from the main shed, their brickwork stained to a uniform rust colour by decades of iron dust settling into the mortar. The river had eaten at the foundations for longer than anyone had been keeping records, and the ground around the base was soft alluvial soil, the kind that required pile foundations rather than shallow strip footings. The Aurel had been built in 1891 on strip footings. That was the first thing Nadia saw when she stepped out of the car, and it was the thing that made her pause.

She paused because the strip footings were the thing that should not have been there. The strip footings were the thing that should have been pile foundations. The strip footings were the thing that meant the building was built on a ground that could not hold it, a ground that was soft, a ground that was alluvial, a ground that was the river’s memory of itself, the river’s memory of the silt it carried, the river’s memory of the fields upstream, the river’s memory of the rain that fell on the fields and ran into the river and carried the silt to the bend and deposited the silt in the ground beneath the building. The silt was the ground, and the ground was the silt, and the silt was soft, and the soft ground was the thing that should have been held by piles, and the piles were not there, and the strip footings were there, and the strip footings were the thing that meant the building was standing on a memory, a memory of the river, a memory that was soft, a memory that was shifting, a memory that was shifting because the tide moved, because the tide moved twice a day, because the tide moved twice a day for a hundred and thirty-one years, because the tide moved twice a day for a hundred and thirty-one years and the building did not move, because the building did not move for a hundred and thirty-one years, because the building did not move for a hundred and thirty-one years until the building started to move, until the building started to move because the ground beneath it started to move, because the ground beneath it started to move because the tide moved.

Nadia looked at the three towers. The north tower leaned two degrees from vertical. She could see it from the car park, a slight tilt in the cornice line, the way a sentence tilts when the writer is tired. The south tower was straight but its base was submerged in standing water that had no business being there in July. The middle tower, the tallest, had a crack running from the third-floor window down to the ground, a crack that followed the mortar joints rather than the brick, which meant the mortar had failed before the brick. That was a chloride failure. Salt water in the joints, salt water in the concrete, salt water in the steel reinforcement bars inside the concrete. The bars were corroding, expanding, pushing the concrete apart from the inside. The building was not merely old. It was actively failing, and the tide was the mechanism of that failure.

She looked at the north tower. The tilt was two degrees. Two degrees was not much. Two degrees was the thing you could not see unless you looked for it, unless you looked for it the way she looked for it in every building she had assessed in the eleven years since she qualified. She had made a career of noticing the small lean, the hairline crack, the damp patch that meant the water had found a new path. She had made a career of it until the career stopped mattering.

She walked toward the gate. The chain was locked with two padlocks, and between them a third thing: a cut link, bright where the hacksaw had gone through and dull where the weather had found it since. Under the fence line, four cigarette ends, filter tips, all the same brand, all fresh enough that the paper had not yet bleached. Somebody had been taking an interest in the Aurel Foundry that was not the interest of an archivist.

She walked back to the truck. Bram was waiting beside the tailgate, a cardboard tube under his arm, sealed with a rubber band gone slack. He carried it the way people carry umbrellas in weather that has already turned: as if the sky had a claim on it.

“1961,” he said. “Condition survey, before the last refit. Signed by the borough engineer. Ink on linen.”

Nadia slid the roll out onto the tailgate and weighted the corners with two spanners and a thermos. The linen had gone soft as a shirt. Three towers in section, a boiler house, a chimney bay, and on the basement level a small cross inked in red with four words beside it in a draughtsman’s slanting hand: DRY STORE — PAY BOOKS, KEEP.
