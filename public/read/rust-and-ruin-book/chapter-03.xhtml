The Stairwell Drops

The basement did not end where the drawings said it ended. The 1961 survey showed a rectangular void forty metres by eighteen, columns in three rows, a boiler house against the north wall, the dry store against the south. What Nadia found, at the foot of the stair, was a room that had swollen. Silt had come in through the river wall and pushed everything it could not drown into the corners. The floor was a shallow dish with a rim of debris and a centre full of standing water the colour of stewed tea. Beams stood out of it in rows, their top flanges a hand's width above the surface, webs disappearing into the murk. Above, the ceiling was a grid of brick jack-arches springing between the beams; below, the slab showed only where it had cracked and settled in bright pale slabs that caught the headlamp and threw it back.

She stood at the foot of the stair and let her eyes take the room in before her feet took her anywhere. Eyes, then feet. Always. The boiler house was a black brick box against the north wall, its firebox door fallen open, its flue pipe hanging from the ceiling like a dropped arm. A hand-cart lay on its side in the third bay, most of its wood eaten down to the iron, the iron gone to lace. Somewhere a pipe dripped, and the dripping had a rhythm — the tide. She did not need to see the waterline to know it was rising. She looked at the column bases, the beam seats, the arch haunches where mortar had washed out and left bricks sitting on their own weight like books on a shelf with the shelf removed.

"Two-metre spacing," she said. "Beam to beam. Don't step on the arch. The arch is only as strong as the slab under it, and the slab is a biscuit."

Bram came behind her, the satchel of ledgers strapped high on his chest, his cotton gloves wet through and black at the fingertips. He walked like a man on a frozen lake — correct, careful — and he did not complain, which she had not expected. Twice she looked back and found him standing still with his head up, reading the ceiling, lips moving slightly as though he were reading a page. Once he said, "The far row is bowed."

She looked. The third row of beams, the southernmost, along the wall where the dry store should have been, had a sag she had not seen from the stair. Not much. A hundred millimetres over seven metres, maybe less. But the sag was new — the chalky spalling on the beam's top flange was still crisp along the belly of the curve, no darkening, no silt stain. Something had changed there recently. Something was changing now.

"It's the tie," she said. "The middle tower's tie. The wall's come in at the top and pushed the reaction down onto that row. The south wall's carrying the tower's lean."

"Is that a problem?"

"It's a load path. One that wasn't in the design." She kept her voice flat; flatness was a tool and she needed it. "The wall's been doing it for a while. If it stops doing it, it will stop all at once."

He thought about that for a step or two. "The wall is carrying the tower," he said. "The wall is holding up the thing that is pushing it over."

"Yes."

"That is what the barrier does. That is what the men in 1944 did. They poured a wall to take what the river pushes, and they did not build it to be seen. It holds and no one knows it holds." He was quiet. "The building is doing the same thing. It is holding itself up, and it is doing it badly, and no one is watching it do it."

"Somebody is watching it now," she said, and wished she had not, because it sounded like a promise and she did not make promises to buildings.

They walked the second bay in silence, and the silence was not empty. It was full of small talk — the tick of cooling iron, the creak of the arch grid taking its load one brick at a time, the lapping of water under the beams where the slab had gone and left a shelf. The headlamp threw their shadows onto the north wall, and the shadows walked ahead and lagged behind and now and then agreed about where the columns were. The wall behind the shadows was papered with a hundred years of other walls: bricked-up windows, whitewashed patches, lines of pipe removed and holes left, and one long green stain that had started at the ceiling and ended, fat and dark, at the floor, having taken sixty years to fall.

She reached the middle of the room and stopped and made herself look up.

The ceiling over the second bay was not square to the walls. She had seen it at the stair and not trusted it; now she measured it with the corner of her eye against the beam line. The arch grid had a twist in it, a slow spiral from the north end of the bay to the south, and the twist was more at the eastern edge, the river edge, the edge with the water under it. A twist in a ceiling means one wall has gone down or one wall has gone out. She turned and looked at the south wall and found what she wanted and did not want: the wall was not vertical. It went out of plumb at the top, leaning into the room by perhaps forty millimetres over the height of the basement. The lean was not new and not old — the dust on the wall told her that, a fine grey veil unbroken over the whole surface, which meant the lean had been taken up slowly, across many years, the way a man takes up a limp.

They reached the middle of the room. The dry store was a brick cell no bigger than a cloakroom, built into the south wall between two columns, with a steel door rusted into the frame. Bram put his shoulder to it and it did not move. He put both hands on the handle and lifted and the door came out of the frame with a crack and a shower of scale, and a smell of old ink and dust and cool dry paper came out of the dark like a letter from June in November.

"Dry," he said softly. "It's dry."

It was dry. She leaned past him and put the headlamp in. Shelves of grey steel, empty in the first bay, then box files leaning against each other, then a stack of ledgers wired in bundles, and on the top shelf a plan chest with two drawers, its dust undisturbed. She could see the corner of a linen sheet inside, the same soft yellow-cream as the 1961 survey. Bram stood in the doorway and did not go in, and she understood, without being told, that he was giving the room its moment. She let him have fifteen seconds of it before she said, "Two minutes. A box at a time. The satchel first, the plan chest second, nothing else."

He went in. She stood in the beam line and watched the water and the beams and the sag on the third row and listened to the connections, which had gone quiet. Quiet connections were not good news. A connection that groans is a connection still working. A connection that stops groaning has stopped working, or found a new shape, and either way the load has gone somewhere else.

She thought about the Rotherhithe stair and put her hand flat on the nearest column cap. The cold of the iron went up her arm and steadied her. She took her hand away.

The water was at the top flange of the second row now. It had come up a hundred and fifty millimetres while they walked, faster than the river alone could explain. She stood very still and let the building tell her why. The floor moved. Not much, not a step, but a shift in the whole slab, a slow tilting of the dish as though a great hand had lifted one edge. The water on the floor moved eastward in a sheet, all at once, and lapped against the base of the column between them and the stair.

That was the moment the room changed its mind about them. She had felt it before, twice in eleven years — once in a foundry in Rotherhithe, once under a hospital in Reading — and both times it had come as a small wrongness under the sole of the boot, a two-millimetre disagreement between what the floor had promised and what it delivered. Both times she had done exactly what she did now: she stopped, she counted her own weight, and she listened.

"Bram."

"Almost —"

"Now."

He came out with the satchel fat with files and his arms around a bundle of ledgers tied with wire. She took the bundle out of his arms and put it on the beam. "Stair. Now. Walk where I walk."

He looked at the door of the dry store and then at her and then at the door again, and she saw him do the arithmetic of a man who had waited three months for this room and would not get another three. Then he closed the door behind him — gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person — and said, "Lead."

They went back the way they had come, and the floor came with them. That was the thing she would remember. It did not fall away in front of them; it travelled, the whole dish of it tilting east and south, and as it tilted the slab between the beams cracked in a dozen places at once with a sound like ice on a pond in March, and the cracks did not stop at the slab but ran up into the bases of the columns, which meant the column bases were moving, which meant the footings were moving, which meant the ground under them had just realised it was silt.

The arch over the third bay let go first. It let go silently, which was worse than noise, and then the bay came down — slab, arch, silt, and the standing water on top of it, all together — and the hole it left was a metre and a half wide and the water fell into it in a spiral like a drain, and the whole room breathed out a smell of rot and river mud and rust.

"Keep to the beams!" she shouted, and Bram kept to the beams, and so did she, and they went up the room at a fast shuffle, hand on the column caps, feet on the flanges. The beams were still holding. She counted them as she passed — four, three, two, one — and then she was at the foot of the stair and the stair was not there.

Not gone. Rotated. The whole flight had swung on the newel pad like a drawbridge on one hinge. The bottom three treads dropped into the water and the rest of the flight leaned out into the room at an angle no stair had ever been designed to hold. The door at the top of it was no longer above them. It was five metres up and three metres across, hanging in the wall like a picture knocked crooked, and the wall it hung in had closed on it. The doorframe had racked so far that the leaf was bound top and bottom, and as they watched, a course of brick above the lintel dropped two centimetres and took the lintel with it, and the gap between the door leaf and the frame closed to a line.

The water in the bottom of the stair shaft was up to the second tread. The second tread was in the water, the first tread under it, and she could see the two of them down there, submerged and pale, through the brown glass of the surface. Above them the rise of the flight and the shift of the whole thing on its pad, the newel wall bulging a finger to the room.

Nadia stood at the foot of the wrecked flight with her hand on the column and did not move.

She knew this. She had stood at the foot of a stair like this before. Rotherhithe, the terraced houses, the crane hire on the invoice, the stair that had come away from the wall while four men were on it. The men had gone down through it and through the floor behind it and into the cellar underneath, and she had stood at the top and watched the dust come up the stairwell and had said into her radio the words she had said a hundred times since in the dark: the structure is stable, the loading is within the design envelope, proceed. She had read it wrong. She had read the stair like a stair and not like a load path, and the stair had been a load path all along, a bad one, and it had been waiting for exactly the weight she put on it. Four men had not walked out of that cellar. She had signed the form and gone to the inquiry and sat in a chair for four hours and listened to a man with a barrister's voice explain the difference between what she had seen and what was there, and then she had not taken a true reading of anything for eleven years, until yesterday, on the plinth of the Aurel Foundry, with the turning tide.

The drawer in the back of her skull was open, and the thing inside it was out, and it filled the room. For a long moment she was not in the Aurel Foundry at all. She was at the top of another stair in the rain, with a clipboard in her hand and a radio in the other, and the dust was coming up towards her, and there was a voice on the radio asking a question and she was answering it and she could not hear her own answer, and the answer she gave had men's names in it. She felt the old cold go through her, the specific cold of having been certain and wrong.

"Nadia."

She heard him from a long way off.

"Nadia — the stair —"

"I know what it is." Her voice came out steadier than she expected, and she made use of it. "It's not gone. It's rotated forty more millimetres on the newel and lost its top bearing at the wall plate. The wall plate's still there. The stringer's still in the wall, just not at the top. Look."

He looked. The stringer, the long iron edge of the stair that had sat on the wall for a century, was still embedded in the brick at its bottom end. The pad at its top end had come out of the wall by a finger's width. The whole flight was hanging on that one finger, and on the newel wall pad below, which was wet and mealy and had already been three contact points and a shadow when she read it at the top of the day.

"It'll fall if I put my weight on it," she said. "It might fall if I don't. It's fallen as far as the newel will let it, and the newel is the only thing holding it, and the newel is standing in water." She took the chalk from her pocket and drew a line on the column, level with the top of the fallen stair. Then she drew a second line, level with the door lintel. The difference between the lines was a metre and a half, and the difference was their problem.

She made herself go through it the way she would go through a survey, in order, writing it in her head where she could not write it on paper. The stair was the only way out. The door at the head of the stair was the only way out. The door was bound in its frame. The frame was carrying a course of brick that had just dropped. The stair's top bearing had failed, so the load that had once gone through the stair to the ground was now going through the newel wall, which was standing in water and had gone soft, and the newel wall was going to give, and when it gave the flight would come down into the shaft and fill it, and the door would stay exactly where it was — racked shut a metre and a half above reach.

Any of those facts alone was a sentence in a report. Together they were an argument, and the argument was: you misread this building at the door as you misread the other one at the top of the other stair, and this time the building is telling you so in time.

She understood, standing there with her hand on the column, that the difference between now and Rotherhithe was not that she had become cleverer. It was that now she was reading the building rather than her memory of a building. The memory said: a stair like this fell once, therefore it will fall. The reading said: this stair is standing on a wet newel pad, has lost its top bearing, and is holding the load through a path you can see and measure. The memory was a closed drawer. The reading was an open room, and she could walk in it.

She put her ear against the column. Above them the building was making a new sound, a slow rhythmic tick, once every two seconds, like a clock with too much weight on it. She knew the tick. It was the sound of a brittle material being asked for the last of its elongation, and it was coming from the middle tower, from the crack, from the tie that was prying the tower open and had found, at last, the bottom of the crack and the end of its patience.

"Bring the rope," she said. "And stay off the stair."
