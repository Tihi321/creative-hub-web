Rust and Ruin

The report took her four days to write and she wrote it the way she wrote everything, from the bottom up, which is to say from the ground by the river.

She did it at her kitchen table because she had no office to go back to and no inclination to rent one, and because the light in the kitchen came in at the right angle after eleven in the morning, and because a report about a building that was standing in water wanted to be written somewhere dry. She had her notes in a shoebox and a stack of photographs from Bram's phone, and the site plan from the 1961 survey spread under the salt cellar, and she worked in the order the water had worked: the revetment, the strip footings, the raft, the silt, the leak at the joint, the lean.

The photographs were poor. Bram's phone had been wet and the flash had caught the sheet in two of them and the ledger spines in three, and she had to hold the last one at an angle to the window to read the pencil in the title block, which was still legible, which was a small miracle of graphite and dry air and a room that had kept its own weather since 1944. She had put the photographs in order with the earliest at the back and she kept turning the stack round by mistake, and by the second day she had stopped doing that and had begun to write it straight.

She wrote two hundred and twelve paragraphs and cut it to ninety.

The paragraph that mattered was paragraph forty-one, and in the final draft it read:

The barrier's east abutment is founded on, and structurally continuous with, the quay wall of the Aurel Foundry. The wall is a gravity structure of coursed rubble built on the strip footings of the foundry and tied to the abutment by a return keyed into the 1944 work. Demolition of the foundry to ground level, by machine or by marine collapse, would remove the lateral support on which the abutment's west face depends. Any proposal to demolish or remove the foundry must be preceded by a full and independent assessment of the barrier's west end. The writer is of the opinion that no such assessment has ever been made.

She read that paragraph back six times and each time she looked for the place where her own memory was doing the reading, and each time she found the place and left the sentence standing, because the sentence was not a memory. It was a measurement. The wall did what the wall did on every tide and had done it since 1891 and she had put her hand on it and felt it carry.

She ended it with the drawings, nine of them, in order of date, and the four names, and a note in a paragraph of its own: The barrier was erected in eleven weeks in 1944 by the workmen of the Aurel Foundry, using the foundry's crane, line shaft, and plant. No public record of the work has been located in the borough's minutes for 1943-46. The drawing set is the only surviving record known to the writer.

She signed it, dated it, printed it, and drove it to the council offices herself, and she sat in the lobby for an hour until a woman came down and took it from her hands. The lobby had a big window and a plastic tree and a queue that shuffled forward by one every four minutes, and Nadia sat with the report in a rigid envelope on her knees and watched the queue and did not read the noticeboard, which was full of things that were not her business. The woman who came down was younger than she was and had a lanyard and no lanyard time to spare for her, and she took the envelope and asked was it the foundry, and Nadia said it was the foundry, and the woman said they'd been expecting it, and went back up the stairs with it, holding it flat in both hands the way you hold a cake.

It was out of her hands. That was the thing about writing it down. A thing written down and handed over had a life that did not belong to the writer, and did not need the writer, and could not be revised by the writer, and Nadia drove home with the empty envelope on the passenger seat and felt, for the first time in a long while, finished with something in the right way.

The listing meeting was in the second week of August and it had a carpet and eight chairs and a table with water jugs, and Nadia sat at the side and was called by the chair to speak to paragraph forty-one and spoke for six minutes. She had written out the six minutes in the car and followed them exactly, because she knew what happened to an engineer who improvised in front of a committee, and she watched eight pairs of eyes do the thing that eyes do when a colleague has handed them something they cannot open, and she watched the chair look at the photographs of the 1944 sheet and turn the photographs round so the public gallery could see them, which was a paper of its own.

The room was hot and the air conditioning made a noise like a distant cough, and the water jugs sweated onto the table, and the chair had a way of saying thank you that meant next. There was a man from the barrier's authority who said the barrier had been inspected in 1998 and found sound, and who said it twice, as if the second time might close the matter. There was a woman from a heritage trust who said the foundry was the last 1891 tidal ironworks in the reach, and who had brought a folder of her own and did not open it, and who looked at Nadia across the table once with an expression that said the two of them had read the same wrong literature and had each found the right thing late. There was a councillor who said the borough had spent eight hundred pounds on photocopies of a drawing of a wall and there was a small, careful laugh from the gallery, and then the chair said the words tide-adapted industrial assembly line out loud, which were not Nadia's words but were close enough, and the vote went nine to two with one abstention and the foundry was referred to the secretary of state with a recommendation for listing, a decision to preserve the drawings and hold the tower until the recommendation was determined, which meant that the machines went quiet and the muncher went home and the leaning tower stood in its wet yard with a barricade at the gate and an engineer's watch and a survey benchmark painted on its base.

The abstention was a man who said, when the vote came to him, that he had read the report and thought it was sound and thought the drawings should be kept, and that he would not vote either way because a leaning tower in a river was not a thing a man like him should be asked to decide, and Nadia thought that was the most honest sentence of the morning.

Bram was in the gallery and shook her hand afterwards in the corridor and said, "You said only surviving record known to the writer, which is an archivist's phrase, which means you were listening," and she said she had been listening, and he said, "Well, it is, and I'll put it in a box with my name on it, and the box will be in the strongroom, and I'll sleep better."

It did not stand for a week. It stood for a week and then some, and then one morning at the top of the tide the middle tower let go the last of its bearing on the water side and slid, slowly, the way a cabinet slides on a wet floor, the foot-and-a-half bow it had carried since the night of the rescue opening into a full lean and then into a fall, and it came to rest with two of its three walls in the river, and the yard was a pool and the basement was silt and the south wall stood over nothing with nothing holding it, and Nadia watched all of it from the top of the slope with her hands in her pockets and did not feel anything she could name, because she had read the lean and the lean had done what she had read.

The noise was not a crash. It was a long wet grinding and then a small collapse at the end, like a cough, and then the water moved in the way water moves when something has given it a room, and Nadia stood at the top of the slope in the grey and watched the whole thing go the way she had written that it would go, which was not a comfort and was not a grief, and was not nothing either.

They had got the paper out. That was the whole of it. Nine drawings, a plan chest, forty-one ledgers, and a title block with four names in it, and none of it had gone into the river.

Bram took the archive to a strongroom at the university and worked on it for the better part of a year, and the barrier was dated, documented, and photographed for the first time, and the four names were cut into a stone set into the parapet of the revetment wall, and the man in orange brought his grand-dee's brother's line of the family to see it and stood in front of it in his good jacket and did not say anything for a while. He had asked her by email, and Bram had organised it, and Nadia had not gone, because it was not hers to be at.

The borough commissioned an assessment of the barrier's west end, as paragraph forty-one had said, and the assessment found a joint in the abutment working open on every tide and recommended an eleven-month repair with sheet piling and a cofferdam and a re-built wing wall. Nadia read the tender documents when they came out. She noticed, without deciding to, that the sheet piling specified was three metres short of the depth of the silt pocket, and she wrote that down in the memo box in the corner of the drawing, and sent it to the borough engineering department with her name on it, and the specification was amended. The amendment came back to her by email with a covering note that said noted, thank you and nothing else, and it was the smallest professional transaction of her life and she kept the email in a folder.

That was the way it worked, she remembered. You wrote it down, and your name was on it, and it went in. That had been the whole of her profession once, before Rotherhithe, and it was the whole of it again.

She took on four jobs that autumn for a small firm in the city and then six, and then a bridge assessment that was two weeks' work and became nine, and then she stopped being surprised when the phone rang. She had been finished, in her own head, for eleven years. The industry had not been finished with her; it had simply waited, which was something she had never understood about industries and was learning to understand about rivers. In November a woman she had trained with twenty years ago asked her to look at a swimming pool in a hospital wing, of all things, a post-tensioned slab with a corroded tendon and a ceiling that was, to Nadia's eye, reading as a sentence, and it turned out that it was one, and the wing was closed for six months and repaired, and nobody died on the stair.

She was careful now about staircases. She was careful about the difference between a memory and a reading. It had taken her something like a night in a cellar to learn it, and she had come out of the cellar with the lesson and the paper both, and she did not intend to put either of them down.

The scrapyard was on the industrial estate upriver, and she found it because Bram asked her to go, and because there was the matter of a tool roll to be settled.

It was a yard of flattened cars and drums of swarf and a weighbridge at the gate, and Kestrel had a portacabin with a window in it and a calendar three years out of date and a chair with one arm. He had a boot on his leg and a stick, and he was sitting in the chair with the window open on the noise of a shear, and he did not get up when she came in.

The shear was going in a long slow rhythm behind the cabin, and the swarf drums stood along the fence in rows, and there was a smell of burnt oil and cold metal that was almost the same smell as the foundry and was not, because a scrapyard smells of the things that have already been cut, and a foundry smells of the things that are still deciding.

"You," he said.

"Me."

"You bringing me my brass."

"No."

He looked at her for a moment and then looked out of the window and said, "Then it's still a loss."

"It's still in the gap," Nadia said. "The gap's under six feet of river mud now. There's nothing to bring. I came to tell you, because you asked, and because I wanted you to know it's gone, and not sitting in a drawer somewhere."

He nodded slowly, one long nod down and one long nod up, and then he said, "Forty tonnes."

"Forty tonnes," she said.

"That was what I priced it at on the first night, and that was right, that's always been my eye, that's the one thing I ever had that nobody could price. Forty tonnes of cast iron, and I had the flatbed coming, and I had a buyer in Leeds for the flywheels and a buyer in Hull for the rail. And I lost it. Not to you. To the tide. The tide got it." He looked out at the yard. "I've had a lot of loss in my time and it never bothered me. Scrap's a business, the market moves, you take the swings. But I've never been stood in a thing that was walking out from under me while I priced it. That's different. I'll say this for you and then we're done: I've asked myself for weeks whether I'd have cut that hatch if I'd known, and I've not got an answer, and I don't like not having answers. I price things. I don't think about them."

"They're not the same," Nadia said.

"No," he said. "That's what I've learned in the last month off my leg. It's not what a thing's worth. It's what you'd trade for it, and how long it'd last, and who else needed it. The brass didn't need anybody. But you." He pointed the stick at her, without malice. "You wanted a thing you couldn't sell. That's mad. I've never got it and I never will. But I've seen it work."

He turned away and picked up a weighbridge ticket and looked at it and put it down again, and Nadia understood that they had finished, and that the finishing was the highest praise Kestrel had, and she left him to his yard and his loss and his one arm on the chair.

She had thought about it on the way and had decided not to tell him about the leg, which had been a knee joint and had been repaired and would be stiff in winter and would carry him, and she understood at the door that he knew already and did not want to hear it said, and that even the truth about his own leg was, to Kestrel, a thing to be priced on the day and not before.

Two of the men at the site that night, she learned later, went to the borough with statements about the early call and the unsafe-structure report; the office did an internal review of its discretion to pull forward, and the word discretion left the standard form in March, replaced with two signatures. The man with the clipboard kept his job and bought himself a better torch. It was, on the whole, more justice than the situation had deserved.

There was a morning in late February when the tide was at its lowest neap and the borough's contractor had the cofferdam open, and Nadia went down to the revetment to look at the abutment before the piling went in.

It was a cold clear day and the river ran the colour of weak tea and the mud flats smelled of iron and rot and salt, and the abutment stood with its face to the west and the river laid bare in front of it for fifty feet, and she walked the flat with her boots sinking to the ankle and put her hand on the 1944 concrete and found the joint the assessment had found and found, under it, four courses of 1891 rubble poured into a trench and keyed with a return that had been laid out by a man with a line and a level and no time at all. It was crude work and it had held for eighty years. Above her the parapet carried the stone with the four names cut into it, and the water was coming back across the flats, and the contractors would start in an hour, and she stood there with the river coming at her boots and read the wall one more time and found it good.

The names on the stone were cut shallow, and the tide would round the edges of the letters in twenty years and soften them in fifty, and Nadia stood in the mud and thought about that for a while, and then thought that a record did not have to last forever to be a record; it only had to last as long as somebody was still reading, and the box in the strongroom was flat and dry and would outlast the stone by a century, and both of them together would outlast her, and that was the most a person could reasonably ask of anything they had had a hand in.

The foundry was a quarter of a mile up the reach, a leaning shell with two walls in the water and one standing over nothing, and people had begun, that winter, to take photographs of it, because a ruin is a thing that people want to have looked at when it is nearly gone.

She thought, standing in the mud with the water returning, that she had spent her whole working life reading buildings for the ways they would fail, and that this was not wrong, because buildings do fail and people die on the stair when they do, and the men at Rotherhithe had died on a stair she had not read, and that would be with her always. But a structure is also a record of what people did with what they had, poured and cut and keyed by men whose names went onto a title block or did not, and a ruin is not a ruin; it is a set of decisions that somebody made, still there, waiting to be read. The mud and the rust and the ruin hold danger and memory equally. The danger is what you owe the living, the memory is what you owe the dead, and understanding the thing is how you save either.

The tide came back over her boots and she stood in it a moment longer than she needed to. The water was cold through the leather and the silt was soft under it and the abutment stood in the middle of the returning river exactly where four men had put it in eleven weeks in 1944, and there was nothing else to see. She turned and walked up the slope to the road and past the barricade at the foundry gate, which by then was a barricade put there by the listing order rather than the demolition, and she looked at the leaning middle tower with two walls in the water and did not stop, because it was not her site any more; it was the city's, and the city had the paper, and the paper said what the building had built.

Then she drove to the office to look at a flat roof that had been reported damp, and got her notes out of the glovebox, and wrote the date at the top of a fresh page in the way she had always written it, from the bottom up.
