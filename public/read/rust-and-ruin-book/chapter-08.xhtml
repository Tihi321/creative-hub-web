The Counterweight

The jack's base plate sat on the cinder bed with the south wall's heel standing over it, and Nadia lay flat on the boards with her arm through the gap and felt for the bearing point.

"Left of my hand," she said. "Under the heel. That hard patch. That's the cinder."

Kestrel's fingers came through beside hers, fast and sure, and he cleared the silt off the patch with the flat of his hand and packed it level. Then he sat back and put both hands on his knees in the water and looked at the jack where she had set it against the roof timber's end.

"Pump that and the timber takes the load," he said. "Which puts it straight into the wall. Which is the thing that's going."

"Which is the thing that's going," Nadia said. "And the thing that's going is the thing that's holding. That's the whole trick."

He looked at her. In the torchlight his face was all bone and no give, and the flat time in him was wearing thin at the eyes. He understood the shape of it now.

"Then pump it," he said.

She put her palms on the screw and turned it. The jack took her weight. The screw went up a turn and she stopped and put her ear near the wall, the way she had put her ear near the stair in the basement three hours ago, and listened. The wall did nothing.

"More," she said.

She pumped the jack for four minutes. She counted the turns because the counting was a rail to hold. A jack is honest, she had told men in classrooms and on sites. A jack tells you the truth about the load and it tells you the truth about the man. After the fourth turn the truth came back up the screw into her wrists: the load was going up, and the load was going out through the wall.

The joint in the south wall closed a hair. She saw it, and she put the light on it, and she held the light on it until Bram saw it too.

"There," she said. "There's your bearing back."

Bram stood in the middle of the room on the dwarf wall line with the 1944 sheet flat under his arm. He did not celebrate. He only looked at the wall and then at the ceiling joists sitting on it and then at her, and then he said, quietly, "What's it doing now?"

She listened. The wall had gone quiet, which was not the same as safe. Quiet meant the load had stopped moving, and a stopped load is a stored load, and a stored load on a soft foot is a store still ticking up a bill.

"It's holding," she said. "But it's holding through a lever, and the lever's fulcrum is a bearing in a brick wall, and the lever's weight is a tonne and a half of cast iron that hasn't moved since 1965 and sits on a shaft bolted through that same wall. The whole of it rests on the fact that nothing down here moves. Kestrel. The gap."

"I'm on it."

He was already across the room on the dwarf wall line, dragging a broken joist end across the floor with his right hand and holding her tool roll in his left. No argument in him at all now. A man who argues has an exit. A man who stops arguing has decided the room is his.

"What do you want?"

"Wedges. The gap between the bearing plate and the cinder bed's already good, but if the heel walks out a tenth of an inch the plate tilts and the jack loses its line, and if the jack loses its line the screw goes back down and we get one more pump and no more. Wedge it either side of the plate."

He wedged it. He folded the tool roll flat and used the canvas under the plate for a shim and drove in two keys of split joist timber with the heel of his hand. Then he sat back on his ankles and wiped his hands on his thighs and looked at the base plate.

"That's a bearing," he said.

That was when the column failed.

It was not the wall. She had spent an hour and a half making the wall the last thing. It was the column at the north end of the room, the one by the plan chests, one of the four iron columns that carried the ground floor's floor through the dry store's ceiling and into the brick piers above. A column she had read on the way in as a standing thing, a nothing, an amenity.

The floor above it dropped an inch and the column's capital came with it.

She heard it as a crack — not the long wailing crack of an arch letting go, but a short hard report, the sound of a thing with a grain. Then she heard the boards up over the hatch take up a load they had not been asked to take for a hundred and thirty-one years, and she heard, in the middle of it, the low sliding bell-note of the line shaft taking up the strain, all the way through the wall to the second flywheel in the dark of the middle tower. The second flywheel answered it with the small movement of a heavy thing waking up.

Kestrel was on his feet and out from under the hatch in one movement. Bram did not move. Nadia looked up.

The ceiling joists over the north end of the room had dropped, and the column head had gone with them. The column had stayed vertical and the floor above it had not stayed horizontal. The difference between the two was maybe an inch and a half over twelve feet, small enough that a man with a ledger would never have seen it and large enough that any structure in the world was now reading it as a sentence.

The north column had no capital, Nadia said to herself, and then corrected herself before the words were even out. The north column had had a capital ninety seconds ago and she had looked at it and read it as an amenity. It had not had a capital at all. It had had a bearing plate rusted through on its north edge where the wet air off the river had had it every day since 1965. It had been carrying its load on the south two thirds of its face, patiently, the way rust carries. The load had just moved north into the third that was rust and nothing.

"Get the chests," she said. "Bram. The chests come out from under the column line."

He moved. He moved the way she had not seen him move all night. The top chest came out and the bottom chest came out on its runners and he had both of them out to the middle of the room on the dwarf wall line in under a minute. The drawers stayed shut. The 1944 sheet stayed flat under his arm. He stood over them looking at the place he had pulled them from like a man looking at water coming under a door.

Crack. The second report came up through the floor and into her kneecaps.

The column was still standing. It would stand all night, probably. A rusted bearing plate on a cast iron column with a live load on it does not fail. It changes its mind. It changes its mind once and then it holds. She had read fifty of them and written the reports on forty-nine. She had been in the room on the day the fiftieth one changed its mind twice. That was Rotherhithe. That was the door in herself she had spent the whole night shutting in her own face. She was not shutting it now. She was opening it, walking through it on purpose, because she could see what was in front of her.

The column was going to settle. It was going to settle a half inch more and take the whole north half of the room's floor with it, and put its load onto the joists. The joists would put the load onto the south wall. The south wall was already carrying the middle tower's lean and a jacked-up roof timber and a tonne and a half of flywheel at a hundred feet of leverage. When the counterweight saw that, the counterweight would release it back down the shaft and the shaft would give it back to the wall and the wall would give it back to the joists. Then the room had no schedule and no exit. Then none of the three of them were leaving.

She did not say any of it. What she said was: "Kestrel. The bar."

"Which bar."

"The travelling crane rail. Ground floor, north bay. I saw it from the hatch. There's a length of rail that's come down off its brackets and it's lying across the machine beds. I need it in this room and I need it now."

"How long's the rail."

"Twelve feet."

"Through the hatch?"

"Bram goes up and takes the pin. It's a two-bolt pin, it'll come. You don't cut it, you don't lift it, you drag it to the hatch and Bram pins it and both of you walk it down the ladder. Don't drop it, because if you drop it we don't have a bar and we don't have a room."

"It's rolled steel," Kestrel said. "It'll drag."

Bram was already at the ladder. He did not look back down at the room. He put his hand on the rung and then he stopped, all at once, like a man who has remembered something. He turned and took his satchel off his shoulder and put it down on the top of the plan chests, put his hand on the flap of it once, clamped the 1944 sheet under his left arm, and went up through the hatch.

Nadia watched him go and understood that he had just done the single most dangerous thing anyone in that room had done all night. He had left the papers behind on purpose. He had decided that if the room came down there was a chance three people came out and no papers, and if the papers went up the ladder with him there was a chance that no one came out at all. He had chosen the way that gave the papers a chance and left the people a chance. He had made that choice in one second with his hand on a ladder in the dark. She had spent eleven years not trusting men who made choices fast. She was revising the ledger on it.

The hatch banged. Feet crossed the ground floor overhead. A pin knocked. Then the two of them were at the top of the ladder and the rail came down the hatch endways, first, slow, with Kestrel on the rungs below it taking the weight on his shoulder and Bram above it taking it on both arms. It came down onto the dry store floor on the dwarf wall line with a noise like a handbell dropped in a church. The whole room read the noise and said nothing.

"Ground it," Nadia said. "North end, under the column's bearing plate. Not under the column. Under the floor boards on either side of the column, across the two joists, and the rail across the four of them. That's a distributing beam. If the column settles, the floor rides the rail instead of cracking off the column. If the floor rides the rail, the load goes out to the walls instead of down onto the joists in the middle."

Kestrel put the rail across the four joists and stood back. His hands were shaking. He looked at them and put them in his pockets and looked at the rail.

"That's a beam that holds nothing," he said.

"That's a beam that spreads everything," Nadia said. "That's a beam we made out of the building. Now the jack needs a second pass."

She went back to the jack and put her palms on the screw and turned it. This time she did not count turns. The load she was pumping into was not the wall's foot any more. It was the whole north half of the room: the settled column, the rail, the joists, the floor above, the middle tower's stopped tick, the flywheel at the end of its hundred feet of iron on the far side of the wall. Nadia at the bottom of it with her weight on a screw and her ear on a brick. The screw went up a quarter turn and would go no further. The lever had reached its balance and it did not want any more.

She looked at the joint in the south wall. It was closed to a hair and stayed closed. She looked at the rail. It had taken a hair's deflection and was holding it. The floor under the column had come off the column's bearing plate by the thickness of two sheets of paper. That was exactly what she had wanted. She had never once in her life been so glad to see a floor leave a column.

She looked at Kestrel across the room. He was looking at her, and his face had arrived somewhere.

"You've done this before," he said.

"No."

"You have. You've done it before."

"Not this." She held the jack's handle and did not let it go. "I got one of these wrong once. Same kind of room. Same kind of column. Bearing plate with a rusted face and a load that walked into it overnight and a crew that went in under it in the morning because the man in front of them said the building was surveyed. I was the man. There was a stair between them and the way out and the stair was the thing I had not read. Four men were on the stair when it read me back." She said the number out loud for the first time outside a hearing. It made a small flat sound in the dry store. Four. She did not flinch from the sound of it. "So no. I haven't done this before. I've done the other one. That's why we did it in this order."

Kestrel stood out of the shaft of the torchlight, working his hands slowly open and shut.

"Fine," he said. It was not agreement and he plainly did not mean it to be agreement. It was a man moving a load off one leg onto the other. "What's next."

Nadia looked at her watch, then at the water in the dwarf gap, over the bricks and up on the boards by two fingers now and would be up on the boards by a hand within the half hour. Then at the shut joint in the wall, closed and being asked to stay closed by a jack and a lever and a tonne and a half of iron and a rail off the ground floor and a cast-iron column she had read wrong on the way in and read right on the way out.

"We hold," she said. "We don't do anything with the building for one more inch. You put your hand on that screw every time I say. Bram gives the 1944 sheet to nobody. You and I put the plan chest drawers on the rail and sit on them, because if the room takes any more load I want it on the rail at chest height where the chests are already the mass and the drawers are already the boxes. If I say leave then we leave. When we leave we leave the satchel and we leave the chests and we leave your brass and we take the sheet and each other out down that corridor."

"The brass is in my tool roll."

"The brass is in your tool roll and your tool roll is on the floor of the room we're about to leave. If I count the seconds and the brass costs me ten of them, you know what the brass costs. You know what it costs because you've spent all night telling me."

Kestrel looked at the roll on the floor and looked at his hands and then crouched and picked the roll up and put it in the gap against the jack's base, not in his belt. He went and sat down on the plan chests on the rail with his back to the south wall and sat as still as a man waiting at a weighbridge. Bram came down off the ladder and sat down beside him with the 1944 sheet flat on his knees.

The three of them sat in the dry store in the dark on the building's own iron with the water coming up through the boards. The south wall stood its ground. The counterweight held at the end of a hundred feet of shaft in a tower that had been silent for ninety minutes and never, once, in a hundred and thirty-one years, failed to answer.
