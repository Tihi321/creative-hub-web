The Broker’s Torch

The rope came first, and then the men.

Nadia heard the hatch before she saw them, because the hatch was a thing she had already read and already discounted. The service hatch was set into the east wall of the basement, a steel plate door a metre square at the level of the old boiler feed, and it had been welded shut in 1996 by the look of the repair plate and the half-moons of rod still standing proud where the weld had been laid. A man with a grinder and two batteries could open it in twenty minutes. She had put the cut padlock link and the four cigarette ends at the gate into a drawer in her mind that Bram had opened and closed for her the day before, and now the drawer was open again.

The grinder had left a line of sparks burned into the wall below the hatch, scorched into the brick in a black arc, and the cut rod lay on the floor where it had fallen, three half-moons of it, each one bright at the cut and furred with rust at the ends, the way the cut link at the gate had been bright at the cut. Four times, she had said at the gate. Four cigarette ends, one brand, and Bram had said scrap boys and rolled his drawing into its tube, and the drawer had closed. Now the drawer was open, and the man who had opened it was coming through the hole he had made.

The torch came through the hatch hole a half-second before the man did, a cold white beam with the throw of a lighthouse and the diameter of a fist, and it swept the ceiling and found the arch grid and found the hole over the third bay where the basement had eaten the floor, and then it dropped and found the two of them standing in the beam line, and it stopped and did not move.

"Evening," the man said. "You're standing on my joists."

He was built the way a jack is built: short, wide, and set down hard on the ground for the express purpose of lifting something. He wore a headtorch over a rabbit-fur hat, a canvas coat with the collar turned up against weather that was not in the basement, and a pair of welders' gauntlets tucked into his belt like a second pair of hands. He came out of the hatch without hurry, found the nearest column with one gloved hand, and stood with his weight on the column and his eyes on the room, and his eyes did the same thing Nadia's eyes did when they entered a building, which was to price it. Where her eye went to a beam seat and a column base and the haunch of an arch, his eye went to the flange of a machine and the length of a shaft and the thickness of a rim, and the two surveys ran side by side in the same torchlight and did not overlap at a single point.

"Kestrel," he said, because she had asked, though she had not asked out loud. "You're the engineer."

"I'm the engineer."

"And you're the one with the paper." He looked at Bram, and at the satchel on Bram's chest, and the torch beam went down the front of the satchel and up again, and his interest in the satchel was the interest of a man reading a label on a crate and not a word of what was on it. "Archivist. I saw your notice at the library. Three hundred for the lot, if it's clean. That's the number I give the museums."

"It's not for sale," Bram said.

"Everything's for sale. What's not for sale is the price you're holding out for." He released the column and took two steps into the room, and the torch swept the boiler house and the firebox door and the flue pipe hanging down like a dropped arm, and then it swept the beam rows, and Kestrel stopped and made a low sound that was not a word. "Now that. That I didn't know about."

The third bay was gone, the hole in the floor still opening its mouth, the water still spiralling down into the dark under the slab. But what the torch had found was on the far side, in the bay nearest the boiler house: a row of machines sunk to the axle in the silt, their flywheels standing up out of the water like wheels of a cart left in a field. She had seen them on the way in and had read them the way she read every object she passed, as loads rather than things: two-ton flywheels on cast-bed machines, a line-shaft along the north wall with its hangers still bolted to the ironwork, a travelling crane with its track still fixed to the columns of the first bay. A works. A small one, but a works.

"There's forty tonnes of CI in this room," Kestrel said, and his voice had changed all the way through, the way a man's hand changes when it finds what it was reaching for in the dark. "Cast iron. Scrap's a hundred and forty the tonne this week and up. The line-shaft's wrought, it'll go for more. There's a flywheel on that middle machine by the door that's got to be a tonne and a half of it and it's whole." He turned the torch on the machine in question, a horizontal engine with its cylinder still lagged in crumbling magnesia, and the flywheel stood out of the silt like the moon rising from the water, ribbed and rimmed and without a crack in it. "And that's before the copper. There's copper in them. There's copper in the pipework, there's copper in the windings if there's any winding left, and the boiler's got to have firebrick and I know a man for firebrick."

He was not lying, and that was the part of it that made her cold. The machines were there, the weights were real, the prices were real enough for a man to come through a welded hatch with a grinder and a spare battery at eight in the evening on a rising tide. He had not come for the archive and he had not come for the building. He had come for a thing she had walked past twice without pricing it once, because the same eye that saw a flywheel as forty tonnes of a foundry's own mass was the eye she was going to have to use, and she had not used that eye yet.

"The building is coming down on Wednesday," Nadia said. "You'll do well to get a tonne out of it before then."

"The building's coming down tomorrow," Kestrel said. "That's the other thing. I've got the crew's list on my phone. They've pulled the date forward. The permit said Wednesday. They moved it to Tuesday. I came tonight because tomorrow I'm here at six with a crane and a flatbed and a bloke called Des, and I'm not here to argue, I'm here to cut. The demolition boys were going to fence the site tonight and I came through the fence before they did it, so we'll say the next four hours are the only ones that exist."

Nadia stood very still and made the arithmetic in her head, and the arithmetic came out and she did not like the number. The staircase was wrecked. The door above the staircase was racked shut a metre and a half above reach. The floor slab was tilting east and south on a void over silt. The arch over the third bay had already come down, and the ceiling grid had the twist in it, and the middle tower's crack had begun its tick, once every two seconds, and the tick was still coming, slow and regular, from overhead, like someone in the tower above them knocking on a pipe with a spoon. If the crew came at six, the next four hours were not four hours of work. They were four hours of the tide, and she knew what the tide did in four hours, because she had watched the wall of the basement and the flood mark on the bricks and the stain on the revetment out on the river, and she had put the numbers in her head on the way in, and the numbers had said: at half-flood, this room was knee-deep in the middle of the beam rows.

She looked at Bram to see whether he had understood the early crew, and Bram's face was doing what it did when he read a date he did not trust: a small tightening around the mouth, the look of a man checking a citation. Then it passed, and it passed for the reason she had expected, because Bram did not hear a crane at six. Bram heard that the building would be rubble by Thursday, and the rubble would take the dry store with it and the plan chest with it, and the drawing would be the only thing left of the men who poured the barrier, and it would be a sheet of linen in a skip, and nobody would ever prove anything again. He put his hand flat on the dry store door frame, and his hand was steady, and she recognised steadiness of that kind, because it was the steadiness of a man who has already lost the argument and is not going to leave the room hearing it said twice.

"You know what's under the floor," she said.

"I know what's under the floor," Kestrel said. "That's why I came the way I came and not the door."

Bram had not moved from the dry store door. He stood with the satchel strapped high on his chest and one hand flat on the door frame, the way he had stood since the floor had moved under them, and he was watching Kestrel with the specific caution of a man who has spent three months alone with paper and knows, without any evidence at all, that he is in a room with a predator. "You said three hundred for the lot," he said, and his voice had gone quieter and more formal, the voice he used in rooms where he had to defend a date, the voice that had letters in it. "There's a plan chest in that store with a linen sheet in it. That's the drawing of the 1944 barrier. There's no other copy. The city has no record of it. It is the only proof that the men who poured that barrier ever —"

"Men and barriers," Kestrel said. "Barriers are concrete. Concrete's got no value. You know what's got value? The satchel. Leather, brass buckles, wartime, I've got a buyer in the Midlands does that. Sixty for the satchel alone. The ledgers, that's paper, paper goes in a skip with the rest of it. The drawing, that's a sheet of linen, no value to me. So here's what we're going to do." He took the gauntlets out of his belt and put them on, working his fingers down into them with his chin, and it was not the gesture of a man about to use his hands; it was the gesture of a man preparing to use his weight. "You put the satchel down by the column and step back to the stair, and I take the satchel and the ledgers as my costs, and the paper you want, you take with you, and we all go home and I don't tell the crew you were in here lifting a heritage asset, because if I do your permit's in the bin and so's his."

"He doesn't have a permit," Nadia said. "It lapsed in March."

"Then it's a police matter and I'm a witness." Kestrel said it like a sum read aloud. "Or you can stay here and argue with me, and I'll drop a bobtail off the line-shaft onto that dry store and see how much of the paper survives a fire, and then we'll talk about what's left to sell. I've done that before. It works."

He let that sit. The torch beam didn't waver. The water in the third bay kept spiralling. The tick from the middle tower came down the column, once, twice, and Kestrel didn't look up. He looked at Bram's hand on the door frame, then at Nadia, and the silence stretched until it had weight.

"If you burn the store," Bram said, "you burn the building."

"I know what I burn," Kestrel said. "I've burned buildings. I know how they go."

Bram's jaw worked once. He was a man who argued with citations, and this was an argument he had no citation for, and Nadia watched him decide something, and the something was not surrender, because Bram did not have surrender in him in the way other men had it. He turned his palm against the dry store door and stood there with the papers at his chest and the torchlight on his glasses, and he did not move and he did not speak, and that was Bram's version of a wall.

"No one is burning anything," Nadia said. She said it into the room rather than at him, flat, the way she had said two-metre spacing and keep to the beams and now, and she watched Kestrel turn his head at the tone and price her. She kept it flat. She had spent eleven years learning how to keep it flat, and the flatness was not for him. The flatness was for her, because the drawer in the back of her skull had been open since the stair, the Rotherhithe stair was standing behind her in the dark with the dust coming up it, and if she let it into her voice now, the voice would come out of the wrong year and say the wrong words, and the words it would say would be the structure is stable, proceed.

She waited for the tick. It came. One, and nothing, and two, and the sound arrived in her the way a reading arrives, which was not as a warning but as a fact with a rate, and she set the rate against everything else she was holding and did not look up at the crack. The crack was not the problem yet. The crack was the clock that told her how long the problem had, and the problem was the floor.

She looked at Kestrel's boots. They were standing on the slab, on the biscuit, eleven feet from the column that carried the sagging south beam row, and the boots were full of sand and silt from the hatch and the silt was a shade wetter than the silt on her own boots, which meant he had walked through water on the way in, which meant the water in the lower ground had already reached the hatch, which meant the tide was not going to wait for tomorrow morning and the six o'clock crew and the crane and Des.

"Look at your own boots," she said.

Kestrel looked down. He was a man who had spent his life reading metal and he did not know how to read a floor, and that was the whole of him, and she watched him look at his boots and see nothing and look up again with the torchbeam ready. Then the slow tick came again from overhead, once, on time, and this time it was not from the middle tower only. There was an answer to it from somewhere lower down, from the south wall, where the beam row sagged and the wall that had leaned slowly for sixty years sat under the middle tower's weight, and the answer passed through the slab and up through the water in the third bay and out into the room. Kestrel's torchbeam wobbled, because his wrist had moved, because the floor had moved under his boots, and he was professional enough to know what it meant when a floor moved under a boot.

"That's the building coming round to your position," Nadia said. "It came round to mine about ten minutes ago. The room is tilting east and south, the columns are moving on the slab, the slab has voids under it, and the wall you've got your back to is carrying a tower that's splitting along a tie, and the tie is going, because it's been ticking since we got down here. The run of the floor is bad and the run of the tide is worse, and the two of them are the same problem now, and the doors are both shut. There's a way out of this room and it is not the hatch and it is not the door, and I am the only person in it who can find it, and I need the rope that's on the middle beam and I need you to stand still while I find it."

Kestrel stood still. He stood still because the floor had moved, and not because she had asked, but that was enough, and she would take it. He lifted the torch off the two of them and put it on the middle beam, where the rope lay coiled on the flange, and the beam ran from the column beside her back across the third bay and over the water, and the beam was a beam she had walked twice, and the rope was where she had left it, and between the rope and the foot of the stair there were nine flanges and one leg of the route had changed shape since the arch went down.

She already knew that. She was still reading it. But she had read it from a standing start with a rope on and one level of the room in her head, and now she had to read it with a torch in it that belonged to another man, and with three people's weight to place, and with the tick still coming down the column at one every two seconds; and the reading was the same and the stakes were not, and the cold place behind her ribs did not care which of the two facts changed. It told her the old thing in the old words — six men, a stair, a clipboard — and she took the hand off the column, and put it flat on the column cap a second time, and the cold of the iron went up her arm and she let it, and then she took her hand away and started to work.

Three people. One route. The tide was rising, and the building had just told them it knew they were here.
