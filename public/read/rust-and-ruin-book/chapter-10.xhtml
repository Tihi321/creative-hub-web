The Wrecking Crew

The yard was cobbles and cinders and standing water, and Nadia stood in the middle of it with the torch dead in her hand and the river noise coming over the revetment like a train that never arrived. Kestrel was on his back on the cobbles with his eyes open. Bram was on his knees beside him with the 1944 sheet still flat inside his coat, one hand pressed to the sheet through the wool as if it might try to leave. The sky was the colour of wet slate. Somewhere out beyond the revetment a gull was working at something and not finding it worth the effort.

She had four minutes before the next thing, and she spent them doing what she did with four minutes, which was read.

The foundry read like this: the middle tower bowing off its base by a foot and a half with the direction of the bow toward the water, the lean accelerating on a curve she could plot in her head with two points and no time; the south corner of the basement gone, folded, the south wall still up like a fence post with nothing to fence; the north tower leaning two degrees the way it had leaned for sixty years, unhurried, indifferent, still a week away from anything. The river was up to the lower flood marks and climbing, and the water in the yard was moving the way water moves when it has found a way in and is in no hurry to leave.

The building had about an hour. Then it would go into the river, not in a fall but in a lean-and-slide, the whole soft footprint of the thing giving up the way a sandcastle gives up, and the tide would take the site the way it had been trying to take the site since 1891, and the demolition crew would find a mudflat with three bits of masonry standing in it.

Which was, she thought, a shape of a mercy. Let the river do the condemning. Let nobody have to swing a ball at it.

"Voss."

Kestrel's voice. She went over. He was sitting up now, one elbow in the cinders, his bad leg straight out in front of him and his face grey with the effort of holding it straight, and he was looking past her at the foundry with an expression she had not seen on him all night, which was the expression of a man doing sums that were coming out wrong.

"That's a loss," he said. "That's a forty-tonne loss. I had a flatbed coming at six. That's a loss of what — say thirty-eight, thirty-nine at the yard gate, and I paid nothing for it but a battery and two hours of my time, and now I've paid nothing and got nothing instead, which is worse."

"You're on a medical list for a knee," Nadia said. "You're going to be on it for a while."

"That's a loss too," he said, and then, without any particular feeling, "I called them."

"Called who?"

"The crews. When I cut the hatch. I told them the building was going over, that it was worse than the survey said, that if they wanted the permit done before the river did it for them they should come tonight." He looked at the foundry. "I told them because I figured if the crews were on their way you'd hurry, and a man who hurries leaves things, and a thing left is a thing found. That's how I work. That's not a confession. That's a price list."

Somewhere in the west, over the shoulder of the north tower, an engine turned over and settled into a diesel idle. Then another engine. Then a third, higher, a compressor, and then the unmistakable dry clatter of a drum winch being run out to take tension.

Kestrel listened to it with his head cocked, like a man hearing his own train come in.

"That's them," he said. "That's the six o'clock job," and he began, with great care, to laugh, which turned into a cough, which turned into him lying back down on the cobbles and staring at the sky.

The gate was forty yards south along the revetment wall, and the crews had opened it, and the crews had driven in.

There were three machines in the yard, and she counted them twice. A wheeled excavator with a demolition boom and a muncher on the end of it, an articulated lorry with a low loader and an assortment of buckets and shears chained down on it, and a smaller tracked machine with a hoe ram. Two flatbed trucks behind them, and a water bowser, and a diesel generator running off a spool on a trailer. Men in orange and men in grey, and a man in a hi-vis vest with a clipboard standing on the tailgate of the lorry like a man standing on a stage.

Nadia had seen a demob crew start up maybe a dozen times. It never looked like this. It looked like this when it was starting six hours early, out of sequence, on a wet morning, under a permit that said the seventh day.

She walked up to the lorry with Bram and left Kestrel sitting on the cobbles because Kestrel was not going to walk anywhere, and that turned out to be the right decision, because the man with the clipboard saw her coming and did not see Kestrel at all, and what he had been told about the site was in his face, and his face said he had been told that the site was empty.

"Site is closed," he said. "You can't be in here."

"Neither can you," Nadia said. "Your permit runs to the seventh day. The permit was issued six days ago. This is the sixth."

"The office has discretion where a structure's unsafe," he said. "We had a report."

"You had a report from the man who cut the hatch," Nadia said. "Would you like to know what the man who cut the hatch does for a living?"

The man with the clipboard looked at Bram and looked back at her and said, "The structure is unsafe. That's not a report, that's a fact, I've seen it, look at the tower." He pointed with the clipboard at the middle tower, which was bowing, which was doing worse than bowing, and as they all watched a course of brick let go somewhere near the top and skidded down the face of it and turned to dust in the air before it hit the ground.

"That's the point," the man said, kindly now, the way a man is kind when he has been proved right and does not want to gloat. "It's coming down on its own. We'd be negligent to wait. The office has discretion, and the discretion's mine this morning, and I'm going to pull it in before it does the river a kindness and takes the wall with it."

The other men had stopped working. Not all of them had been working anyway — you do not start a muncher at six when the site is half underwater — but the ones who had been chaining down buckets had come off the lorry, and the generator man had come round the side of the trailer, and they were all of them standing in the yard now, looking at the tower, and looking at her.

She had them for about a minute. She had maybe less.

She reached over and put her hand on Bram's chest and found the flat of the sheet and Bram understood and put his own hand over hers and together they worked the 1944 sheet out of his coat and laid it on the tailgate of the low loader, and Nadia found the corner of it with two fingers and turned it flat, and the men in the yard looked at a piece of drafting linen a metre by a half-metre, faint-ruled, with a hand-ruled border, browned along the fold lines, curling slightly, holding the last of the rain off itself out of sheer stubbornness.

"Read the title block," Nadia said.

"What is this?" said the man with the clipboard.

"It's the reason this building is still here," she said. "Read the title block."

The man leaned in. He was not, she understood, a stupid man. A stupid man does not get given discretion. He read the block, moving his finger under the letters, and Nadia watched him read, and behind him the generator idled, and above him the middle tower leaned on its own compass and did whatever it was going to do next on a schedule that was written in water and not in ink.

"Aurel Flood Barrier," he read.

"The Aurel Foundry," Nadia said, and put her finger on the line under it, "erected the flood barrier across this reach in eleven weeks, 1944, in the middle of the war, with the foundry's own crane, its own line shaft, its own workmen." She slid her finger down. "Those four names are the men who built the barrier that has kept this stretch of the city dry for eighty years. That barrier is still standing. It's standing between your yard and the houses behind the revetment right now, and it has never been the council's barrier, it has never been the borough's barrier, it has been nobody's barrier since 1965 because nobody knows they built it, and if you put your muncher through this basement on your discretion this morning, the survey that proves what that barrier is and where its drawings are goes with it."

The man with the clipboard looked at the 1944 sheet and he looked at the tower and he looked at his own watch and then he looked at the tower again, because the tower was the loudest thing in the conversation.

"The barrier's fine," he said. "The barrier's over there and we're over here."

"You're on the foundry's footings," Nadia said. "The barrier's abutment ties into the foundry's quay wall, because the foundry built it and a firm does not build a thing across a reach without using what it already owns. If this quay wall goes, the abutment goes, and you can watch the flood mark on the revetment wall if you want me to tell you what happens to ten thousand houses when an eighty-year-old barrier loses its end. Ask your engineer. I'll wait."

"There's no engineer on the job," the man said, and stopped, and heard himself say it.

"Ask your office, then," said Nadia. "And in the meantime, I will tell you three things, and you can pull the muncher in any time you like, but you'll be pulling it in over a named engineer's report, and if anything happens to that abutment you and this office will be explaining why you were told about it and pulled anyway."

She did not raise her voice. She had one thing she could do and that was to speak in the flat absent voice she used for a memo, the voice with no stake in it, the voice that read a wall the way a wall should be read, and she used it.

"One. This building is going in the river on the tide, on its own, in an hour, without any help from you. The basement's already failed. The middle tower's off its base by a foot and a half and bowing at a rate of about a tenth of an inch an hour, and it will be visible by dinner. You will not need a permit to demolish anything here, because there will be nothing here. The tide's had the site rehearsed for sixty years and this morning it gets its run.

"Two. If you drop that tower into the water before the tide takes it, the rubble, the silt that comes with it, and the collapse of this quay wall are going to dump the whole lot against the barrier's east abutment and undercut the wing wall. Your muncher will do this in the next two hours to gain the office a morning.

"Three. Everything's on the sheet. Every line. The survey, the bearing of the quay wall, the abutment ties, drawn by the men who built the thing and signed off by a county surveyor in 1944 whose name is in the corner in pencil and still legible, and once you've seen it you cannot unknow it, and I am asking you to leave it standing."

There was a silence in the yard. In the silence the middle tower clicked. It was one click, and everybody heard it, and every head turned to it, and Nadia did not have to say anything at all, because a building can make its own case.

The man with the clipboard said, "Who are you?"

"Nadia Voss. Chartered structural engineer. Eleven years' assessment and this building's assessor of record since six o'clock yesterday morning." She was not sure of the clock. It did not matter. "Ask your office."

He was already on the radio, turned away, one finger in his ear. The men in the yard stood and watched the tower, and one of them, a grey-haired man in orange, came over and looked down at the sheet, not reading it, just looking, the way a man looks into a room he used to live in. "Aurel," he said. "That's old Aurel. My grand-dee's brother was at Aurel."

"That's one of the names," Nadia said, and the man bent and found it without help, fourth line down, a common surname, and stood there a long time with his finger on it.

Then the tower leaned by a hand's width, in the time it took to draw a breath, and came to rest again, leaning, and the river, which had been working at the revetment for the whole conversation, took the top of the revetment wall and came over it, and the yard flooded to the width of the gate in about six seconds, and a man near the generator said something Nadia did not catch and started shutting the generator down.

The man with the clipboard came back off the radio. He looked at the sheet. He looked at the water in the yard. He looked at the tower, which was going to do what it was going to do, on its own, on the tide.

"Office says hold," he said. "Office says do nothing, put a watch on it, don't touch the quay, and refer the sheet. They want a photograph. They want a copy of that photograph, and the name of the engineer, and the time."

"Four twenty in the morning," Nadia said. She had not looked at a watch. She did not have to.

"Four twenty," he said, and one of the men already had a phone up and was photographing the sheet, corner to corner, title block first.

They got Kestrel into the ambulance when it came, which took a further eleven minutes because the gate had half flooded and the driver would not come in past the flank of the middle tower, and by then the light was grey all the way through and the river was at the upper flood marks, and the foundry stood in its own wet ruin and leaned.

They had moved the machines out. That was the thing she had not expected. The man with the clipboard had done that before he did anything else, walking back down the yard with his radio at his mouth, waving the muncher out first and then the low loader and then the ram, and the machines went out of the gate one by one in the half-dark like animals called out of a field, and the yard was left with the water in it and the men at the edge of it, watching.

It occurred to Nadia, standing in the water, that she had spent the whole night trying to save a building that could not be saved and had been wrong about that, and now she was watching the water rise and the tower lean, and no, she had been right; but the thing she had saved was not a building. It was what the building knew. That was what Bram had been telling her all along, in the way he had, mostly by holding onto a satchel. A structure's job is to keep its paper dry. A city's job is to keep its records of itself. Somebody had built a barrier in eleven weeks in 1944 and kept ten thousand houses dry for eighty years and nobody had ever said thank you, because the drawings were in a foundry the city had been trying to pull down since 1965, and a thing that is not remembered is not a thing you can be grateful for.

That was the failure she had been carrying, and it had not been Rotherhithe, or not only Rotherhithe. It had been this: that she had spent eleven years reading structures for signs of what would bring them down and had never once read one for signs of what it had done.

Bram came and stood beside her in the water. He had the 1944 sheet folded in his coat again and had not let anyone take it anywhere without following it with his eyes.

"Missus Voss," he said. "It took him a while to call it the Aurel Flood Barrier."

"Yes."

"He read the title block out loud and then he read the line under it twice."

"They'll do that," Nadia said. "Give it to the council. Give it to the paper. Give it to the man in orange with the grand-dee's brother. It's not ours to spend."

"No," Bram said. "It's theirs. That was the whole point of it, I believe. To be found." He looked at the huddle of men at the yard gate and then out at the water, which was doing what it had always done. "Will it come down today?"

"No," she said. "It'll lean for a week and then it'll go, and it'll go into the water where there's nothing under it, and the quay will stay and the abutment will stay and the barrier will be exactly as strong tomorrow as it was yesterday, which is the whole of what I told them."

"And the council?"

"Will refer a photograph, and the borough will get a letter from a chartered engineer, and there'll be a listing meeting, and somebody will say the word barrier out loud in a room with a carpet, and the foundry will become a document instead of a building, and somebody will have to decide what to do with a leaning tower standing in the tide that has a 1944 drawing in it."

"Somebody," Bram said.

"Yes," Nadia said. "Not me. I'll write the report. That's what I do."

The ambulance doors closed. Kestrel had asked no one to come with him and no one had offered. Lying on the trolley with a splint on his leg, he had looked for a while at the water, and then he had said, with his jaw set, "There was brass in my tool roll, in the gap, against the jack," which was true, and nobody had brought it, and nobody said so, and he said, "That's a loss," and turned his face to the roof of the ambulance, and Nadia had thought: the man prices everything, and the price he has paid is the one thing he cannot value.

The crews stayed. They had a watch on it now, and a barricade at the gate, and a clipboard man on the tailgate, and a photograph going by email into a council inbox. The foundry leaned into its own shadow and the tide came over the revetment and covered the lower flood marks and stopped climbing, and the water stood in the yard of the Aurel Foundry in the grey early light, level, indifferent, patient — and Nadia, standing to her knees in it, read the whole building one more time the way you read a letter you have already answered, and did not find a single thing in it she had misread.

The demolition clock had been six days. It had never been six days. It had been one tide. It had been eleven weeks in 1944, and one tide, and now it was whatever the city decided, and the city had a sheet of paper, and the paper said the building had built the thing that kept the city dry, and the city, if she read it right, had just finished reading the title block out loud.

Bram said, "You'll come back to the site?"

"The site is water," Nadia said. "I'll come back to the meeting."

She turned and walked out under the barricade, and the man in orange with the grand-dee's brother lifted the tape for her, and outside the gate the road was a normal road going up a normal slope into a town that was wet but dry inside its walls, and Nadia put her hands in her pockets and stood at the top of the slope for a minute, watching the town and the water and the tower, and then she went to write it down, because it was the only thing left to build.
