The Unsurvivable Room

The flywheel came off its bed with a sound like a bell being rung in a well.

Nadia heard it from the beam row where she had gone to read the floor between the second column and the south wall. The sledge had found the wedge, the wedge had given, and the tonne and a half of cast iron had dropped the half-inch the pins had been holding it off. The whole basement answered. The ring went up through the ceiling grid and came back down off the water, and the three tide marks on the south wall shimmered, and every one of them moved at once, which is how a water level behaves when the room it sits in is shaken but not broken. She counted the marks. Still three. The silt under them still hard. She put the reading in her chest and did not let it change anything.

Kestrel had the rim of the wheel in both gauntlets and was walking it down off the plinth a handspan at a time. He was stronger than he looked and he knew exactly how much he could lift. He had already worked out that a tonne and a half that falls is a tonne and a half nobody owns. Bram had the sledge and the pins in the satchel pocket that wasn't the satchel, and he was standing well back with his weight on the beam. The two of them had struck the first real bargain of the night without saying anything: the scrap man moved the weight, the archivist carried the tools, and neither of them asked the other to trust him.

The flywheel Kestrel moved was the one from the middle machine by the door — the one he had priced at forty tonnes for the room, a tonne and a half for the wheel. Its twin still sat on the line shaft in the middle tower, unmoved since 1965. That one would stay where it was. This one, freed, he parked against the foot of the south wall where the wall met the cinder bed, and stood with one gauntlet off, breathing, looking at the slope of the fallen bay with a scrap man's eye.

Nadia took the rope off the beams and paid it out toward the south bays.

The route she had read since the tick stopped lay along the south wall — not down the middle of the floor, not along the beam row where the slab panels between the columns were sagging, but hard against the wall foot, where the old brick rested on the strip footing and the strip footing rested on the silt the wall had been compressing since 1891. A wall that has been leaning for sixty years has been pressing its own weight into the ground beneath its heel for sixty years, and ground that has been pressed for sixty years is ground that has stopped moving. The route was along that heel. She had known it was there since the first hour. She had called it a fault then. She was calling it a road.

She walked it first, because she had made the rules and rules meant nothing if she carried them loose. Boots on the seam between the wall and the slab, one hand trailing the brick, weight coming down through the arm and the wall and not through the floor. The brick was cold and sweating and gritty under her palm, salt bloomed on it in a white map, and the lime mortar between the courses ran out and away from her like the pointed strokes of somebody else's handwriting. She read it with her hand. The mortar was soft in the lower four courses and firm above them, and the firm courses began at a height she could measure with her belt buckle, and that height was above the tide marks, and that meant the softer brick was soft because of the water and not because of the age, and the wall knew the difference and had kept the dry course out of the wet.

Fifty metres of that road and she reached the corner where the arch had come down.

She stopped there and made herself stand in it and look at it properly, because the arch was the reason she had ruled the room beyond unsurvivable, and a route that crosses a place she has already called deadly is a route she has to check twice. The arch had failed at the third bay and the bay's rubble had come down into the opening and piled up into a slope that ran up to a void and over it. She had seen it from twenty metres away in worse light and read it as a collapse, a place where the structure had stopped being a structure and become a pile. From the wall foot it was a slope of brick and render with a face at fifty degrees, and a slope is a thing a man can climb, and the void over it was the keyhole where the arch had been, and the keyhole's top was the intact arch ring of the bay beyond, and the arch ring of the bay beyond was carrying.

She put the torch up to the keyhole and looked through it. The beam went along the corridor beyond and lit the corridor's floor, and the floor was a boarded floor on joists — timber joists, old creosoted timber, dry where the rest of the basement was wet — and the joists had been there since 1891 and had not been got at by the salt, because the corridor behind the south wall ran on the dry side of the lying. Timber tells you things concrete won't. Timber that is a hundred and thirty years old and still creaking straight is timber that has never been wet.

"Nadia." That was Bram, behind her on the wall foot, the sledge over his shoulder and the satchel across his chest.

"Give me the count." She took the light off the keyhole and ran it down the slope of the fallen bay and counted what she needed: the clean stones on the face, the gaps behind them, the tendency of a pile to keep its shape. A pile of rubble is not a random heap. It is a heap that has already fallen, which is to say a heap that has reached its lowest energy state, which means it will hold the shape it has until you disturb it, and disturbing it is a matter of how you put your feet on it.

"Twenty, thirty feet of muck," Bram said. "Up and over."

"Up and over. Kestrel." She turned. "You see a way through that?"

"I see a way over your dead body, is what I see," Kestrel said. "That's a hole with a pile of shit in it. You go up there and the whole lot slides back down through the keyhole with you."

"The whole lot doesn't slide. The keyhole is wider than the pile. The pile is at angle of repose, which is the angle it makes when it has stopped sliding, and if you put your weight on the crown you're standing on the part that slid last and stopped first. You don't go up the face. You go up the shoulder, where it's met the wall and stacked."

"You got a plan or a poem."

"Both." She took one end of the rope and tied it off to the ring bolt she had spotted at the wall foot, a half-ring of black iron set into the brick at ankle height, the kind they put in for hauling engines down from the ground floor. Iron in brick since 1891 and salt round its roots and it held when she tested it with her weight, and she tested it because she did not take any iron on this site at its word. "Bram goes first with the sledge, you go second with your hands free, I go last with the light. Rope on every man, tied off here, paid out by the one behind. If the slope moves we're not on it. We're beside it."

Kestrel looked at the ring bolt. He looked at the wall foot with its dry courses and its sweating lower bricks. He looked at the grey water crawling up the south wall, three inches above the lower tide mark now, and Nadia watched him price the water and price the slope and price the tonne and a half of flywheel he had just set down against the wall, and she saw the arithmetic close in his face before his mouth opened.

"Or," he said, "we don't go up your hole in the ground at all. We go back out the hatch. Water's only at my boots. It'll take an hour to reach my knees, and by then we're all three out on dry land and whatever's left of the papers is your problem. And my flywheel's already off its bed, which was the only thing I came down here for."

The sledge was in Bram's hands and Bram's hands were on the shaft. Nadia did not look at him. She kept her eyes on Kestrel and kept her voice flat, because flat was the only voice that worked on a man who priced things.

"The hatch is a metre square," she said, "and the water outside it is a falling tide and not a rising one. You came in at near-high water through a hatch three feet above the flood line, and the flood line is going down now, which is why you think the water on your boots is the last of it. The water on your boots is the tide that's still in the made ground behind the hatch. It comes out slower than it came in, because silt does not give water back at the same rate it takes it on. You stand in that hatch mouth for ten minutes and you'll be standing in a drain."

"You don't know that."

"I read it at the wall. Same wall you're standing at. Three tide marks, hand's width apart, and the third one is the one that came in this afternoon and the second one is the one that came in this morning. You can stand there and watch the fourth arrive or you can come up the slope with us."

Kestrel said nothing for a beat. Then he started putting his other gauntlet back on. "You go first," he said. "I don't walk behind a wall in a building that hasn't got one."

"Suit yourself." She stepped onto the rubble at the shoulder — the seam between the fallen bay and the standing wall, where the bricks had stacked at a shallower angle — and the pile did not move under her, and the rope came taut behind her and Bram made it fast and she climbed.

The slope was not bad. That was the thing she had to keep telling herself, because the level part of her mind had already marked the arch as the worst step of the night and the level part of her mind had been wrong about the arch twice now. The rubble under her boots was mixed brick and render and a length of broken corbel that had been part of the arch springing, and it held. At the top the keyhole was a ragged hole in the fallen ring and she put her shoulder through it and came out into the corridor.

She stood up in it and looked back for the others and looked down at the floor.

The corridor ran east to west along the inside of the south wall, and it was narrow — three feet between the wall and a row of brick piers — and the floor was boards on joists on dwarf walls, and the dwarf walls went down into the dark past the bottom of her torch. She put the light down the open gap beside the first dwarf wall and saw the timber standing in standing water, and the bottom of the timber was black and the water was black and the two blacks met without any visible seam, which would have scared her this morning. It did not scare her now. She had read enough this morning to know what she was looking at.

She crouched and put the torch in her fist and put her other hand down into the gap and found the dwarf wall's foundation and read it with her fingers. Brick to the bottom course and then no brick at all, and below that a timber pile, one of a row of them, drier than the brick and harder than the brick, and below the pile nothing she could touch, which was the point. The corridor was not on the silt. The corridor was on piles, and the piles had been driven into whatever is under silt, and they had held for a hundred and thirty years, and the water standing between them was water that drained between them, because a piled floor with open space beneath it is a floor the tide passes under without lifting.

She knew the pattern. It was the same pattern as the failed arch, only inverted. The ground floor had asked its joists to bridge silt and the silt had squeezed the joists. The corridor had asked its joists to cross water and given them a pile to stand on, and the pile had never gone soft. The drawing she had been reading all day — the 1961 survey, the strip footings, the alluvial soil — had told her the whole site was a raft on a sponge. Nobody had told her the south corridor was a bridge.

"Nadia." Bram's voice from behind her at the keyhole. "You're standing on it."

"I'm standing on piles. Come up. Both of you."

Kestrel came through the keyhole last, and when he straightened in the corridor and saw it — narrow, dark, leaning, water beneath the boards, the wall sweating at his shoulder — he stopped and read it like a man who reads buildings for a living, which he did, in his way, because a scrap broker who cannot read a building is a dead scrap broker.

"No," he said. "No."

"It's piling."

"It's a sewer."

"It's a boarded floor on dwarf walls on piles, and it's dry under the joists where the water's stood for a century and hasn't rotted them. Look at the wall side. Dry course above the tide marks and the tide marks are lower than the boards." She swung the light across the bricks. "The south wall's got a rebate at this level, they laid the dwarf walls in the rebate, the pile heads are tied into the rebate, and the whole corridor is hanging off the wall we've been reading as a fault. It's a supported walk. It's the strongest floor in this building."

Kestrel came forward one plank and put his glove on the wall and felt the rebate and read it with his hands. Nadia watched his face do the thing she had seen a dozen times since the hatch — the moment he stopped arguing and started agreeing and did not say so. He took his glove off and felt the brick and the pile head under the boards, and he grunted.

"Then it's a corridor that goes somewhere," he said.

"It goes to the dry store."

"It goes to the dry store with the plan chest inside it and the wall above it and the water in the basement coming up behind us."

"Then we'd best walk it."

They walked it. Nadia first with the torch, Bram second with the sledge, Kestrel third with his gauntlets and his tool roll. The corridor was too narrow to walk abreast and too low to walk upright and they went along it in single file with their shoulders to the wall, and the boards gave a hand's width under each step and came back, and behind them the basement ticked and the water climbed the archhead and the keyhole through which they had come began to fill.

Halfway down the corridor Bram said, quietly, "The water's louder."

"It's the tide coming back over the silt, not the water in the basement rising. Same rise, same rate, same direction. It'll be at the arch foot by the time we come back."

"Coming back through the arch."

"Coming back through the arch." She did not stop and she did not turn round. "Look left."

He looked left. The wall had a window in it — a small one, bricked up in the lower half and glazed in the upper, and the glass was thick and green and full of air bubbles and light, and through it they could see the basement outside the corridor, the flooded floor, the beam row, the machines standing in the standing water, and the flywheel Kestrel had laid against the south wall, and beyond them the middle tower's crack running up the brickwork like a struck match.

"That's us," Bram said. "That's where we were."

"That's where we were, and the water on the slab is at Kestrel's boot-top mark and the mark is still there under the silt and the slab hasn't moved a hand's width since we left it. I told you that wall was carrying the tower. Nobody believed me. Including me, for a while."

"I believed you."

"You believed the part where the papers come with us. Different article."

Behind them Kestrel made a noise that was almost a laugh. "You two want to do the marriage later. I've got a flywheel in the water and a flatbed at six and a plan chest to pull out of a hole in the ground. Move."

They moved. The corridor bent north at the end where the south wall met the old core, and the old core was the room at the corner, and the corridor went into the old core through a doorway with a door in it, and the door was an old one — vertical boards, hand-forged strap hinges, iron ring for a handle — and it had an iron lock and no key, and it had not been opened since the day it was shut, and the door stood in its frame and the frame stood in the wall and the wall stood in the ground and none of the three had moved a measurable amount in seventy years.

Nadia put her hand on the door and read the frame first, because the door would take a shove and the frame would tell her what the shove would do.

The frame was in the same rebate as the dwarf walls. It was on the same piles. There was a step up at the threshold and the step was brick and the brick was dry and there was a hairline of light between the door and the boards, and that hairline of light did not happen by accident — whoever had built this room had built it with a threshold and a rebate and a raised floor, and they had built it dry.

"This is the room," she said. "This is where your 1944 drawing is, Bram."

Bram did not answer. He came up beside her and put his hand on the hinge strap, the archivist's hand, and he stood in front of the door for a long moment, and his shoulders came down.

Kestrel came up behind them both and looked over their heads at the door and at the corridor behind them and at the light coming through the little window.

"Open it, then," he said. "Before I change my mind about the flywheel."

Nadia put her shoulder to the door. The door gave. The frame took the load and passed it to the piles and the piles held and the room beyond was dry and dark and smelled of iron and paper and standing time, and she stood in the doorway with the torch up and the two of them behind her, and the tide came up the corridor behind Bram's heels, and she looked into the unsurvivable room and saw that she had been right about the wall and wrong about the room and wrong in exactly the way she was now, finally, right about every other thing in the building: the fault was the support, and the danger was the route, and the thing she had ruled out was the only way in.
